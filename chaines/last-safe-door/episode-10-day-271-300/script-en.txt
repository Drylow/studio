On day 271, nobody opens the gate.

The knock does not come again. For a long time, there is only the torch beam lying across the vent grille like a finger on your lips, and four people in the dark trying not to breathe.

Your name is Patch. Two hundred and seventy days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, Wes, who used to live on a roof, and Vera, who drove the last bus out of your yard, and a month ago drove a few thousand of the dead into a gravel pit and shut a gate behind them. You know everything about the dead. How they walk. What they follow. Where they stop. You know nothing at all about whoever is standing at your gate.

Then a voice comes over the wall. A woman's voice. Not loud. Not whispering either.

"We're not dead," she says. "And we're not here to take anything. Where we come from, two knocks means that."

Nora looks at you. Wes looks at you. Vera looks at the stairs, the way she looks at every door.

The rule is the boy's rule now, the one Nora taught him on day 245. If somebody knocks any other way, you don't open.

"We don't open at night," you call up through the grille.

A pause. Then, surprisingly, a laugh. A tired one.

"Good," says the woman. "Neither do we."

The beam clicks off. You hear boots, a few steps, and then the soft sound of people sitting down on the pavement on the other side of your gate, as if they are ready to wait all night. They are.

On day 272, at first light, Wes goes up to the gantry with his mirror.

He comes back down two minutes later and describes them the way he used to describe the river, one thing at a time.

"Three," he says. "A woman in a yellow raincoat. Very yellow. You could see her from the moon. A boy, maybe twelve, in a white bicycle helmet. And a big man with a blue and white checked blanket round his shoulders, like a cape. He's asleep sitting up." Wes thinks about it. "Like Vera."

"Nobody sleeps like me," says Vera.

You go up and see for yourself. They are sitting with their backs against the wall across the street, right under the sign. FOUR ALIVE, and the frequency. The woman in yellow sees your head over the gate and lifts one hand. Empty.

"My name's Ruth," she says. "That's Kit. The one snoring is Bram. We walked nineteen days to knock on that gate."

"Why?" you ask.

She points up at the sign.

"Because somebody on that frequency says Still here every night," she says. "On a clear night, we can hear it all the way from the sea."

Behind you, on the stairs, Nora stops dead.

From day 273 to day 274, you talk through the gate.

You don't open it. You carry the bench up into the yard, and they sit on their side of the bars, and you sit on yours, and it is the strangest conversation you have had since day one, because nobody is whispering and nobody is dead.

At sunset, Ansel comes on the radio, the way he does every night.

"Pit's quiet. Bridge is ours." Then, "Did three people come your way? Yellow coat, white helmet, a big man in a blanket?"

You look at the gate. "They're sitting outside it."

"They came through the city eight days ago," says Ansel. "They asked about the depot. They mended the pump at our spring, which nobody here could do. And the big one sings better than anyone on our bridge." A pause. "That isn't difficult."

"Anybody can mend a pump," says Vera, when the radio is off.

"I can't," says Wes.

On day 274, you ask Ruth the question that kept you awake.

"Your torch went straight to our grille. How did you know where to look?"

Ruth looks almost sorry. "Your lantern," she says. "Every night, a little glow comes out of that grille, right at the bottom of the wall. We watched it for three nights before we knocked. It goes down when you sleep. It comes up again when somebody has a bad dream."

Nobody says anything. For two hundred and seventy days, you thought the dark was keeping you safe. It turns out the dark had a little light in it all along, and anybody could have seen it.

That night, Wes tapes a square of cardboard over the inside of the grille. Neatly. Very neatly.

"It's not personal," he tells the grille.

On day 275, you open the gate.

In daylight. That's the new rule, and you say it out loud, so it's been said. You open in daylight, or you don't open.

Vera stands on the gantry with the rope of the bolt in her hand. Nora stands beside you with the flashlight, although it is noon. Wes stands slightly behind everybody, oiling nothing.

The bolt lifts. The gate whispers open. And three people walk into your yard, very slowly, the way you would walk into somebody else's kitchen.

Kit looks at everything. The fire. The dark rectangle where the coach used to stand. The canopy, the solar panel, the loudspeaker with its switch taped on OFF. He looks at your yard as if it is a famous place he has read about in a book.

Bram looks at the gate. Then he takes a small can of oil out from under his blanket and holds it out to Wes, without a word.

Wes takes it. "Finally," he says. "Somebody who understands."

Before anything else, Nora teaches them the knock. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer. Ruth tries it on the gatepost twice, until she has it.

"And yours," Nora says. "Two. Hard."

"Two hard means we're alive and we're asking," says Ruth. "Not telling. Asking."

Nora nods. "Ours means we're home."

From day 276 to day 278, Ruth tells you where they came from.

They sit at your fire and eat the hill's potatoes, and Ruth draws it for you with a stick in the ash, the way Ansel drew the gravel pit in the frost.

A coast. A long beach. And at the end of the beach, out in the sea, a rock with a little town on it, and a lighthouse. One road goes out to it, a long, low road of stone laid across the sand.

"The Point," says Ruth. "That's what everybody calls it. Thirty-one of us live there. Kit's father keeps the lighthouse."

"One road," says Vera, leaning in.

"One road," says Ruth. "And twice a day, the sea comes in and covers it. For six hours, nothing can walk to the Point. Not us. Not them."

She rubs the road out with her thumb. Then she draws it again.

"Then the sea goes out, and the road comes back."

Wes stares at the drawing for a long time.

"A gate that closes itself," he says. "And opens itself."

"Twice a day," says Ruth. "You get used to it."

"And them?" you ask. "The dead. Do they get used to it?"

Ruth's face changes, just a little. "Some of them come along the beach at low tide," she says. "Not many. A few dozen every winter. They walk out along the road until they reach our gate at the end of it, and they stand there with their hands on it, until the sea comes in and pushes them gently back to the beach." She shrugs. "Next low tide, they walk out again."

"Like the Doorman," says Wes.

"Who's the Doorman?" asks Kit.

Wes tells him. It takes the rest of the evening.

On day 277, you ask her the other question. Why walk nineteen days inland, past every empty town, to knock on one gate?

Ruth looks at Nora.

"We listen to the radio every night," she says. "For a long time, there was nothing. Then one winter, a woman's voice. Still here. Every night. Sometimes there were men with her, saying other things, about rivers and horns. But every single night, Still here." She shrugs. "Kit wanted to know who it was. So did I. When you spend a winter on a rock, you start to need to know the rest of the world hasn't stopped."

Nora doesn't say anything. She looks at the fire. Then she gets up and walks away, very fast, toward the depot building.

"Did I say something wrong?" Ruth asks.

"No," says Wes. "You said something right. She's not used to it."

On day 278, Bram fixes the drip over your bench, the one that has been dripping for as long as you can remember. He doesn't say a single word the whole time. When it's done, he whistles three notes, low, and goes back to the fire.

"He whistles instead of talking," Kit explains. "Three notes means done."

On day 279, Ruth sees the locker.

Nora shows it to her herself. The narrow room at the end of the depot, the row of tall metal lockers, the one door standing open. The folded cleaning jacket. The gloves. The road map, with its pencil circle at the edge of the sea. And the postcard, taped inside the door. Blue sea. Yellow sand.

Ruth stands in front of it, very still. Then she reaches out and puts one finger on the corner of the postcard, where a tiny white tower stands on a dark rock at the end of the beach.

"That's our lighthouse," she says.

Nobody moves.

"That's the Point," says Ruth. "That's our beach. Somebody printed it on a card. I always wondered who bought them."

"Me," says Nora. Her voice is very small. "Before. I bought it before. I was going to go."

Behind her, Wes takes off his beanie. It is only the third time you have ever seen him do that.

"That's the beach," he says quietly. "I promised."

From day 280 to day 284, there are seven at the fire.

It takes some getting used to. Seven bowls. Seven places on a bench that was only ever built for four. Kit tries to sleep on the canopy ladder, until Nora makes him come down. Bram sleeps sitting up against the gate with his blanket over his head, and Vera, who has slept sitting up for nearly a year, watches him do it with a look you have never seen on her face before. Respect, maybe.

Wes takes the pencil and adds three marks to the people met column. Then, after a moment, he hands the pencil to Kit.

"Your turn," he says. "Do us."

Kit adds four.

On day 281, Kit stands under the canopy and points up at the loudspeaker, with its switch taped on OFF.

"What's that for?"

"Calling them," says Wes. "It's on the list of things I'm sorry about."

"Does it still work?"

Wes looks up at the solar panel, shining in the sun, the way it has every day since you met him.

"The battery's full," he says. "That's the problem with it."

On day 282, the hill people come down with apples, and the boy with the blue bandage meets the boy in the white helmet. They stare at each other for a full minute, two very serious faces, like two dogs meeting in a park. Then the hill boy teaches Kit the horn code, and Kit teaches the hill boy to knock twice, hard, and by evening they have invented a third knock that nobody else is allowed to know.

Every sunset, Nora says Still here into the radio. Now she knows that somebody on a rock by the sea might be listening, and she says it a little slower, as if she wants it to arrive safely.

From day 285 to day 287, Ruth asks.

She waits until the fire is low, on day 285, and everybody is there.

"Come with us," she says. "To the Point. All four of you. There's room. There are boats. There's fish. And a gate the dead can't open."

Nobody answers. You knew it was coming. You still have nothing ready to say.

"Two days' drive," says Ruth, "when the roads are clear. You've got a truck. You've got a driver." She looks at Vera. "I hear you've got a very good driver."

Vera turns a stick over in the fire.

"One way in," she says. "And the sea for a gate." She almost smiles. "My favorite kind."

Nora looks at you. And you understand that she isn't asking you whether to go. She has been going since the day she bought that postcard. She is asking you if you are coming.

You look at the gate. At the bunker door. At the yard you lived under, then took back, then made your own. Two hundred and eighty-five days, and you have never had to decide to leave. Leaving was always something that happened to other people.

"When the roads are clear," you say.

It isn't a yes. Nora knows it isn't a yes. But she smiles anyway, as if it's the first half of one.

On day 287, Wes starts a list on the wall, beside the chart. Things we'd take. The first thing on it is the orange bag.

From day 288 to day 295, it rains.

Not snow. Not sleet. Rain, the first real rain of spring, warm and heavy and endless. It drums on the canopy the way the river used to. It fills the dark rectangle in the yard until it is a pond. It finds its way around the cardboard on the grille and runs down the bunker wall, and Bram stands under it for a long time with his hand flat on the concrete, listening. Then he whistles two notes, which Kit says means not good.

On day 290, Ansel's voice on the radio changes.

"Pit's quiet," he says. Then, "Pit's wet."

On day 292, he says, "Pit's filling."

You stand on the bunker stairs with the radio, and you can see it as if you were up on the rim. The frozen puddle at the bottom of the gravel pit, the one the size of a car park, growing. Spreading. Rising around the wheels of the coach. And thousands of the dead standing in it, perfectly still, facing a bus with nothing left to say, while the water climbs past their knees.

"They don't seem to notice," says Ansel. "They're just standing in it."

On day 294, he says, "It's up to their waists."

On day 295, the rain stops.

From day 296 to day 299, the world dries out.

The yard steams in the sun. The pond in the rectangle shrinks back. Bram climbs onto the canopy and clears the gutter, Kit hands him things, and Wes supervises, which mostly means sitting on the ladder.

On day 297, Ruth takes the road map down from the locker and draws the way home on it in pencil, a thin line from the edge of the city to the circle by the sea. Two days. Back roads. One bridge she isn't sure about.

"When the roads are clear," she says, and folds it up again.

Nora pins it back in the locker beside the postcard, and for the first time, you notice she has left room next to them. As if something else is going to go there.

On day 298, you go down to the bunker alone and stand in front of the chart. Days. Zombies gone. People met. Days in daylight. Wes's list beside them. You put your hand flat on the wall, the way Bram listens to concrete, and you try to imagine the room without anybody in it. The lantern cold. The bench empty. The door open, with nobody behind it.

You can't. You have tried before. It always turns into the first night instead. The stairs. The steel door. The sound of the ceiling filling up.

Every sunset, Ansel says, "Pit's quiet." Every sunset, Nora says, "Still here."

On day 300, the radio is silent.

Sunset comes and goes. Nora sits on the bench with the radio in her lap. Seven people around the fire, not talking, all listening to nothing.

Ansel has not missed one evening since day 241.

It is properly dark when the radio finally crackles. And the voice that comes out of it is Ansel's, but he is whispering again. The way the whole city whispered for a month under a bridge full of the dead.

"Depot," he says. "Depot. Do you read?"

"We read you," says Nora.

"The wall came down," says Ansel.

Nobody moves.

"The rain did it. A whole side of the pit just slid. It isn't a wall anymore. It's a slope. Mud, all the way from the bottom to the top. A second way out."

You think of the gravel pit. The steep walls. The one road down. A place with one way out. You think of the rule you were so sure of. They do not climb.

They don't have to. Not anymore.

Vera is already standing. She has her scarf in both hands, and her face is the face she had on day 241, when the radio asked for the driver.

"Where are they going?" she says. "Exactly."

Ansel takes a long time to answer.

"They're walking up it," he says. "All of them. Out of the pit and across the fields." A long breath. "Toward the city."

Then the radio clicks off.

Three hundred days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight and the radio. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 301. Seven people are sitting around your fire, a postcard is waiting in a locker, and the sea is two days away. But tonight, the river you shut in a hole in the ground has found a second door. And it is walking toward the only people in the world who know exactly how to whisper.

Would you have opened the gate to strangers? Would you leave your bunker for a rock in the sea? Tell us in the comments, and subscribe to Last Safe Door, so you're here for day 301.
