On day 121, the hill wants your speaker.

The woman is still waiting on the radio. Down in the bunker, the three of you sit on the bench with the lantern turned low, and nobody wants to be the first to speak.

Your name is Patch. A hundred and twenty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, and Wes, who used to live on a roof. Last month, you filled the yard with thousands of the dead and got locked out for your trouble. You walked home in the rain and climbed a rope over the wall. Now the tail of that same horde is climbing toward the people on the hill, and the only thing loud enough to turn it around is bolted to your canopy.

"If we call them," Nora whispers, "where do they go?"

You all know. The yard is full. They will stop at the gate. In the street. Under the sign. Right where the rope came down.

"She put the whole city on our ceiling," Nora says. "Now she wants the rest of it on our doorstep."

"She turned the truck around for me," you say.

You think about day 90. The street. The tail of the river turning to follow you. A cab door swinging open before you even knew it was there. You have owed people things before. Never anything this big, and never to someone whose name you don't know.

Wes raises one hand. "I vote yes. Quietly. In case anyone's listening."

Nora looks at the chart on the wall for a long time. The days. The walkers gone. The people met. Then she picks up the radio.

"We'll do it," she says. "On one condition."

"Name it," says the woman.

"Food and water. Every week. Delivered."

"Your street is about to be full of them."

"Then you'll have to be clever about it. Patch says you're very clever."

A long pause on the channel. "Fine."

"And one more thing," says Nora. "Your name."

This pause is longer.

"Ask me again when you can see my face," says the woman, and the radio goes quiet.

Day 122 begins at the control box.

Wes opens the lid. The little battery light glows fully green. For a whole month, the sun has been quietly filling it up again, while nobody was asking it to.

Nora stands beside him with her arms folded. "I spent two weeks trying to shut that thing up," she says.

"I know," says Wes. "It's on the list of things I'm sorry about."

He flips the switch. Over the yard, over the street and over the rooftops, a voice you know rolls out across the town.

"Passengers for Platform 1."

From the gantry, you watch the hill road. The dark line that has been crawling uphill all night slows down. Stops. Then, slowly, like the needle of a compass, it turns.

All day, they come back down. At the split, they take the low road. Then they pour around the last corner into your street, the way water fills a ditch.

By afternoon, the street is full from wall to wall. They stand under the sign. Around the lamppost. Against the parked cars. All of them looking up at the canopy, waiting for the bus. Above them, the sign sways in the wind. For the first time, it has an audience.

At sunset, Wes switches the speaker off. The stragglers keep arriving after dark, walking toward the last thing they heard.

In the morning, there is a figure at the gate with both hands flat against it, like a man waiting to be buzzed in. An olive vest.

"He's back," Wes whispers. "He must have missed us."

Then you notice something strange. The gate isn't rattling. The yard is pushing it out. The street is pushing it in. For the first time in months, the gate stands perfectly straight.

"That's not how it's supposed to work," says Nora.

"Nothing is," says Wes.

On the radio, the woman's voice is calm again. "They've left the hill. All of them." A pause. "Thank you."

You have never heard her say it before. Neither, you suspect, has she.

That night, at the hour, Nora blinks the flashlight twice from the gantry. And far up the hill, on top of the tallest tank, a small light blinks back. Somebody up there has learned the code.

From day 123, the depot is an island.

Nobody can reach the wall. Nobody can reach the gate. The sign hangs over a sea of heads, and the place where the rope came down is just more shoulders.

In the bunker, the footsteps on the ceiling never stop now, not even at night. Thousands of feet shifting on concrete, slow as weather. Wes says it sounds like living under a beach. Nora says she has never been to a beach. Wes promises to take her, when all this is over, which is the kind of promise that only works down here.

On day 124, Nora lays everything you own across the bench and does the arithmetic in a whisper, the way she did on day 35. Twenty days of food if you eat like normal people. Twenty-five if you eat like careful people. Thirty if you eat like people who are about to start arguing. Two and a half jugs of water.

You choose careful. You know the way by now.

On day 125, the three of you share the last apple. You pass it around and suck each bite until it gives up. It takes most of the afternoon. It is the best afternoon of the week.

On day 126, it rains.

Nora stands at the end of the gantry and watches the water. It runs along the edge of the canopy roof, through the long metal gutter, and pours out of a pipe into the packed yard below, where it does nobody any good.

"Nine years," she says. "Once a year, up that ladder, scraping that gutter. I hated every rung."

You climb over the gantry rail onto the canopy roof, the way you did on day 80. Hands and knees. Spreading your weight. Every time the thin metal flexes, it booms, and under the canopy, the coach rocks on its springs.

At the corner, you pull the top of the drainpipe out of the gutter and wedge an empty water jug under the spout.

It fills in an hour.

By day 128, there are two full jugs at the bottom of the bunker stairs, and the rain is still falling.

Wes tastes it and declares it the finest vintage of the season. It tastes of metal roof and pigeons. Nobody complains.

Nora looks at the jugs for a long time. "Every rung," she says. "Every single one. It was for this."

Water, it turns out, falls from the sky. Food doesn't.

On the night of day 129, the woman's voice comes on the radio. "Look across the street."

Opposite the far end of the gantry, above a little shop with a torn awning, there is a window on the first floor. In it, a light blinks once.

She came on foot, from the back alley. Her truck coasted in with its engine off and is parked three streets away, out of sight.

On day 130, she leans out of the window with a coil of thin cord. There is a heavy bolt tied to its end. Her first throw falls short. The bolt drops into the crowd, and a hundred faces turn toward it. She pulls it back, slowly, very slowly, through a forest of legs.

Her second throw clears the gantry rail. Wes catches it against his chest, and doesn't make a sound, and is extremely proud of himself for the rest of the day.

You pull. Behind the cord comes a rope. You tie it to the rail. On her side, she ties it to a radiator. Now a line runs across the street, high over a few thousand heads.

On day 131, the first sack comes across, hanging from a hook. The line sags in the middle. Every face below follows it, like the crowd at a very slow tennis match.

Tins. Crackers. Ration packs. Then a full water jug, so heavy that the line dips low over the crowd. Green fingers brush the bottom of it. You and Wes pull faster than you have ever pulled anything.

From day 132 to day 135, the deliveries become a routine.

Twice a week, after dark, a light blinks in the window opposite. Then the sacks come across, one at a time, never two at once. The woman always comes alone. When she leaves, the engine starts first time, three streets away, and she is gone before the slowest of them has turned its head. The rest of the hill stays behind its fence and, for the first time in months, sleeps all night.

On day 133, Nora stands at the rail and sees the woman's face for the first time, in the window, in the light of a lantern. The goggles. The long grey scarf.

"Goggles," Nora says. "You weren't joking."

That night, the sacks hold batteries, a can of oil, more blue bandages, and a small tin of white paint.

"For your sign," the woman says over the radio. "Yours is running."

Wes immediately wants to change THREE to FOUR, to see what happens.

"We're not four," says Nora.

"We could be," says Wes. "She's just across the road."

Nora puts the paint on the rack, unopened.

From day 136 to day 140, the nights get colder.

Your breath shows on the gantry. The metal rail stings your hands. In the yard and in the street, the dead stand a little closer together, though they couldn't possibly feel it. In the coach, frost creeps across the windows from the corners, and eleven faces watch it come.

Every delivery night, the woman stays at her window after the last sack, and you stay at the end of the gantry. Two radios. Twelve meters of air. A few thousand people in between, none of them listening.

On day 137, you ask her why she never goes into places with one way out.

She is quiet for a while.

"On day four," she says, "I was in a supermarket with forty people. It had one way out."

Then: "That's all you get."

On day 139, Nora takes the radio and asks again. "Can I have your name yet?"

"You can see my face," the woman admits. "But I'm on the wrong side of the street."

At dawn on day 141, the radio crackles, and it is not delivery night.

"Depot." The woman's voice is very flat. "The truck won't start. The cold got into the battery."

Then you hear it, faint, over the rooftops. A grinding cough, three streets away. The starter motor. Again. And again.

In the street below the gantry, heads turn.

"Stop," Nora whispers into the radio. "Stop turning the key."

The grinding stops. But a slow movement has already started at the edge of the crowd. Part of it peels away, down the side road, toward the sound. Then more. They don't hurry. They never do. But there are hundreds of them, and only one of her.

From the end of the gantry, you can see over the rooftops toward the side road. You can see where they are going. She can't.

"Not the hill road," you whisper. "They're coming down the side road. Go left. The alley behind the shops."

For an hour, you watch the crowd you called down from the hill walk away from your street, toward the woman who asked you to call it. Then, in the window opposite, a light blinks once. She made it.

Then another sound, from behind the shops. A dustbin, tipping over in the back alley.

Day 142 dawns with the woman at the window opposite, and the sound of fists on a door somewhere behind her.

The dead found the truck. Then they found the alley. Then they found the stairs. She has pushed a wardrobe against the door at the top. It moves a little more every time they lean.

The only thing left in that room that goes anywhere is the line.

"I don't go into places with one way out," she says on the radio.

"You're already in one," you say. "Come into ours. At least it has us in it."

There is a long pause. Behind her, the wardrobe shifts again.

Nora takes the radio. "You told me to ask again when I could see your face."

"Not now, Nora."

"Fine," says Nora. "After."

She ties a loop of cord under her arms, clips it to the hook, and climbs onto the windowsill.

She comes across the street the way the sacks did. Hanging from the hook, both hands on the line, her goggles pushed up, her scarf knotted at her chest. You and Wes haul on the pull cord, hand over hand.

She is heavier than any sack. The line sags lower than it ever has. Halfway across, it dips down toward the crowd, and a few thousand faces tilt up to watch her come. Arms rise, slowly, like a field of wheat in the wind. A green hand brushes her foot. Then another. One of them comes out of an olive sleeve.

Of course it does.

"Pull," Nora whispers, and holds the flashlight on her, steady, the way she has held it every night for a hundred and forty-two days.

You pull. The line rises again. The hook reaches the rail, and Nora grabs the woman's arm and drags her over onto the gantry.

Behind her, in the window across the street, the door gives way. Green shapes fill the room. One of them walks into the radiator. The knot slips. And the line drops into the crowd, gone.

The woman sits on the metal floor of the gantry and looks back at the empty window for a long time.

"Well," she says, at last. "That's one way out."

From day 143 to day 149, there are four of you in a bunker for two.

Wes offers her the stairs. She looks at the steel door at the top of them and says no, thank you, very politely, like someone refusing a seat on a sinking ship. So Wes keeps the stairs. The woman sleeps sitting up against the wall, facing the steel door, because it is the only door. On the first morning, she reorganizes the racks. On the second morning, Nora puts them back. On the third morning, nobody touches the racks, and that is how a peace treaty gets signed down here.

She doesn't complain about the dark, or the ceiling, or the footsteps. She just listens to them, the way you did on day one, as if she is learning a language.

Wes asks whether she counts as people met twice, now that she's indoors. Nobody answers him.

At sunset, the hill's radio speaks with a new voice, one of the dozen, nervous and young. Without the truck, nobody up there can herd. But nothing is climbing the hill road anymore. Everything that walks in this town is standing around your depot. For once, it is the hill that has a quiet ceiling.

On day 147, Wes opens the tin of white paint and looks at Nora hopefully.

"Not until she tells us her name," says Nora.

"Then leave it at three," says the woman, without looking up.

The tin goes back on the rack. Food for about a month, if you are careful. Water, as long as it rains.

On day 150, the woman climbs to the gantry with you at dawn. There is frost on the rail.

For a long time, she looks down at the yard. Thousands of them, packed from the gate to the canopy. And in the middle of them, under the canopy, the coach. Its folding door, shut since day 55. Its luggage hold, empty. Its windows, furred with frost.

"Nora," she says quietly. "This is a bus depot."

"I know. I cleaned it."

"And that is a bus."

"Its battery died on day three," says Nora.

The woman looks up at the solar panel on the canopy roof, sitting directly above the coach, the panel you cleaned one strip at a time.

"Batteries can be charged," she says. "If that coach could drive, I could take your whole yard out of that gate behind it. And your street. And never bring them back."

Wes looks from the coach to the woman. "Who drives?"

The woman pulls her goggles down over her eyes, for the first time since you have known her.

"I always drive."

You look down through the coach windows. Eleven passengers. The one at the top of the aisle, as always, is the night driver, in his blue depot vest with its two pockets.

"It would need its keys," you say.

Nora doesn't answer for a while. When she does, it is barely a whisper.

"They're in his pocket."

A hundred and fifty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 151. There are four of you in a bunker for two, with the dead on both sides of the gate and frost on the rail. The woman who never goes into places with one way out has finally found the way out of yours. And the key to it is in a dead man's pocket.
