The ceiling is quieter than it has been in a month. Quieter is not the same as empty.

Your name is Patch. Thirty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived in a concrete room under the depot, with a steel door, a bench, two racks of shrinking supplies, and Nora, who used to clean this building and now keeps both of you alive inside it. Yesterday, a truck crawled past the street opening and pulled a long stream of the horde away behind its engine. Somebody living was driving it.

Nora is holding the flashlight. Your hand is on the orange bag. Day 31 has started, and neither of you has moved.

"Upstairs first," she whispers. "Then we decide."

From the corridor window, the yard looks like a stadium after everyone important has gone home. A few dozen walkers still stand between the loading bay door and the street. Most of them are bunched around the coach. The truck is gone. So is its sound.

You want to follow it. You say so quietly, because that is how all your arguments happen now. Nora points at the street opening. Then at the walkers between you and it. Then at the desk wedged against the bay door, which is the only lock you have left. Once that desk moves, nothing holds the door except whoever stays behind it.

"Following an engine you can't hear anymore," she says, "is what they do."

You look at the walkers in the yard, all still facing the street where the truck disappeared. She has a point. You would just be the best organized one.

So you stay. But staying can no longer mean waiting. The world outside is changing, and you need to understand how.

On day 32, you climb to the gantry. Without the loudspeaker, it is almost peaceful up there. You keep the flashlight off. Anything that moves above the yard gets looked at. Nora stops at the first turn of the stairs. Her hand finds the rail and stays there. Then she breathes out and keeps climbing.

This time, you stay at the far end long enough to look over the depot wall. A street. Parked cars nobody will ever move again. A road climbing toward the hill. Somewhere beyond it, the man on the radio is hiding. And at the street opening, a heavy sliding gate, pushed all the way back against the wall. It has been open since the first day. Nobody stayed long enough to close it.

Below you, the remaining walkers drift around the coach. One of them wears a blue depot vest. Nora looks at him for a long time and says nothing. You do not ask.

Days 33 and 34 belong to the radio. The store gave you one box of batteries, and the radio eats them like it is being paid by the hour. You agree on five minutes at sunset. No more.

On day 33, the man answers almost immediately, as if he has been sitting beside his speaker all week. He asks your names. You tell him. There is a pause.

"Is Patch a name or an injury?"

"Both," you say.

He laughs very quietly, which tells you he has learned the same lessons you have. His name is Wes. Learning it this late feels like finding the instructions after you have already built the furniture.

Wes has seen the truck twice. Both times in the morning. Both times slowly, with one short blast of the horn at every corner. Both times, a long tail of walkers followed it out of his street and did not come back.

You think about that all through day 34. The people in that truck are not running from the dead. They are moving them. For a month, you have treated sound as the enemy. Somebody out there is using it as a tool.

On days 35 and 36, Nora lays everything you own across the bench and does the arithmetic in a whisper. Eleven days of food if you eat like normal people. Fifteen if you eat like careful people. Twenty if you eat like people who are about to start arguing.

You choose careful. By the second evening, your hands are slower and your temper is shorter. Nora drops the pencil twice. Both times, you freeze and stare at the ceiling until it stays quiet. Hunger, it turns out, is noisy too.

The store is bare. The office is bare. There is no next easy supply room. But there is a next hard one. It has been parked in the middle of the yard since day one, with its door half open. A month ago, you were supposed to catch a bus. Now you are planning to rob one.

Days 37 and 38 go into watching. From the corridor window, you learn the yard's habits. On dry days, the walkers spread out across the concrete. When it rains, they crowd under the canopy around the coach, as they have all month. So the coach needs a dry day.

Then you make the rule that matters. You will go out alone. Nora will hold the bay door. If walkers come toward it, she closes it. Even if you are still outside.

She refuses. You insist. She refuses again, more quietly. In the end she agrees, and does not speak to you for the rest of the evening, which is how you know she means it.

You agree on one more thing. If you are ever separated, she will flash the light from the corridor window once an hour. One flash. Still here.

Day 39 starts dry and grey. Clouds sit over the hill. Clouds are not rain, you tell yourself, with the confidence of someone who has spent five weeks underground. You and Nora slide the desk back a centimeter at a time. She opens the door just wide enough. Then you are outside.

The air is enormous. The yard smells of diesel and old rain. You keep your shoulder to the wall and move slowly, steadily, without stopping. You are trying very hard not to be interesting. The nearest one is twenty steps away, facing the street. It does not turn.

You climb into the coach and pull the folding door almost shut behind you. Inside, there is nobody, unless you count abandoned jackets and the small, sad leftovers of other people's journeys. You open the overhead racks one by one. Crackers. Two bottles of water. A bar of chocolate. A first-aid kit. You pack the orange bag without letting anything touch anything else.

Then something taps the roof. Then something else. Then the whole coach becomes a drum.

The rain does exactly what rain has always done here. The walkers come in under the canopy and pack around the coach, shoulders brushing its sides. You go flat in the aisle between the seats. A few of them drift toward the bay door. Through the windshield, you see it close. Nora kept the rule. You wanted her to. It still feels like being left on the wrong side of the world.

The rain does one kind thing. It covers small sounds. So you keep working, slowly, in its rhythm. Dried fruit. Cookies. A tube of sunscreen, which feels optimistic. And a wind-up travel alarm clock, a device whose only purpose is to make a sudden loud noise. You hold it like it might be listening.

In the first-aid kit, there is a fresh bandage. You change yours in the dark. After thirty-nine days, you have a new one. It is also orange. Apparently, that is just who you are now.

Every hour, through the rain, a small light blinks once in the corridor window. Still here.

On day 40, the rain stops at dawn. The walkers do not move. Of course they don't. They did not leave when the coach alarm stopped, and they are not leaving because the weather has improved. You press the orange bag against a window so Nora can see it, and you wait.

Then something clinks high above the yard. On the gantry, a small figure with a purple band on her arm is throwing your trash. Nora has climbed up there alone, with a bag of the empty cans you spent a month never dropping. Her first can hits the top of the wall and falls back into the yard, near the street opening. Every head turns. Half the crowd shuffles toward it, away from the coach. Her second can sails clean over the wall and rattles down the street. The walkers nearest the opening follow it out.

You slip out of the coach and move along the wall. At the bay door, you knock three times. Then a fourth time, softer. Thirty-six days ago, Nora knocked on your door exactly like that. You have been waiting a long time to return the joke.

The door does not open. Nora is still running down the stairs. It is the longest minute of the month. Then the gap appears, and her hand pulls you inside. Her fingers are shaking.

"I didn't look down," she says. "Not once."

On days 41 and 42, you count what the coach paid. About ten more days of food, if you stay careful. Water. A clean bandage. It also charged a fee. A night in a wet coach gives you a cold, and your body invents a brand new alarm. You spend two days sneezing into the orange bag like it owes you money. And when Nora slammed the bay door in the rain, the crooked latch finally snapped off. Now the door cannot even pretend to stay shut on its own. You fetch the trolley you abandoned upstairs three weeks ago and wedge it behind the desk. It squeaks once, in protest. You both stare at it until it stops.

But you have learned something worth more than crackers. The walkers that followed the cans into the street have not come back. For a month, the rule that they don't leave when the noise stops kept them on your ceiling. Now it keeps them on the street.

From day 43 to day 46, you thin the yard one can at a time. Every dry morning, you climb to the gantry together. Nora throws. She is better at it than you, and she has decided this is not up for discussion. You watch and count. Two walkers out on day 43. Five on day 44. Three on day 45. On the bunker wall, your chart grows a second column: days on the left, walkers gone on the right.

Empty cans become your most valuable possession. You suggest eating faster to make more ammunition. Nora explains, slowly, why that is not how food works.

Then, on day 46, the right-hand column stops growing. You throw. They leave. And new ones wander in from the street, through the open gate. The yard is a bathtub with no plug.

On days 47 to 49, the gate becomes the only thing you talk about. A heavy sliding panel on a rail, with a bolt that drops into a hole in the ground. Nora knows it screams when it moves, because she spent years hearing it every evening. That scream will reach the yard and the street. Everything in both places will come to see what made it.

So you plan around the scream. You add the gate to the corridor sketch. You walk the route in your head until you can do it with your eyes closed. Nora says that is a bad habit to practice. She will be on the gantry with a bag of cans. You will carry the sunscreen. You intend to squeeze the whole tube onto the gate wheel. If nothing else, it will be the best protected gate in the city.

Day 50 is dry, and you check the sky three times to make sure. Nora's first can lands behind the coach. The walkers left in the yard drift toward it. You go out.

You move along the wall to the gate, empty the sunscreen onto the wheel and push. The sunscreen helps the way it always has. A little. Not enough. The gate lets out a long metal scream that seems to last a full minute. In the yard, every head turns toward you. Out in the street, figures you had not even noticed start walking your way. You push harder. The gap narrows. A hand appears in it, searching, exactly like on day 18. It slides back out a second before the gate closes. You drop the bolt.

Behind you, the walkers in the yard are halfway across. Above, a can bangs off the canopy roof. Another clatters behind the coach. They hesitate, then turn. You run along the wall. Three knocks, one softer. The door opens.

On the far side of the gate, fists begin hitting metal. For fifty days, you have listened to walkers trying to get to you. This is the first time you get to listen to them fail.

The gate holds through days 51, 52 and 53. The pounding does not stop. A new crowd now stands in the street, held back by a gate instead of your ceiling.

At sunset on day 51, Wes tells you he is down to two cans of soup. The next time the truck passes, he is going up on his roof with a mirror. "If they're herding," he says, "I'm going to be a very obvious sheep."

On day 52, he does not answer. On day 53, he does not answer. You leave the radio on longer than you agreed. Finally, Nora switches it off. "We're spending batteries on a silence," she says. Neither of you says out loud what the silence might mean.

Eleven walkers are still inside the yard: the slow ones, the stubborn ones. Cans make them shuffle away from the coach, and then they shuffle back. On day 54, you realize you need them somewhere they cannot come back from. You look at the coach. Then at the travel alarm clock. Nora watches you look at both.

"No," she says. Then, a minute later: "How long can you set it for?"

On day 55, you walk out to the coach, open its door wide, wind the clock to five minutes and wedge it under the back seat. Then you hide behind a canopy pillar. Five minutes is a very long time. When the clock rings, it is a tiny, furious sound, and every walker in the yard turns toward it as if it has called their name. One by one, they climb the step into the coach. In an orderly line. Better behaved than most passengers you have ever traveled with. The man in the blue depot vest boards last.

Nora has told you where the outside door release is. She cleaned these coaches for years. You slip forward, push the folding door shut and pull the lever down. The clock rings itself out. Eleven passengers stay aboard. They do not leave when the noise stops. They never do.

On day 56, Nora finally talks about the man in the vest. He drove the night route, she says. He used to say the coach was the only quiet place in the whole depot. She looks at it for a while. "Well," she says. "He's got it now."

On day 57, you stand in the middle of the yard in full daylight, and nothing turns toward you. Your knees do not know what to do with that. The coach sits with its eleven quiet passengers. The gate rattles now and then. Everything else is sky.

But the truck has not come back, and neither has Wes. If the living are herding the dead, they are doing it somewhere else. So you decide to become easy to find. Nora brings out the cardboard she has been flattening since the first week. In the maintenance cupboard, you find the yellow paint once used to tell buses where to stop. You paint two words and a number: TWO ALIVE, and the radio channel. On day 58, you hang it from the far end of the gantry, over the street.

Nora watches it sway. "A sign is a door you can't close," she says. She is right. Anybody can read it. You leave it up anyway.

On day 59, you open the luggage hold under the coach. Eleven passengers watch you through the windows. None of them gets off. Inside the hold, there are suitcases, mostly full of clothes. And a cardboard box of somebody's wedding favors: two hundred tiny bags of sugared almonds. Somewhere out there, two people got married and never got to hand these out. Now their wedding is feeding you.

That evening, you look up at the canopy. The solar panel is charging the loudspeaker battery in the last of the sun. The switch in the control box still says OFF. You leave it alone. Some promises are worth keeping, even to a loudspeaker.

Near dawn on day 60, an engine sounds at the bottom of the street. The pounding on the gate stops. From the gantry, you watch a white truck crawl around the corner. One short blast of the horn. The crowd outside the gate peels away and follows it out of sight in a long, ragged line.

Twenty minutes later, the engine comes back alone and stops outside your gate. The radio, silent for a week, crackles.

"Depot. This is Wes. Nice sign. Your handwriting's terrible."

They saw his mirror on day 52. His batteries died the same afternoon. He has spent a week convincing strangers that two people are living under a bus depot. Then a second voice comes on, a woman's, calm, used to being listened to.

"Wes says there are two of you. How many of them are on your side of the gate?"

You look at the coach. "Eleven," you say. "All seated."

There is a long pause on the channel.

"Open the gate," she says. "We need to talk before the crowd comes back."

Nora is holding the flashlight. Your hand is on the orange bag. Sixty days. A new bandage, same color. Food for a few more weeks, most of it sugared almonds. A yard you emptied with your trash, and a coach that finally took some passengers. For sixty days, every door you closed kept the dead out. Tomorrow is day 61. Somebody alive is standing at your gate. And you have to decide whether this is a door you open.
