On day 151, there is frost on everything.

On the gantry rail. On the canopy roof. On the windows of the coach, where eleven faces stare out through a thin white fur of ice. Down in the bunker, the four of you sit with your hands wrapped around nothing, because there is nothing warm to wrap them around.

Your name is Patch. A hundred and fifty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, and Wes, who used to live on a roof. Last week, the woman from the truck crossed the street on a rope above a few thousand of the dead, and now she lives here too. The yard is full. The street is full. And yesterday, she looked at the coach in the middle of it all and said the only sentence that matters this winter.

"If that coach could drive, I could take your whole yard out of that gate behind it."

She counts on her mitten fingers what a bus needs.

"Keys. A battery. And an empty driver's seat."

The keys are in the pocket of a dead man. The battery has been dead since day three. And the driver's seat is surrounded by eleven passengers who have been waiting for this bus to leave since day 55.

"So," says Wes. "Easy."

The woman explains the rest the way other people explain a recipe. Somebody opens the gate. She drives the coach out, slowly, with the horn going. The yard follows the engine. The street follows the yard. She leads them all the way out of town, over the flyover and far beyond it, to the last sound they will ever hear from her. Then she turns the engine off, and rolls away.

Days 152 to 154 are for watching the cold.

You have never seen the dead in a real frost before. In the mornings, when the yard is white, they hardly move. Their heads turn slowly, like rusty hinges. A shoulder takes a whole minute to shift. By midday, when the sun touches the concrete, they loosen up again, and the slow shuffle comes back.

"Cold kills batteries," says the woman. "Apparently, it slows down everything else, too."

You write it on the wall, next to the chart. Not in words. Just a small drawing of a sun with a line through it. On mornings like that, you will have an hour. Maybe two.

Nora watches the coach the whole time. The man in the blue depot vest stands at the top of the aisle, as he always does. In the frost, he doesn't turn at all. Out in the street, the Doorman freezes with both hands flat on the gate, like a man who has fallen asleep waiting to be buzzed in.

On day 155, Wes climbs onto the canopy roof with the screwdriver.

The roof is made of long metal sheets, screwed down onto beams. The sheet right above the front of the coach is held by twelve screws. They have been rusting there for longer than Nora has been cleaning the depot.

So Wes does what he did to the bay door. One screw at a time. He waits for the wind to blow, then turns. When the wind stops, he stops. Each screw comes out with its own tiny, rusty squeak. Below him, the coach passengers tilt their faces up, slowly, and wait for the next one.

The woman watches from the gantry. She doesn't like roofs. Roofs have one way down.

It takes three days. On day 157, Wes lifts the sheet, and there is a hole in the canopy roof.

You lie on your stomach and look down through it. Two meters below is the roof of the coach. In the middle of it, near the front, there is a square hatch with a handle. All around the coach, packed shoulder to shoulder, there are heads.

"Management will want to know who did this," Wes whispers.

"Management did," says Nora.

On day 158, Nora tells you about the hatch.

It is an emergency hatch. It opens from outside with a twist of the handle. On hot nights, at the end of the night route, she used to climb onto the coach roof from the canopy ladder and open it to let the air through. Then she would come down and wait at the folding door.

"He always came down the aisle last," she says. "After the last passenger. He'd stand at the top of the steps and drop the keys into my hand. And he'd say, all yours."

She looks at the wall for a while.

"For nine years. All yours. Every single night."

On day 159, she draws the coach for you, from above. The hatch. The aisle. The step down to the door. The driver's seat, on the left. And a cross on the man in the vest, on his chest, on the right-hand pocket.

"Right pocket," she says. "Always the right. He said the left one had a hole."

On day 160, nobody mentions how much food is left. Nobody has to.

From day 161 to day 163, you get ready, and you get hungry.

Nora lays everything out on the bench and does the arithmetic. Two weeks, if you are careful. The tins are almost gone. The crackers are gone. The bottom of the box of sugared almonds is visible for the first time since day 59.

"The wedding's nearly over," says Wes.

On the hill's radio, the young voice says they have food up there. Potatoes. Apples. Jars of beans. They just have no truck to bring it, and no street to bring it through. For once, it's the hill that can't reach anybody.

On day 162, you notice that the woman eats half of what she is given and slides the rest back into the box when she thinks nobody is looking. Nora notices too. The next day, Nora hands her an extra cracker and stares at her until she eats it. That is the second peace treaty.

Wes builds the hook. He takes the long handle from the old floor polisher you pulled off the maintenance stairs on day 19, and tapes a bent piece of wire to the end of it. He tests it on the woman's scarf. It catches perfectly. She lets him know, with one look, that he will never test it on her scarf again.

The plan is simple, the way the worst plans always are. On the first hard frost, you go down through the hole onto the coach roof. Nora comes with you. She says he turns for her. Wes and the woman hold the rope.

On the night of day 163, the sky clears. The stars come out, sharp and close. Nora looks up at them from the gantry.

"Tomorrow," she says.

At dawn on day 164, the yard is white from the gate to the canopy.

The dead stand in it like statues in a garden. Frost on their shoulders. Frost on their heads. Their breath doesn't show, because they don't have any.

You climb over the gantry rail with the rope around your waist and the polisher handle in your hand. Across the canopy roof on your hands and knees. Each step booms. Below, a few thousand heads begin to turn toward the sound. Very, very slowly.

At the hole, Wes and the woman brace their feet and lower you down.

Your feet touch the roof of the coach. It is so cold that it burns through your shoes. Under the frost, it gives a little, like thin ice. The crowd is right there, on every side, an arm's length below the edge of the roof. Faces tilt up. Eyes find you. Nothing else moves.

Then Nora comes down the rope behind you, the purple band on her arm, the flashlight on its cord around her wrist. For a woman who hated every rung of the canopy ladder, she lands very gently. She looks down once at the crowd around the coach, closes her eyes, and opens them again. Then she nods at you.

You crawl to the hatch.

The handle is frozen. You twist. Nothing. You twist again, harder. With a crack like a snapped stick, it turns, and the hatch lifts. The smell of the coach comes up to meet you: old seats, cold air, a hundred and nine days of closed windows.

You look down into the aisle.

Eleven faces look back up at you. And at the very top of the aisle, right below the hatch, in a blue depot vest with two pockets, stands the night driver.

He lifts his head toward the light, slowly. His arms start to rise.

You lower the polisher handle through the hatch. Down past his face. Down past his reaching hands. Toward his chest. Toward the right-hand pocket. Behind him, ten passengers have started up the aisle, one slow step at a time.

The wire touches the pocket. Slides in. You feel it catch on something.

You pull.

His hand closes around the handle.

You pull harder. He pulls back. For a moment, you are in a tug of war with a man who has been dead since the first week, over the keys to his own bus. And he is winning.

Nora leans over the hatch beside you. She does not shout. Nothing here is allowed to be loud. She just holds out her open mitten hand, the way she did at the bottom of the coach steps every night for nine years, and says one word, very softly.

"Keys."

The night driver turns his face toward her.

His hand lets go of the handle and reaches for her instead. Up, up toward the hatch. And while he reaches, you pull the polisher handle out through the hatch, and on the end of the wire, swinging, there is a ring of keys.

Nora closes the hatch. Very gently. The handle turns with a small click.

"All yours," she whispers.

Then the sun comes over the rooftops.

You feel it before you see it. A little warmth on the back of your neck. And all around the coach, the frost on a few thousand shoulders starts to turn to water.

The heads that turned slowly start to turn faster. A hand comes up over the edge of the coach roof. Then another. The coach rocks on its springs as the crowd leans in.

"Rope," you whisper.

Nora goes first. Wes and the woman pull her up through the hole like a bucket out of a well. Then the rope comes down again, and you grab it, and the keys are in your teeth, because you need both hands. The coach rocks again. Green fingers brush your heel.

Then you are through the hole, lying on the canopy roof, looking at the sky, with a ring of keys in your mouth. You do not let go of it for a long time.

From day 165 to day 169, the keys hang on a nail in the bunker, next to the chart.

Nobody touches them. They just look at them. Five keys on a steel ring. One is big and black. One is small and silver. One has a little plastic tag shaped like a bus.

Nora sits on the bench in the evenings and watches them in the lantern light. She doesn't say anything. On day 167, she gets up, takes the ring off the nail, holds it in her open hand for a long minute, and hangs it back up. Then she goes to bed early.

You don't ask. Some things are all hers.

On day 168, the woman takes the big black key off the nail, weighs it in her hand, and puts it back.

"One out of three," she says. "Now the battery."

From day 170 to day 173, you work on the battery.

The solar panel on the canopy roof feeds a battery in the control box, the one that runs the loudspeaker. The woman says a battery is a battery. In the maintenance cupboard, Nora finds two long cables with heavy clips on the ends, coiled up behind the yellow paint shelf, where nobody has touched them for years.

"Jump leads," says the woman, as if somebody has given her flowers.

But the coach's battery is not inside the coach. Nora says it sits behind a small door low on its side, just behind the front wheel. Down where the crowd is.

So on the frost morning of day 172, you go back down through the hole. This time, Wes comes too. He lies flat on the coach roof and holds your ankles, and you hang head first over the side of the coach, a little at a time, until the battery door is right in front of your face.

On the other side of the door, a frozen walker stands close enough to share a scarf with. Its head is turning toward you. Slowly. Slowly.

You open the little door. You clip the first cable on. Then the second. The walker's face finishes turning. It looks at you from a hand's width away. Its mouth begins to open.

Wes pulls. You come up over the edge of the roof like a sack, and the walker's hands close on the air where your head was.

When you climb back through the hole, the woman is waiting with her goggles pushed up and her face very still. "Two out of three," she says. Her hands are shaking. You pretend not to see.

That evening, at the other end of the cables, the woman clips them onto the battery inside the control box. A tiny spark. Nothing else happens for a while. Then, down in the yard, so faint you almost miss it, a little light comes on behind the coach's windshield, on the dashboard.

"It's drinking," she says.

"The loudspeaker will have to go hungry for a while," says Wes. Nobody in the bunker objects.

From day 174 to day 177, the coach drinks sunlight.

Every day, you check the little light on the dashboard. Every day, it glows a bit brighter. The sun is low and weak this time of year, and the panel is half covered with frost every morning, so you climb out and wipe it with your sleeve, one strip at a time, the way you did in the summer.

Food is down to a week. Nobody counts it out loud anymore. The almonds are gone. The wedding is over.

On day 175, it snows for the first time. Snow on the panel means no sunlight, so you crawl out with a broom from the maintenance cupboard and sweep it clean, while the dead below stand with white caps on their heads like a crowd at a winter wedding.

On day 176, the hill's radio asks what the plan is. The woman tells them. There is a long silence on the other end.

"With the passengers still on board?" asks the young voice.

"We're working on that," says the woman.

On the evening of day 178, you and Nora sit on the bench with the lantern turned low, the way you have on so many last nights before so many stupid plans.

"If it works," you say, "the yard will be empty. The street will be empty. You could open a window."

"I could," says Nora.

"Would you leave? Go up the hill?"

She thinks about it for a long time.

"No," she says. "But I'd like to sit in the yard. In the daylight. Just once. Without anybody watching."

On the stairs, Wes is pretending to be asleep. The woman is sitting against the wall, facing the door. Her eyes are open. She heard every word.

On day 179, she says, "I want to see the driver's seat."

So you take her to the hole in the canopy roof, and she lies on her stomach and looks down through it for a long time, past the coach roof, through the frosted windshield, at the big steering wheel and the empty seat behind it. Her hands move a little on the metal, as if they are already holding something.

On day 180, the light on the coach's dashboard is bright and steady.

At dawn, the four of you stand on the gantry and look down at the coach. The battery is full. The keys are on the nail. The hole in the canopy roof is right above the hatch. And inside, packed between the seats, eleven passengers are waiting, faces turned up, as if they can feel that their bus is about to leave.

"So," says Wes. "How do we ask them to get off?"

Nobody answers. Down in the yard, the man in the blue depot vest stands at the top of the aisle, as he always has, right behind the driver's seat.

Then Nora looks up at the loudspeaker under the solar panel.

"We ask them politely," she says.

A hundred and eighty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 181. You have the keys to the last bus out of the depot. Its battery is full of winter sunlight. You have food for a few days. And between you and the driver's seat stand eleven passengers who have been waiting since day 55 for this bus to leave.
