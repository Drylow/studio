On day 361, Vera walks the bridge.

She does it alone, at first light, from one end to the other and back, in her long grey scarf, looking down. Not at the river. At the edge. At the place halfway across where the road just stops, and a long jagged bite of concrete has fallen into the brown water, and what is left beside it is barely wider than a coach.

Your name is Patch. Three hundred and sixty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot, then above it on a hill, and then you gave the whole yard to the dead and drove away. Behind you, a sign over an empty street says SEVEN ALIVE. GONE TO THE SEA. In front of you, there is a broken bridge. And somewhere past it, at the end of a beach on a postcard, thirty-one people are standing around a lighthouse, waiting for the lowest tide of the year.

Vera comes back and stands in front of the coach with her hands in the ends of her scarf.

"It's wide enough," she says. "Nearly."

Bram looks up at the roof of the coach, where four sections of motorway barrier are strapped down tight. Then he looks at the gap. Then he whistles three notes.

"Done," says Kit. "He means he's already done it. In his head."

They lay the barrier sections along the broken edge, side by side, flat on the road, sticking out over the gap like the fingers of a hand. Then you weigh down the back ends. Bram sits on one by himself. You and Kit sit on the next. Ruth and Nora on the third. On the fourth, the spare wheel from the truck, and every loose stone you can carry from the riverbank. It gives the road one more stripe of width. Not much. A stripe.

Vera climbs into the driver's seat. Wes walks backward in front of the coach, holding up his little round mirror so she can see the edge. Nobody else breathes.

The coach comes onto the bridge at the speed of a very slow queue. At the broken place, the right-hand wheels roll off the concrete and onto the barrier. The metal groans. It bends, just a little, under the weight, out over the water. You feel it through the seat of your trousers. And Vera turns the big wheel with two fingers, like a teacup, and keeps going, and doesn't look down once.

Then the wheels are back on concrete. The coach is across.

Then it's your turn.

The truck is narrower than the coach. It doesn't need the barrier. It only needs somebody who doesn't look at the water. You climb into the cab, and Nora climbs in beside you, with the turquoise bag on her knees, and she doesn't say anything at all. Wes walks backward in front of you with the mirror, the way he did for Vera.

At the broken place, you can see the river through the gap out of the corner of your eye. Brown. Fast. Very far down. You don't look at it. You look at Wes, and at his mirror, and at his free hand going left, left, straight, straight.

Then concrete. Then the far end. Then Vera, standing beside the coach, nodding once, the way she nods at a good bus.

Nora lets go of the bag. You notice she has been holding it so hard that her fingers have gone white.

Bram gets up, picks up the first section of barrier, and carries it back to the roof.

"In case anything needs a gate," says Wes. "I know. I know."

On day 362, you see the sea.

You come over the last low hill at midday, the truck behind the coach, and suddenly there is no more land. Just a long pale beach, curving away, and beyond it the sea, enormous and flat and shining, all the way to the edge of the sky.

You stop the truck. You don't decide to. Your foot does it.

Nora gets out without a word. She stands at the side of the road with the turquoise bag hanging from one hand, and she looks. Then she takes the postcard out of her bag, holds it up in front of her, and lowers it again.

It's the same. The long curve of sand. And at the far end of the beach, out in the water, a dark rock with little houses on it, and a white lighthouse. The Point.

And then you see the rest.

The road to it, the long, low causeway of stone, runs out across the sand from the end of the beach. The tide is half in, and the causeway is half covered. And along it, standing up to their knees in the sea, to their waists, to their chests, all the way out to the rock, stand the dead. A long dark line of them, facing the lighthouse. On the beach behind them, hundreds more. Waiting for the sea to go out.

At the end of the causeway, where a gate should be, there is nothing at all.

"They're on the rock?" says Wes quietly. "All of them?"

"All of us," says Ruth.

On day 362, after dark, Ruth knocks on the lighthouse.

There is no other way to call. The radio only hisses this close to the sea. So Ruth takes the flashlight from Nora and stands on the roof of the coach, and points it across the dark water at the white tower, and flashes it. Twice. Hard.

Nothing happens for a long time.

Then, at the top of the lighthouse, the great lamp comes on. It doesn't turn. It just flashes, slowly, straight at you. Once. Twice.

Kit makes a sound you have never heard a person make. Ruth hands the flashlight back to Nora, and sits down on the roof of the coach, and puts her face in her hands.

On day 363, a boat comes for you.

It comes at high tide, when the causeway is under the sea and the dead have been pushed back up onto the beach in a long, dripping crowd. A small wooden fishing boat, rowed around the far side of the rock, all the way along the coast, to a little stone slipway at the other end of the beach, where none of the dead are standing.

The man at the oars wears a dark green oilskin coat and a dark green fisherman's hat. When the boat touches the slipway, Kit is already in the water up to his knees.

"Dad."

Tam doesn't say anything for a long time. He just holds on to both of them, Kit and Ruth, in the shallow water, while the boat bumps against the stones. Bram stands on the slipway with his blanket around his shoulders, and whistles three notes, very softly.

Then Tam looks up at the rest of you. The coach. The truck. A man in a red beanie. A woman in a grey scarf. A girl with a purple band and a turquoise bag. And you.

"You're the depot," he says.

"We're the depot," says Nora.

From day 363 to day 364, Tam tells you about the door that opens once a year.

He draws it for you in the wet sand of the slipway, with an oar. The beach. The causeway. The rock. And then, across the bay, on the other side, something else. A long, low tongue of sand running out from the beach into the sea, to a small hump of an island with an old stone fort on it. Nobody lives there. Nobody has lived there for a hundred years.

"The Hump," says Tam. "You can only walk to it once a year. At the spring tide, the sea goes out so far that the sandbar comes up dry. For a few hours, you can walk across. Then the sea comes back. And it doesn't go out that far again until next spring."

You look at the drawing for a long time. So does Vera.

"A place with one way in," she says. "And the sea for a gate. For a year."

"My favorite kind," says Nora, before Vera can.

Vera looks at her. And she smiles. A real one. The second you have ever seen.

The plan takes all of day 364, and it is a plan you know by heart, because you have done it before. Only this time, the gate is the sea.

At the lowest water, Vera will drive the coach down onto the hard wet sand, with the horn. Tam says the sand will hold; the fishermen used to drive tractors on it. The dead will come off the causeway and the beach toward the horn, the way they always do. Vera will lead them across the bay, along the sandbar, onto the Hump. Wes will tape the horn down. And then Vera and Wes will go out through the roof hatch, across the island, and down to the rocks on the far side, where Tam will be waiting with the boat.

"Second door," says Wes.

"Second door," says Vera.

You and Nora will be in the truck at the top of the beach, to turn the stragglers who never follow anything the first time. And Bram, with the Point people, will carry four sections of motorway barrier across the rock to the end of the causeway.

In case anything needs a gate.

On day 365, the sea goes out.

You have never seen anything like it. All morning, the water slides away from the beach, farther and farther, until the whole bay is a shining plain of wet sand, and the causeway stands up out of it, dry from end to end, for the first time in a year. Out across the bay, a long pale back of sand rises out of the water and runs all the way to the Hump.

And the dead walk out along the causeway, toward the rock. Hundreds of them. Slowly. All of them.

On the rock, at the end of the causeway, Bram and the Point people bolt the four sections of barrier together, end to end, waist-high, from one side of the road to the other. The first of the dead reach it just as the last bolt goes in. They stop. They put their hands on it. They do not climb.

It's the second gate you have ever closed with everyone on the right side of it.

On day 365, Vera drives the coach onto the sand.

You watch from the truck at the top of the beach, with Nora beside you and the engine running. The coach rolls down the slipway and out onto the wet sand, beige and patient, with the depot's name down its side and Wes in the seat beside Vera, and its wheels leave two long dark lines behind it.

Then the horn.

Not short. Long. The one that empties a flyover. It rolls out across the whole bay, and the long dark line on the causeway turns at once, like a field of wheat in the wind.

They come down off the causeway onto the sand. Slowly. Then all of them. From the beach, from the causeway, from the edge of the water, hundreds of the dead walking out across the shining flats toward one beige bus.

A few on the beach don't move. The ones who never follow anything the first time. You give them a second time. One blast of the truck's horn, and then another, and they turn toward you, and then toward the bigger sound out on the sand, and they go.

The coach crawls across the bay, horn every few minutes, the dead strung out behind it in a line that runs back almost to the beach. Along the sandbar. Out toward the Hump. And everywhere around them, very quietly, the sea is starting to come back.

On day 365, the tide turns.

You can see it from the top of the beach. A thin bright line of water creeping in from the edges of the bay, filling the low places first, wrapping around the end of the sandbar. The coach reaches the Hump and grinds up a slope of pebbles onto the island, right up beside the old fort. The dead follow it up out of the wet sand, onto the island, around the coach.

The horn goes on and on. Wes has taped it. Neatly. Very neatly.

Then the roof hatch opens.

Vera climbs out first, and Wes after her, and they run across the roof and drop down on the far side, and across the island, past the fort, down toward the rocks, where a small wooden boat is bobbing in the water that wasn't there an hour ago.

Behind them, the last of the dead are still coming up off the sandbar, onto the island. Behind them, the sea is closing the sandbar like a zip.

Wes slips on the rocks. His little round mirror flies out of his pocket, flashes once in the sun, and drops into the sea. He doesn't even look. Tam grabs his arm. Vera is already in the boat, holding out both hands. Wes falls in on top of her.

Tam pulls on the oars.

And behind them, the sea finishes closing.

You stand on the top of the beach with Nora and watch the Hump become an island again. Around the coach, hundreds of the dead stand on the pebbles, facing a bus with a horn that is going to stop tonight and never blast again. And all around them, for a whole year, the sea.

Nora doesn't say anything. She just puts her hand on your shoulder. Three times. And then a fourth time, softer.

From day 366 to day 375, you live on the rock.

The Point is small. One crooked street of stone houses, a harbor with six boats, nets drying on every wall, and gulls that are not afraid of anybody. The thirty-one of them make room for seven more as if they had been keeping it ready.

Wes takes the cardboard with the totals out of his pocket, and adds thirty-one marks to people met, one at a time, very slowly, enjoying every single one. Then he gives the pencil to Tam, who adds five marks to his own wall, in the lighthouse, and then, after a while, two more, pressing very hard, with a fisherman's knot drawn next to them.

On day 368, Bram oils the new gate at the end of the causeway, and whistles three notes, and goes to sleep against it, sitting up.

Vera sleeps lying down, every night, with the door open.

From day 376 to day 380, Nora goes to the beach.

She waits for low tide. Then she walks out along the causeway, through Bram's gate, and onto the sand, and down to where the waves are coming in, and stands in the sea up to her ankles in her old cleaning shoes. You stand behind her. Wes stands behind you.

The beach is empty. Not one of the dead, from one end to the other. Just sand, and wind, and gulls.

"That's the beach," says Wes. "I promised."

He takes his red beanie off. Fourth time.

Nora takes the postcard out of her bag. Blue sea. Yellow sand. A white lighthouse. On the back, for the first time, she writes something, with Wes's pencil, leaning on your back. Then she gives it to you.

It says: Still here.

You put it in the orange bag, very carefully, next to Vera's goggles, next to everything else you are keeping.

From day 381 to day 389, the door opens and closes by itself.

Twice a day, the sea comes in and covers the causeway, and for six hours, nothing can walk to the Point. Twice a day, the sea goes out again, and the road comes back, and nobody comes down it. You learn the tides the way you learned the dead. When they come. Where they stop. You never have to close a gate. The sea does it for you.

Every sunset, Nora goes up the lighthouse with the radio, and says Still here into it. Most nights, nothing comes back. But one night, very faint, through the hiss, two words come back from a city a long way inland.

"Bridge's ours."

On day 386, Vera takes Ansel's lamp up to the top of the lighthouse, and lights it, and puts it in the window. Not the great lamp. Just a small, ordinary one, in the window of the room below.

"So somebody knows," she says, "that somebody has seen them."

On day 390, somebody sees it.

It is your turn on watch at the top of the lighthouse, with Kit, past midnight. The sea is calm and black. The causeway is under the water. Behind you, the small lamp burns in the window.

And out on the sea, far away, where there should be nothing at all, there is a light.

It's moving. Slowly. Toward the rock.

Kit grips your sleeve. You watch it come, for a long time, until it's close enough to tell that it is low on the water, and swinging a little, the way a lamp swings on a boat.

Then it flashes.

Twice. Hard.

Kit is already running down the stairs, shouting. Ruth comes up, and Tam, and Nora with the flashlight, and all of you stand at the window, staring out at the dark.

"That's our knock," says Ruth slowly.

"Every boat of ours is tied up in the harbor," says Tam.

Out on the black water, the light flashes again. Twice. Hard. And then, behind it, farther out, a second light comes on. And a third.

Three hundred and ninety days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 391. The dead are on an island the sea won't open for a year, Bram's gate stands across the causeway, and a postcard that says Still here is in your bag. For three hundred and ninety days, every door you ever had was on land. Tonight, for the first time, somebody is knocking on the sea.
