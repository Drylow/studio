On day 91, you are still driving.

The headlights are off. The woman steers by the shapes of the rooftops against the sky, and every few streets she gives the horn one short blast, the way you would remind a dog that you are still there. In the side mirror, the street behind you is moving. It is the tail of the river, and it is following you.

Your name is Patch. Ninety days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, and for the last month with Wes, who whispers all day and snores even quieter. Yesterday, the three of you filled the depot yard with thousands of the dead. Then you stepped into the street and shouted, so Nora could close the gate. She closed it. You were on the wrong side.

Now you are in a truck with a woman whose name you don't know, whose face you haven't seen, and who hasn't said a word for an hour.

"Where are we going?" you whisper.

"Up," she says.

Before dawn, the road starts to climb. It is the bypass, a long concrete ramp that lifts over the railway and comes down on the far side. At the very top, she stops in the middle of the road and leans on the horn. Not a short blast this time. A long one. It rolls out over the whole town.

Then she turns the key, and the engine dies.

The truck starts to roll. Down the far side of the ramp, in total silence, with no lights, faster and faster, the tyres hissing on the concrete. You grip the dashboard. She holds the wheel with two fingers, like a teacup.

"They walk to the last sound they heard," she says. "So I give them a last sound somewhere I'm not."

At the bottom, the truck coasts along an empty road, slows, and stops beside a dark petrol station. She doesn't turn the key again for two hours. By then, a kilometer is a wall.

When the sun comes up, you can see the flyover behind you, black from one railing to the other. The tail of the river is standing at the top, facing nowhere, waiting for a horn that is never going to blast again. Near the front, in the middle of the road, there is an olive vest.

The Doorman has a new door.

Days 92 and 93 are spent in slow loops through the lower town. The woman stops often, checks her mirrors, and only moves on when nothing is following that she didn't mean to bring. You sleep in the cab, sitting up. It is your first night in three months outside the depot walls, and you keep waking up to check that the sky hasn't moved.

At sunset on day 92, she hands you the radio.

"Depot," you whisper. "This is the truck."

A long hiss. Then Nora.

"Still here."

She tells you everything in one breath, very quietly. The gate held. The yard is full right up to the canopy. Nobody can step onto the gantry without a thousand faces turning up. So they live in the bunker now, and only go upstairs to flash the light.

"Your ceiling had hundreds," says Wes, leaning into the radio. "Ours has thousands. We're thinking of charging rent."

You ask Nora if she's alright. There is a pause long enough to say no in.

"You shouted," she says.

"You closed it."

"I know. I'm angry at both of us."

On day 93, the radio says the same thing at sunset, in fewer words. Still here. You realize it is the only news you need.

On day 94, the truck finally turns uphill.

The old waterworks sits on the very top of the hill, behind a tall wire fence, with three round water tanks and a brick pump house. Two people swing the fence gate open, and it doesn't make a sound. You have a good idea who supplies their oil.

The woman parks and climbs down from the cab, and for the first time, you see her in daylight. A pair of brown driving goggles pushed up on top of her round head. A long grey scarf wrapped once around her neck. She looks like someone who has spent three months telling strangers what to do, and being right about it.

"You expected horns," she says.

"I expected a face."

"Well. Now you've seen it. Don't spread it around."

About a dozen people come out to look at you. They look at your orange bandage. Every one of them has a blue one somewhere. Someone hands you a cup of water. It is cold and clean and tastes of absolutely nothing, which is the best thing you have tasted since day one.

In the old filter beds behind the tanks, they grow potatoes. Along the fence, three apple trees lean over the wire as if they would like to leave.

Wes was right. It is a very good view.

Days 95 to 97 are for learning to sleep above ground. The hill gives you a room in the pump house, with a mattress, a blanket and a window. On day one, you decided that not having a view was excellent interior design. Now you lie awake and stare at yours.

The silence is wrong. There are no feet on the ceiling. There is barely a ceiling. Every time the wind rattles the fence, you sit up.

So at midnight on day 95, you borrow a flashlight and climb the ladder on the side of the tallest water tank. From the top, the whole town lies dark below you. The motorway. The split in the road. The flyover, with its black line. And far down the hill, a dark square with a canopy. The depot.

On the hour, at its far corner, a small light blinks once.

Still here.

You point the flashlight at it, and press the switch. Once.

On the radio the next evening, Wes is delighted. "We saw you. You blink like a nervous lighthouse."

Nora doesn't mention it at all. But at the next hour, and every hour after that, the light at the depot blinks twice. Once for still here. Once for you.

On day 98, you start earning your water.

At dawn, the truck goes out to herd, and you go with it. You learn the code you first heard about on the radio, back when Wes was only a voice. One short blast at every corner. Slow, so the slow ones can keep up. Never stop where you want to come back.

All morning, you pull small crowds away from the bottom of the hill. Twenty here. Forty there. They come out of shops and doorways and follow the engine in long, ragged lines, like the worst parade in history. Then the truck turns a corner, coasts, and leaves them somewhere useless.

By day 100, you understand the hill. Clean water. Potatoes. A view. And every single morning, this. It never ends. Every crowd you lead away drifts back, the way rainwater finds the lowest street.

On the third morning, the truck passes the bottom of your old street. You see the depot wall. The top of the canopy. The sign, still hanging over the road. THREE ALIVE. And at the far end of the gantry, a bright spark, flashing in the sun.

Wes's mirror. He has been waiting all morning for the sound of the truck.

You don't have a mirror. So you put your hand out of the window and wave, very slowly, like a man who has never been interesting in his life.

On day 101, you learn what the hill calls your home.

They call it the pen.

Not unkindly. The way you would name something you are glad exists. The pen keeps the river off the hill. The pen is why they can sleep at night. And Nora and Wes, you learn, are the keepers.

You ask the woman when she can drive you back.

"I can't," she says.

She takes you to the window of the pump house and points down at the depot. "That yard is full. Every one of them is leaning on that gate. If I park an engine outside it, they all lean the same way at once. You've seen what that does."

You have. On day 78, the bolt jumped out of its hole with a crowd a fraction of this size.

"An engine at that gate isn't a ride," she says. "It's a key."

She makes you the offer she made on day 64. A bed. Clean water. Daylight. You can stay.

"I could have come up here on day 70," you say. "I stayed down there for her."

"I know," she says. "That's exactly why I don't give out my name."

She doesn't explain. By day 103, you decide you don't need her to. You need a plan. And you need rain.

On day 104, the radio is late.

When Nora finally answers, she is whispering even more quietly than usual. In the night, the yard shifted. Thousands of them, packed against each other, leaned at once toward the loading bay. The bay door boomed like a drum. The wall of suitcases slid. One of them tumbled down the corridor and burst open, full of somebody's summer shirts.

"And the door?" you ask.

"The latch held," she says.

Behind her, Wes whispers, "Tell him whose latch."

"Wes's latch held," says Nora, in the voice of someone handing out a medal she doesn't approve of.

Management came through after all.

But the push doesn't stop. All through day 105, the bay door creaks in a slow rhythm, like breathing. Nora and Wes carry every suitcase back into place, then add the empty shelves from the store, one at a time, without letting them touch.

On day 106, Wes counts what is left in the bunker. Three of the four water jugs. Food for maybe six weeks, if nobody eats the sugared almonds out of boredom. Nobody promises.

And from the top of the water tank, at midnight, you watch the depot blink twice, then wait, then blink twice again, like a light that is out of breath.

On day 107, you draw the route on the back of an old waterworks form, the way Nora drew the corridor on day two: thick lines wherever something can go wrong.

Down the hill road, on foot. Through the split. Down the long street, past the side road where the truck picked you up. To the depot wall, right under the sign. No engine. No horn. Just you, being uninteresting, for two kilometers.

Then up. Nora will lower the rope from the far end of the gantry, the same rope you hauled tins up on day 70. She doesn't like it. She agrees anyway, then goes quiet on the radio, which is how you know she means it.

But you need one more thing. You need rain. Rain covers footsteps. And rain pulls every walker in the yard in under the canopy, away from the gate and the wall, the way it has since the first week. In the rain, the yard will be watching the coach, not the sky.

The woman reads your drawing for a long time. "This is stupid," she says. Then she marks the three streets where her herding left crowds this month, and crosses out your route through two of them.

On day 110, she puts a radio in your hand. "The hill only has three," she says. "Don't make it two."

Day 111 is dry. So is day 112. You check the sky three times a day, a habit you picked up on day 50 and never put down.

You pack the orange bag. Water. Crackers. The blue bandages. The hill's radio, wrapped in a shirt so it can't knock against anything. And six apples from the trees by the fence, the first fresh food you have touched since you were waiting for a bus.

On the evening of day 113, the woman sits beside you on top of the water tank and watches the depot blink.

"Wes once asked you on the radio," she says, "whether Patch was a name or an injury."

"Both."

"Mine's only a name," she says. "Nothing happened to it. I'd like to keep it that way."

"Wes says names are for people who stay."

"Wes talks a lot."

"I'm staying too," you say. "Just not up here."

She looks at you for a long time over the top of her scarf. For a moment, you think she is going to tell you. Then the depot blinks twice, and she looks at that instead.

On day 114, the clouds come in low over the hill and sit there, heavy and grey. Clouds are not rain. You have known that since day 39.

Day 115 starts with the sound of rain on the pump house roof, and you are already dressed.

The fence gate opens without a whisper. The woman stands beside it with her scarf pulled over her head. She doesn't wish you luck. She says, "One short blast at every corner." You don't have a horn. You suspect she knows.

The hill road runs with water. You walk down the middle of it, slowly, steadily, without stopping. At the split, the motorway ramp climbs away into the rain toward the city. You take the other road. Down.

The lower town is grey and full of small noises. Drips. Gutters. A shop sign creaking on its chain. Rain makes everything a little bit interesting, which means nothing is very interesting. That is the whole plan.

At a bus stop, a walker sits on the bench, facing the road, waiting. You pass within arm's reach. It doesn't turn. On day one, that bench was yours.

Past the side road where the truck came for you. Into your old street. And there is the gate. Solid, wet, closed. You walk right past it. From the other side comes a sound like the sea: thousands of feet shuffling on concrete in the rain, all of them heading for the canopy. The gate creaks once as you pass. You don't stop. You don't breathe.

The sign hangs over the street at the end of the gantry, heavy with rain. THREE ALIVE. The yellow paint has started to run. And beside it, a rope comes down the wall, swaying, with a loop tied at the bottom.

On the gantry above, a small figure with a purple band on her arm holds the other end. Behind her, a red hat.

You put your foot in the loop, and you climb. The rope is wet, and so are your hands, and the wall seems twice as high as it did from the roof of the truck on day 70. Halfway up, your head rises above the top of the wall, and you see the yard.

Thousands of them. Shoulder to shoulder, from the gate all the way to the canopy. Packed around the coach, where eleven faces press against the glass. The rain is drawing them in under the canopy, slowly, like a tide. But the ones nearest the wall notice something moving above them. One face tilts up. Then ten. Then a hundred.

They can't climb. They can only watch. You have never been watched by so many people in your life, and that includes the day you shouted.

Nora and Wes pull. You come over the rail and lie on the gantry, soaking wet, like something the river washed up.

Nobody says a word. Nothing up here is allowed to be loud. Nora kneels down beside you. She knocks on your shoulder three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

You start laughing without making a sound, and for a long time, you can't stop.

On day 116, you wake up in the bunker, and there is a ceiling again.

It has thousands of feet on it. On day one, it had hundreds, and you lay awake all night. Now you sleep for eleven hours straight. Wes is back on the stairs with his hat over his eyes, snoring so softly that Nora decides to allow it all over again.

You empty the orange bag on the bench. Six apples. Nora holds one up to the lantern for a long time, as if it might be a trick. Then the three of you discover that apples are the loudest food in the world. Every bite cracks like a branch. You end up sucking on each piece until it gives up.

Wes studies the chart on the wall. The people met column still has one mark.

You take the pencil. You met a dozen people on the hill, but you add only one mark. The other eleven never turned a truck around for you.

"What's she like?" Wes asks.

You think about it. "Goggles."

He nods, as if that explains everything.

On day 117, the hill's radio sits on the rack next to the depot's. Two radios. For the first time, the bunker can listen to the hill and talk to it at the same time. Wes says it is the most luxurious thing that has ever happened to him.

And outside, in the rain, the sign over the street says THREE ALIVE, and it is true again.

On day 120, the rain finally stops, and at dawn you climb to the gantry to look at the town.

The hill. The tanks. The split in the road. And the flyover.

It's empty.

For a month, the tail of the river stood on top of that bridge, facing nowhere. Now the concrete is bare from one railing to the other. A moment later, you find them. A long dark line, moving slowly along the road beyond the railway.

Uphill.

Rain makes them look for a roof, and the flyover doesn't have one. For five days, they have been drifting. And somewhere in that drift, every morning, they heard a horn. One short blast at every corner.

Wes climbs up behind you with the hill's radio held against his chest. It is already crackling.

"Depot," says the woman. For the first time, her voice is not calm. "They're on the hill road. Hundreds. More behind them."

A pause.

"I need your speaker."

A hundred and twenty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. And this time, you are on the same side of the gate. Tomorrow is day 121. The tail you led away is climbing toward a dozen people and their clean water. The hill that calls your home a pen needs it to open for business. And the only way to save them is to call thousands more of the dead to your door.
