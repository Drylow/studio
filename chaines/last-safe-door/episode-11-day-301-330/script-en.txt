On day 301, Wes peels the tape off the switch.

Nobody has slept. At first light, the radio crackles, and Ansel is still whispering.

"They came over the fields in the night," he says. "They're on the bridge again. They're in the streets. They're standing under our windows." A breath. "We're all upstairs. We're fine. But the spring is on the other side of them, and we have water for nine days."

Your name is Patch. Three hundred days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, Wes and Vera, and for the last month with three people from the sea: Ruth in her yellow raincoat, Kit in his white helmet, and Bram, who doesn't talk. A month ago, you put a few thousand of the dead in a gravel pit and closed a gate on them. Then it rained. And this morning, they are standing under the windows of nineteen people who were finally allowed to stop whispering.

Wes doesn't say anything. He just walks across the yard to the canopy, climbs the ladder, and stands under the loudspeaker, with its switch taped on OFF since day 239.

He picks at the corner of the tape with his thumbnail. Then he pulls it off, all in one long strip, and sticks it to his own sleeve, so he doesn't lose it.

"It's on the list of things I'm sorry about," he says. "Might as well be on the list of things I'm not."

On day 302, you ask the question again.

"Where do we take them?"

Not away. Away is just somebody else's door. You have learned that twice now.

Ruth spreads the road map on the bench. Ansel's pencil circle by the sea. Her own thin line to it. Fields, towns, motorways, rivers. Bram looks at it for a long time, and whistles two notes.

"Not the pit," says Vera. "It's got two doors now."

"Not the sea," says Ruth. "Two days at their speed is two weeks. And then they'd be on our beach."

You look at the map. Then you look up, at the yard around you. The high walls. The one gate. The canopy, the loudspeaker. The dark rectangle where the coach stood for seven months, with eleven passengers inside it.

You lived under the river for two hundred days. You know exactly how many of them this yard can hold. All of them.

"Here," you say.

Nobody answers. Nora looks at the bunker door for a long time.

"It's got one way in," says Vera, at last.

"And we know how to live under it," says Wes.

"No," you say. "Not this time. This time, we give them the yard. And we go and live somewhere with a view."

Nora finally looks at you. Then she nods, once, the way she nods when something is decided and there is no point talking about it anymore.

From day 303 to day 305, you empty the bunker.

The hill people come down with handcarts, and everything you own goes up the hill road, one load at a time. The tins. The blankets. The jump leads. The radio from the shelf. The rope on the bolt stays where it is, because you are going to need it one last time.

Nora goes to the locker room alone. She comes back with the postcard and the road map, folded together, and puts them in the very bottom of the turquoise bag. She leaves the locker door open.

Wes stands in front of the chart on the bunker wall with a piece of cardboard and a pencil, copying the totals. Days. Zombies gone. People met. Days in daylight. When he gets to the bottom, he writes one more line.

"Moved house," he reads out. "Day 305."

You don't take much from the bunker yourself. Just the orange bag. It has everything in it already. On the way up the stairs, you stop at the steel door and put your hand flat on it, once, the way Bram listens to concrete. It's cold. It always was.

On the last night, the seven of you sleep in the bunker together, shoulder to shoulder on the floor, with Bram sitting up against the steel door. Nora turns the lantern down, the way she has every night for three hundred and five nights. Then, for the first time, she turns it all the way off.

On day 306, you drive to the city.

Three of you go. You drive. Vera sits beside you, because nobody knows the roads to the city better. Bram sits by the window, because if anything goes wrong with the truck, Bram is the one who will fix it, without a word.

Wes stays on the canopy roof, with the microphone on its long cable. Nora stays on the gantry, with the rope. Ruth and Kit stay on the hill, with the radio and the mirror. And the gate stands wide open behind you, the way it hasn't stood since day one.

You drive out under the sign. FOUR ALIVE. For the first time, the sign is wrong in a way that makes you smile.

On day 307, the city is full again.

You come over the last rise of the motorway at dawn, and there it is. The junction. The bridge, black from railing to railing. The slip road, black. And beyond them, the edge of the city, the empty streets, all filled up again with the dead, standing in rows like people waiting for news.

On the corner, the tall, narrow house. In the top window, a lamp is lit.

"They put it back," says Vera quietly.

You stop the truck at the bottom of the slip road, exactly where the coach stood on day 254. You roll the window down. And you put your hand on the horn.

Not short. Long.

The whole city turns.

It's like watching the tide turn. First the ones on the bridge, then the slip road, then street after street, faces lifting, bodies turning, all at once, toward the one sound in the world. Up in the top window, a long coat and a yellow mitten press against the glass. Ansel lifts one hand. Then the green one.

At the front of the river, coming down the slip road first, are two figures you would know anywhere. A navy depot vest with two pockets. And an olive one, one step behind.

"Of course," says Vera. "Of course it's them."

The radio on the dashboard crackles. Ansel, not whispering anymore.

"Don't hurry on our account," he says. "We've waited a week. We can wait while you turn round."

"We're not turning round," says Vera. "We're taking them home."

A pause. "Whose home?"

You look at the long black river coming down the slip road toward you.

"Ours," you say.

From day 308 to day 312, you lead them home.

At the speed of a very slow queue. One long blast every few minutes. The truck crawls back down the motorway you have driven three times now, with the river walking behind it, filling every lane, kilometer after kilometer of the dead, all following the same white truck home.

At night, you stop on the empty side of the central barrier, and turn the engine off, and the river stops behind you and waits. Bram sleeps sitting up. Vera sleeps sitting up. You don't sleep much at all.

On day 309, the truck's engine starts coughing on the long hill after the bridge, and the river catches up until the first hands are nearly on the tailgate. Bram gets out, lifts the bonnet, does something with one finger and a strip of Wes's tape, and gets back in. The engine stops coughing. Bram whistles three notes and goes back to sleep.

Every sunset, Ansel comes on the radio. "Spring's ours," he says. "Bridge is ours." And now, after the second night, "Thank you," every single time, as if he is afraid you will stop if he forgets.

On day 310, the truck passes a field you recognize. Frost has turned into mud, and the mud has turned green at the edges, but you know it. Vera knows it too. She makes you stop, and gets out, and walks into the field in her long grey scarf, a hundred meters ahead of the river, without hurrying once.

She comes back with something small and dirty in her hand. A travel alarm clock.

"It stopped," she says.

She winds it, carefully, sitting in the cab, while the river catches up behind you. And it starts to tick.

From day 313 to day 315, you cross the viaduct.

The same viaduct where you spent the night of day 216, with Vera in the cab for the first time. You stop at the top of it on day 313, and the river fills the whole long bridge behind you, end to end, and stands there, in the dark, waiting for the next horn.

That night, Nora comes on the radio from the gantry.

"Still here," she says.

"Still here," you say.

"Still here," says Vera, from beside you, which she has never done before.

There is a long silence on the other end. Then Nora's voice comes back, and it doesn't sound quite steady.

"Two more days," she says. "The yard's ready."

On day 316, the depot calls them home.

You come down off the main road at midday, with the whole river strung out behind you. Half a kilometer from the depot, the hill road climbs away to the left, and that's where the plan says the truck has to leave them.

You take one long look in the mirror. The navy vest. The olive vest. And behind them, thousands.

Then you switch the engine off, and let the truck roll silently up the hill road, and the river stops behind you, all of it, on the main road, facing nowhere, waiting for the next sound.

On the radio, you say one word. "Now."

And on the canopy, Wes reaches down over the edge and switches on the loudspeaker.

It crackles. It hums. And then Wes's voice comes out of it, rolling down the street and over the walls and across the fields, louder than anything you have heard in months.

"Passengers for Platform 1," says Wes. "This is your last call. Last call for Platform 1."

On day 316, the river comes back to the yard.

The way it came in on day 90. Down the street, all of it. The first ones walk through the open gate as if they've just come home from work. Then more. Then the yard starts to fill, from the back wall forward, the way a bathtub fills, slow and certain. Around the canopy. Around the dark rectangle. Around the cold fire.

Wes keeps talking. "Please have your tickets ready. Please mind the gap." He tells them the weather. He tells them that management apologizes for the delay. He tells them the sad story of a man who once lived on a roof. His voice gets hoarse. He keeps going.

On the gantry over the gate, Nora holds the rope of the gate bolt in both hands.

They keep coming all afternoon, until the yard is fuller than it ever was, wall to wall, the way it was in the winter, when you lived under them.

And then there are only two left in the street.

The night driver walks through the gate.

The Doorman doesn't.

He stops right in the gateway, exactly where he has always stood, and puts both hands flat on the open gate. The way he did on day 67. The way he did all winter. He has never been on the inside of this gate in his life. And the gate can't close with him standing in it.

The loudspeaker calls and calls. He doesn't move.

On the gantry, Nora leans over the edge, right above him. And she does something nobody ever taught her. She knocks on the top bar of the gate. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

The Doorman lifts his head toward the sound. Then he takes his hands off the gate, and walks inside, one step behind the night driver, the way he has been since day 190.

Nora lets go of the rope. The gate swings shut. The bolt drops.

"Welcome home," she says.

On day 316, at sunset, somebody has to switch it off.

By late afternoon, Wes has no voice left at all. So he holds the radio up to the microphone, and on the hill, Kit reads out an old bus timetable, stop by stop, and the loudspeaker carries it down into the yard. If it keeps calling all night, it will call every dead thing for miles. And the switch is down under the edge of the canopy, over the heads of the whole river.

Wes goes. Nobody could stop him if they tried. He crawls along the canopy roof, flat on his stomach, to the very edge, right above the switch. Below him, thousands of faces are turned up toward the loudspeaker, mouths open, hands lifted, as if they are all waiting for one more announcement.

Wes reaches down. His fingers find the switch.

Click.

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight. And then nothing at all. The river stands in the yard, exactly where the last sound left it, facing the canopy, waiting for a voice that is never going to come back.

Wes crawls back along the roof and onto the gantry. Over the gate, he stops, takes the strip of tape off his sleeve, and very neatly tapes the bolt shut. Then he climbs over the wall and down Bram's ladder into the empty street.

From day 317 to day 323, you live with a view.

The hill people give you a long room with big windows at the top of their hill. They offered you a place up here back on day 64, and you said no. A bunker for a view, Wes called it. Now you have the view.

From the window, you can see the depot. The canopy. The solar panel. The yard, full again, wall to wall, quiet as a field of wheat with no wind. And over the street, the sign, swinging a little. FOUR ALIVE. Over a yard full of the dead. You've never seen anything that looked so much like home and so little like it at the same time.

Every sunset, Nora says Still here into the radio. Every sunset, Ansel answers. "Bridge is ours. Again."

On day 320, Kit asks why the sign still says four.

"Because we're not there anymore," says Nora. "We'll change it when we're somewhere."

Every night, after Nora says Still here, Ruth takes the radio and turns it slowly to another frequency, and says the same thing into it, again and again.

"Point, Point, this is Ruth. Do you read?"

Nobody ever answers. She sits with it in her lap until Kit falls asleep against her shoulder, and then a little longer.

From day 324 to day 329, you think about the sea.

There is a list on the wall of the long room now, in Wes's writing. Things we'd take. The first thing on it is still the orange bag.

Seven people. One truck with a cab built for three. Two days of roads that nobody has driven for a year.

"We need something bigger," says Ruth.

Bram looks out of the big window, down at the depot, at the dark rectangle in the middle of the full yard where something bigger used to stand. He whistles two notes. Then, slowly, a third.

"That means maybe," says Kit.

On day 327, Ansel calls with news. He has sent two of his people along the farm tracks to look at the gravel pit. The water has gone back down into the gravel. The slope of mud is drying in the sun. The pit is almost empty.

"Almost?" says Vera.

"There are a few dozen left at the bottom," says Ansel. "Stuck in the mud around the coach. They don't seem to want to go anywhere."

Vera turns the travel alarm clock over in her hands. It is still ticking.

"The coach," she says. "Is it still standing up?"

"It's still standing up."

Vera says nothing else. But that night, she sleeps lying down, for the first time in a year.

On day 330, the radio speaks with a new voice.

Late at night. Everybody is asleep except Kit, who sits by the radio every night now, the way Nora used to. And so it is Kit who hears it first, through the hiss, very faint, as if it is coming from the bottom of the sea.

"Ruth. Ruth, if you can hear me. It's Tam."

Kit grabs the radio with both hands. "Dad!"

Everybody is awake now. Ruth is across the room before you can blink, her yellow raincoat over her nightclothes.

"Kit," says the voice, and it cracks in the middle. Then it steadies. "Listen to me. Both of you. Put your mother on."

"I'm here," says Ruth.

"They're coming down the beach," says Tam. "Not a few dozen. Hundreds. More every low tide. They walk out on the road and they stand at our gate, and the sea pushes them back, and they come again. The gate is leaning, Ruth."

Ruth closes her eyes.

"And in five weeks," says Tam, "it's the spring tide. The lowest water of the year. The road will be dry for half a day. They'll have all the time in the world."

Then the hiss swallows him, and he's gone.

Three hundred and thirty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 331. The river is home in your yard, the bolt is taped shut, and the bridge belongs to the living again. But two days away, at the end of a beach on a postcard, there is a door the sea opens by itself, twice a day. In five weeks, it will open wider than it has all year. And the dead are already waiting in front of it.
