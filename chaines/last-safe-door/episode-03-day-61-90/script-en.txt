On day 61, the truck is still outside your gate.

Yesterday, you asked for one night to decide. The woman on the radio said one night was all the crowd would give you. Then she switched off her engine and slept in her cab. That is either trust or a very good bluff.

Your name is Patch. Sixty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it. You emptied the yard with your trash. You locked eleven walkers in a coach. You hung a sign over the street that says TWO ALIVE. Somebody read it.

At first light, Nora climbs to the gantry and comes back down fast. Figures are walking back up the street. Slowly. The way the dead do everything.

"Now or never," she whispers.

You both know this gate. It does not open. It screams. So you agree on a gap exactly one person wide, and not a centimeter more.

You lift the bolt and pull. The gate shrieks along its rail, and down the street, every head turns. A man squeezes through sideways, hugging a cardboard box to his chest. He wears a red knit hat, and a small round mirror hangs from a cord around his neck. You shove the gate back. It screams again. The bolt drops.

Outside, an engine starts. One short blast of the horn. Then the woman's voice, on the radio.

"I don't go into places with one way out. Wes does. He'll explain. He's had a week of practice."

The engine rolls away down the street, and the footsteps turn to follow it.

The man in the red hat turns to you.

"Hello," he whispers. "I'm the explanation."

In the box, there is a can of oil and two packs of batteries. Before anyone can stop him, Wes kneels at the gate wheel and pours half the can onto it.

"Housewarming present," he says. "I've heard about your gate."

Days 62 and 63 teach you that a bunker for two is a bunker for two.

Wes sleeps on the stairs below the steel door, with his hat over his eyes. He snores so softly that Nora decides to allow it.

He studies your chart. Days on the left, walkers gone on the right.

"You need a third column," he says. "People met."

You add it. It has one mark.

Then he eats a sugared almond, closes his eyes, and says it is the nicest wedding he has ever been to.

The truck took him off his roof on day 52. It belongs to about a dozen people living at the old waterworks on the hilltop, behind a fence, beside tanks of clean water. Every morning, they drive out and herd the dead away from the hill.

"Like you," Wes says. "Only with a horn instead of rubbish."

On day 63, he tells you the rest. From the hilltop, you can see the motorway to the city. For two weeks, something has been moving along it. The people on the hill call it the river. Thousands of the dead, all walking the same way, at the speed of a very slow queue.

"How long before it gets here?" Nora asks.

"Four weeks," says Wes. "Maybe less."

On day 64, with fresh batteries, the radio can finally run for longer than five minutes. The woman uses every second.

She still won't give her name. Wes says she thinks names are for people who stay.

Her plan is simple. Where the motorway reaches town, the road splits. Uphill to her people, or downhill past your gate. Her truck can lead the river to the split. Something else has to call it down. Something loud that never runs out of fuel.

You and Nora look up at the same moment. Above the ceiling, under the canopy, sits the loudspeaker you spent two weeks trying to shut up.

"Your yard has walls," the woman says. "Your gate locks. Your speaker runs on sunlight. Call them in. Close the gate. Then come up the hill. Food. Clean water. Daylight."

"A bunker for a view," says Wes, who is trying to help.

That night, you argue in whispers. You want the hill. Sunlight. Other voices. A door that shuts without a desk behind it. Nora is quiet for a long time. Then:

"She wants to put the whole city on our ceiling."

On day 65, the woman sets a deadline. "Day 70. Have an answer."

On day 66, Nora walks out to the coach.

She sits on the concrete beside the folding door. Behind the glass, the man in the blue depot vest stands at the top of the aisle, facing her. He turns whenever she comes close.

You sit down next to her.

"Up there, I'm a stranger with a teal bag," she says. "Down here, I know where every key is. Which stair creaks. Which locker was mine. I've kept this building going longer than it's kept me."

She nods at the coach. "And if we fill the yard, he gets the whole city for passengers. He'd have hated that."

You don't argue. Some arguments are lost before they start.

On day 67, the pounding comes back. The crowd the truck keeps leading away has drifted home to your gate, the way rainwater finds the lowest street. From the gantry, you watch the first one arrive: a walker in a ragged olive vest, who walks straight up to the gate and puts both hands on it, like a man waiting to be buzzed in. By evening, forty more stand behind him.

Wes names him the Doorman. You wish he hadn't. Now he's somebody.

By day 68, Wes has inspected everything you own and found most of it disappointing.

He stops at the bay door. The snapped latch. The desk. The trolley wedged behind it.

"Who did this?" he asks.

"Management," says Nora.

So Wes takes the latch off the office door, which has had nothing to protect for seven weeks, and spends two days fitting it to the bay door. One screw at a time, each with its own tiny squeak. Nora holds the flashlight and barely breathes.

On day 69, the bay door clicks shut on its own for the first time since day 39. Nora pushes it. It holds. She pushes again, harder, the way you test good news.

"Management said they'd replace this," she says.

Sixty-two days late. But management came. You leave the desk and the trolley where they are anyway. Some trust takes longer to install.

Day 70 is the deadline. You and Nora make the decision the night before, on the bench in the dark.

You won't go up the hill. She won't keep the yard. It is the first argument you have both lost, and somehow it feels like the right result.

At mid-morning, the truck comes back, leads the Doorman's crowd around the block, then returns alone and parks against the depot wall, right under your sign.

"You can have the yard," you tell the radio. "You can't have the depot. We stay."

There is a long pause.

"Fair," she says.

Two silhouettes climb onto the roof of the truck. You lower a rope from the end of the gantry, they tie sacks to it, and you haul. Tins. Crackers. Ration packs. Four big jugs of water from the hill. More batteries. Another can of oil. A first-aid kit full of bandages. Every one of them is blue.

You keep the orange one on. You have been through too much together.

Wes could leave with the truck. He looks at the cab, then at Nora.

"I spent two months as a very obvious sheep," he says. "I'd like to try being the dog."

"Day 90," says the woman. "Give or take."

The engine pulls away. A hand waves once from the cab window, and is gone.

From day 71 to day 75, you build a trap with yourselves inside it.

All the food and water go down to the bunker. The suitcases from the coach's luggage hold go up against the bay door, behind the desk and the trolley. A wall of other people's holidays.

Nora fetches the canopy ladder from the maintenance cupboard. She used to climb it once a year to clean the gutters, and she hated every rung. You lash it to the far end of the gantry, where it reaches down into the yard beside the gate. Whoever closes the gate will need a way out above the crowd.

That will be you. When the gate slides open, its heavy panel lies flat against the inside of the wall, with a narrow slot between them. You practice standing in it, the orange bag held against your chest. It is the smallest room you have ever lived in, and you have lived in some small rooms.

Wes will stay at the control box. Nora will stand at the top of the ladder with the flashlight. One flash means close it.

On day 74, Nora climbs out to the sign with the last of the yellow paint. She crosses out TWO and writes THREE. Wes pretends not to notice that she is pleased.

On the bunker wall, the chart grows a fourth column. Days until the river. It is the first column you have ever kept that counts down.

On the night of day 76, you wake up, and Wes is not on the stairs.

He is at the top of them, by the steel door, whispering into the radio. You only catch the end.

"No. I haven't told them."

You lie very still. You have been betrayed by a latch, the rain and a trolley. It never occurred to you that a man in a red hat could join the list.

In the morning, you ask him. Not loudly. Nothing down here is loud.

Wes takes off his hat, which you have never seen him do. Underneath, his head is exactly as round as yours. Then he tells you.

The hill counted the river again. It is not thousands. It is many thousands. More than your yard can hold. The speaker can take the front of it. The truck will try to lead the rest away. But if the tail is still coming when your yard is full, nobody knows what happens at the gate. The woman told him to keep quiet until it was too late for you to change your minds.

"I told her you wouldn't," Wes says. "She said everybody does."

Nora studies him. "So why tell us?"

"Two months alone on a roof," says Wes. "I'm out of practice at keeping quiet."

Nobody changes their mind. But that night, Nora sleeps with the flashlight in her hand.

Day 78 brings more of them to the gate. The river's early strays are arriving, and the Doorman's crowd is twice the size it was.

That night, the pounding changes. It falls into a rhythm, as if they are all leaning at once. Then a dull clank, and the gate moves.

You learn something about oil. It works for everyone. The bolt has jumped out of its hole, and the gate that used to scream now slides open with a polite little whisper. A hand's width. Then two.

You and Wes go down the ladder in the dark, while Nora holds the flashlight from the gantry. Green fingers are already curling around the edge of the gate. Among them, an olive sleeve. Of course.

You throw your weight against the panel. It slides back slowly, then slower, and the fingers slip out at the last moment. Wes stamps the bolt down into its hole.

On day 79, you find a steel bar in the maintenance cupboard and wedge it into the rail beside the wheel. Now the gate can't move until somebody lifts it out.

The Doorman pounds on it all day, deeply offended.

On day 80, Nora opens the control box. Next to the switch, a small battery light glows. It is half green.

"Half isn't enough," she says. "It has to call all morning."

The solar panel on the canopy roof has spent two months collecting dust and birds. Somebody has to clean it. The canopy roof is thin metal, and right under it sits the coach.

You climb over the gantry rail with a cloth and a bottle of water. You crawl on your hands and knees, spreading your weight, the way you would cross ice you didn't trust. Every time the roof flexes, it booms.

Below you, the passengers notice. Eleven green faces tilt up at the coach windows. The man in the blue vest presses against the glass. The whole coach rocks once on its springs, like a sigh.

So you clean slowly. One strip a day.

On day 83, the light in the control box turns fully green.

"Now it can shout all day," says Nora.

"Good," says Wes. "Somebody should."

On the night of day 84, you hear something you have never heard before.

It comes from far away, beyond the hill. A low, endless rustle, like the sea, or like traffic on a road that has no traffic anymore. It doesn't rise. It doesn't fall. It just continues.

"That's them," Wes whispers. "That's feet."

At dawn on day 85, you see it from the gantry. Far off, past the rooftops, a dark line that moves when nothing else does.

The radio crackles. "Five days," says the woman. "Maybe four. The river doesn't keep a schedule."

Then something happens at your own gate that hasn't happened in weeks. The pounding stops. One by one, the Doorman's crowd turns away and starts walking up the street, toward the sound. Even the Doorman goes.

On day 86, your gate has nobody behind it. It is the most frightening silence of the month.

Days 87, 88 and 89 are for checking things that are already checked.

The steel bar comes out of the gate rail. The wheel gets more oil, though the gate already slides like a rumor. The orange bag gets water, crackers and the blue bandages, because it goes wherever you go. Wes rehearses the switch with his eyes shut.

On the last night, you and Nora sit on the bench with the lantern turned low.

"The rule," she says.

"If they come toward the gate, you close it."

"Even if you're outside."

"Even if I'm outside."

She doesn't say another word all evening. You have learned what that means.

On the stairs, Wes pretends to be asleep, and does it badly. He forgets to snore.

Day 90 starts with the sea in the streets.

The rustle is under every other sound now. Nora climbs to the far end of the gantry with the flashlight. Wes opens the control box. You climb down the ladder, lift the bolt and slide the gate open. It barely whispers. Then you step into the slot between the panel and the wall, with the orange bag held against your chest, and you wait.

From the slot, you can see one narrow strip of street. For an hour, nothing.

Then, around the corner, one short blast of a horn.

Above you, a click. And for the first time in sixty-two days, a voice you know rolls across the yard.

"Passengers for Platform 1."

The first ones come through the gate almost at once. Then more. You stop counting. No number would help. Legs pass the edge of the slot, close enough to touch. Shoulders brush the panel. The metal hums against your back. Nobody looks in.

The river pours into the yard all morning. It fills the concrete from the canopy almost to the gate. It packs around the coach, where eleven passengers watch through the glass like the only ones who bought tickets. It stands under the canopy with its faces turned up, listening to an announcement for a bus that is never coming.

At midday, the voice stops. Wes has switched it off. The yard is full.

The river is not.

Late on day 90, the tail is still coming.

Slower now. Stragglers. Twos and threes. Up on the gantry, a small light flashes once. Close it. You reach for the edge of the panel. And there is always one more, stepping into the gap. And one more behind that one.

The tail is longer than anyone said. It will not end while the gate is open. And the gate will not close while it keeps coming.

So you do the one thing Nora asked you never to do, on day 4, the first time she ever spoke to you.

You slip out of the slot, through the gateway and into the street. Between them. Around them. Ten steps. Twenty. Then you turn, fill your lungs, and shout.

"Over here!"

Every head in the gateway turns. Every head in the street turns. For ninety days, you have tried very hard not to be interesting. Now you are the most interesting thing in the city.

They come out of the gateway, toward you. The last one out wears a ragged olive vest.

Behind him, through the narrowing gap, you see an arm with a purple band, pushing. The gate slides shut with a whisper. The bolt drops.

Nora kept the rule. You made her promise.

You back up the street, and the tail follows, and the top of the street is only more street. Then an engine, close. The white truck swings out of a side road with its cab door already open. A hand reaches out.

"Get in."

You get in. The door slams. The truck rolls, and the tail of the river turns to follow the engine.

In the gloom of the cab, the woman is only a shape with two hands on the wheel.

"Wes said you were the careful one," she says.

"I am."

"Then I'd hate to meet the other one."

She doesn't drive toward the hill. Not with that behind you. She drives the other way, into streets you have never seen.

At the corner, you look back. Above the depot wall hangs a sign that says THREE ALIVE. Tonight, only two of them are behind it. And at the far end of the gantry, a small light blinks once.

Still here.

Ninety days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. For the first time, they are on opposite sides of a gate. Tomorrow is day 91. Nora and Wes are under the biggest horde in the city. You are in a truck with a woman whose face you have never seen, leading the rest of it somewhere she hasn't told you. And for the first time since day one, you are on the outside of the last safe door.
