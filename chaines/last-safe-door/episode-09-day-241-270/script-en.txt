On day 241, the driver comes to the radio.

Nobody has slept much. The radio sits on the bench between the four of you, quiet now, as if it is waiting too. Vera has not let go of her goggles all night.

Your name is Patch. Two hundred and forty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, Wes, who used to live on a roof, and Vera, who drove the last bus out of your yard with the whole horde behind her. Your yard is empty. Your gate is your own. And at the end of the motorway, a few thousand of the dead are standing on somebody else's bridge, because you put them there.

At sunset, the radio crackles. Vera picks it up before anybody else can.

"This is the driver," she says.

A pause. Then the same careful, tired voice.

"Thank you for coming to the radio. My name is Ansel. Before you say anything, nobody here is angry." Another pause. "Well. Some of us were. For about a week."

Vera says nothing. Her thumb moves over the button.

"That bridge is the only road from our streets to our fields, and to the spring where we get our water," says Ansel. "For a month, we've been walking under a few thousand of them to fetch it. Very quietly. We'd like to stop being quiet."

"So would everybody," says Vera.

"We watched you move them," he says. "We have diesel. And nobody here can drive anything bigger than a wheelbarrow."

From day 242 to day 244, the depot talks about it.

Not in whispers. You don't whisper anymore. But it is the kind of talking that goes around in circles, round and round the bench, the way the river used to go around the yard.

"We could say no," says Wes, on day 242. "We only just got the yard."

Nobody answers him. Not even Wes. He goes up and oils the gate, although he oiled it yesterday, and comes back down and sits on the bench, and looks at the jump leads hanging on their hook.

"I'm just saying it out loud," he says, "so it's been said."

On day 243, Nora stands in front of the chart on the bunker wall for a long time. Days. Zombies gone. People met. Days in daylight. She puts her finger on the third column.

"We know what it's like," she says. "Having them on your ceiling."

On day 244, Vera asks you the question nobody has asked yet.

"What's the rule?"

"If they come toward the gate, you close it," you say. "Even if somebody is outside."

"And if you're the one who sent them?"

You have kept that rule for two hundred and forty days. It has never said anything about that.

"Then we go and get them back," you say.

Vera turns her goggles over once in her hands. Then she pulls them on.

On day 245, the depot gets a new keeper.

At midday, three people walk down the hill road with blue bandages. Two grown-ups and the boy who learned the horn code. They are going to keep the depot, because this time, Wes is coming.

"Last time, I got the gate," he says. "This time, I want the motorway. I've never seen one with things on it that weren't dead."

You show the boy the rope on the bolt, the canopy ladder and the gantry. He listens very seriously. Then Nora gives him the flashlight for the gate and shows him the knock. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

"If somebody knocks any other way," she says, "you don't open."

He nods as if he will remember it for the rest of his life.

You drive out at dusk, four of you in a cab built for three, with the orange bag between your feet, and the sign over the street swinging in the wind behind you. FOUR ALIVE. For the first time, all four of them are on the wrong side of it.

On day 246, you drive back to the coach.

It sits exactly where Vera left it, alone in its lanes, beige and patient, with frost on the windows. Vera walks around it once and puts her hand on the side, the way the hill people patted the truck.

Across the frozen field beside the motorway, three people are walking toward you, pulling a handcart. A woman, a girl, and a tall, thin man in a long coat, with one yellow mitten and one green one.

He stops on the other side of the barrier, looks at the four of you, and then looks only at Vera.

"Ansel," he says.

"I know," says Vera. "You said."

The handcart is full of fuel cans. Ansel lifts the first one over the barrier himself, and puts it down at Vera's feet like an apology, although it isn't his to make.

"There was a light," Vera says. "On the edge of the city. One window."

Ansel nods. "That was us. We saw you up on the roof of that bus, the first morning. We couldn't get to you. Not with all of them in the way. So we kept a lamp in the window every night, so you'd know somebody had seen you."

Vera looks at him for a long time.

"I thought you were very tidy dead," she says.

"Some days," says Ansel, "we were close."

From day 247 to day 249, the coach comes back to life.

The diesel goes into the tank, can after can, with the girl holding the funnel. Wes climbs into the driver's seat and tries the key. The dashboard lights up, faintly. The starter turns once, slowly, like somebody waking up on a Monday. Then nothing.

"It's been standing in the frost for two months," says Vera. "It's sulking."

So you drive the white truck around to face the coach, nose to nose, and Wes takes out the jump leads. The same jump leads he has been looking at since day 206. Red to red. Black to black. He checks it three times, with hand movements.

On day 248, Vera turns the key.

The coach coughs. Coughs again. Catches. A deep diesel rumble rolls out over the motorway, and Vera sits back in the driver's seat with both hands on the big wheel, and closes her eyes, as if somebody has just played her favorite song.

On day 249, you sit on the barrier with Ansel and his people, eating the bread they brought, and you ask him the only question that matters.

"Where do we take them?"

Not away. Away is just somebody else's door. You have learned that.

Ansel thinks about it. Then he picks up a stick and draws in the frost on the road.

"There's an old gravel pit," he says, "east of our fields. Steep walls on every side. One road down into it, and it is the same road out."

From day 250 to day 252, you go and look at it.

You and Ansel take the truck, the long way round, along farm tracks, with the fields white on both sides. The pit is six kilometers past the junction. You hear it before you see it, because there is nothing to hear. Just wind, going around and around inside a hole in the ground.

It is huge. A great grey bowl dug into the earth, with walls as steep as a staircase stood on its end. At the bottom, a frozen puddle the size of a car park. And one road, curving down along the inside of the wall, all the way to the bottom.

You stand on the rim for a long time. You know what the dead do. They walk toward a sound. They stop where it stops. And they do not climb.

On day 251, you bring Nora and Vera to see it.

Nora looks down into the pit, then along the single road curving down into it.

"A place with one way out," she says.

"My favorite kind," says Vera.

On day 252, you count everything twice. The coach's tank. The truck's tank. The rope. The jump leads. The tape, which Wes keeps in his pocket now, in case anything else needs a promise kept.

On day 253, you take the motorway apart.

Not all of it. Just four sections of the long metal barrier down the middle, the bent ones, the ones that leaned toward you on day 215 with a few thousand people pressing against them. Ansel's people bring spanners. The bolts are rusted and stubborn and squeal like gulls. By dark, four sections of barrier lie in the back of the white truck.

A waist-high barrier is as good as a wall to them. You found that out the hard way. Now you are going to use it the easy way.

That night, everybody takes their place.

Vera will drive the coach. Wes will ride with her, with the tape. You will watch them come off the bridge, then drive the truck with the barrier the long way round, and wait out of sight near the top of the road into the pit. Nora will be on the rim, with the flashlight, and the rope. Ansel's people will be on the rim beside her.

"The rule," says Nora, on the motorway, in the dark.

"If they come toward the gate, you close it," you say.

"There isn't a gate," says Nora.

"Then we'll have to build one," says Vera.

On day 254, at first light, Vera drives the coach up to the junction.

From where you wait with the truck, far down a farm track, you can only see the top of the bridge. It is still black from one railing to the other. A month of standing has not moved one of them an inch. They are facing nowhere, waiting for a horn.

Then the horn comes.

Not short. Long. The one that empties a flyover. It rolls up the slip road and over the bridge, and the whole black line on top of it turns at once, like a field of wheat in the wind.

They come down off the bridge. Slowly. Then all of them. They pour down the slip road toward the coach like sand out of a bag, and Vera lets them get close, closer than you would ever dare, until their hands are almost on the back windows. Then she pulls away at the speed of a very slow queue, and you turn the truck around and take the long way to the pit, fast, with four sections of barrier rattling in the back.

It takes all day.

The coach crawls along the farm road with the river walking behind it, six kilometers of the dead strung out between frozen fields. Every few minutes, the horn. Every few minutes, a few thousand heads lift toward it. On the rim of the pit, Nora watches them come over the fields, and flashes the light once. Still coming. All of them.

Late in the afternoon, the coach reaches the pit, and starts down the road into it.

Down along the wall. Around the long curve. Halfway down, on a patch of ice, the back of the coach slides toward the edge, and from the rim you see Vera's arms turn the big wheel, slowly, with two fingers, like a teacup. The wheels grip. Nora breathes out for the first time in a minute. Then on, all the way to the bottom, where it stops beside the frozen puddle. Behind it, the river comes over the edge and starts down the road, a dark ribbon winding into the bowl.

Inside the coach, Wes leans over the wheel and tapes the horn down. Neatly. Very neatly. The horn goes on and on, filling the whole pit like water filling a sink.

Through the back window, the road behind them is already full of faces, coming down. Not angry. Not hungry. Just listening, the way they always listen.

Then the roof hatch opens.

Vera climbs out first, and Wes after her. Second door. From the rim, Nora throws down the rope. It unrolls down the steep wall and lands beside the coach, and Vera ties it under her arms without hurrying once.

"Pull," says Nora.

You pull. Ansel pulls. Everybody pulls. Vera comes up the wall hand over hand, and then Wes, as the first of the dead reach the bottom of the road and flow out across the floor of the pit, around the coach, toward the sound.

They keep coming until dusk. Thousands of them, down one road, into one bowl, the way they came into your yard on day one. And the last two down the road, walking slowly, side by side, wear a blue depot vest and an olive one.

The night driver. And the Doorman, one step behind him, the way he has been since day 190.

Nora stands at the very edge of the rim. She doesn't say anything this time. She doesn't need to.

"It was a good bus," Vera tells him, quietly, from up there. "I told you."

Then you drive the truck to the top of the road.

Everybody lifts. Ansel's people, the girl, Wes, Vera, you. Four sections of motorway barrier go across the top of the road, bolted end to end, waist-high, from wall to wall.

It is the first gate you have ever closed with everyone on the right side of it.

That night, the horn stops. The battery is done. Down in the pit, the river stands exactly where the last sound was, in the dark, facing a bus, waiting for a horn that is never going to blast again.

From day 255 to day 258, the bridge belongs to the living.

On day 255, Ansel's people walk across it in broad daylight, with buckets, toward the spring. Nobody whispers. One of them starts to sing, very badly. Nobody tells him to stop.

You stand on the bridge with Nora, at the top of the slip road, where a month ago you leaned on a horn and rolled away in silence. There is nothing on it now but frost, and footprints, and a girl pulling an empty handcart, running.

On day 256, Ansel shows you the city. Just the edge of it. Empty streets. Shut windows. And a tall, narrow house on a corner, with a lamp in the top window, unlit now, because nobody needs to be told anymore that somebody has seen them.

There are nineteen of them. They live upstairs, the way you lived downstairs.

"You could stay," Ansel tells Vera. "We could use a driver."

Vera looks out at the motorway, toward the town, toward the hill and a depot you can't see from here.

"I've got a place with one way out," she says. "But thank you."

On day 258, before you leave, Ansel gives Nora a road map, soft and folded a hundred times. On it, in pencil, there is a circle around a place at the edge of the paper. Blue sea. Two days' drive, when the roads are clear.

Wes looks over her shoulder.

"That's the beach," he says.

From day 259 to day 262, you drive home with full tanks.

On day 262, you stop at your own gate, and before anybody in the cab can move, there is a knock from the inside. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

The bolt lifts. The gate whispers open. And there is the boy with the blue bandage, holding the flashlight like a promise, very proud of himself.

That evening, Wes takes the pencil and adds nineteen marks to the people met column. One at a time. Very slowly. Enjoying every single one.

From day 263 to day 269, the depot rests.

Every sunset, Ansel comes on the radio. "Pit's quiet. Bridge is ours." Every sunset, Nora says, "Still here." Wes oils the gate. Vera sleeps sitting up, with the door open. Nora pins the road map inside the open locker, next to the postcard of the beach. The loudspeaker switch is still taped on OFF.

Most mornings, the truck still goes out, one short blast at every corner, but there is less and less to lead. Some mornings there is nothing at all, and you just drive around the lower town with the window down, because you can.

On day 266, Vera sits down next to you on the bench and puts her goggles in your hands.

"You drove that slip road with two fingers," she says. "Keep them. I know I'm still here now. I don't need anything to remind me."

You don't know what to say. So you put them in the orange bag, very carefully, next to everything else you are keeping. Wes says that bag is going to need its own bunker soon. Nora says it already has one.

Nora adds a mark to days in daylight every evening. By day 269, the column is longer than any column on the wall except the days.

On day 270, the night is clear and quiet. The four of you go down to the bunker, the way you always do, and Nora turns the lantern down low.

Then, through the vent grille above your heads, you hear footsteps.

Not shuffling. Not dragging. Boots. Living ones. Several of them, crossing the street toward the depot, and stopping at the gate. A thin line of torchlight slides across the yard, finds the grille, and stops there, as if somebody knows exactly where to look.

You hold your breath. The others hold theirs.

And somebody knocks.

Twice. Hard. Then nothing.

Nobody you know knocks like that.

Two hundred and seventy days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 271. The river is in a pit with a gate across it, the bridge belongs to the living, and the sign over the street says FOUR ALIVE. For two hundred and seventy days, you have known exactly how the dead come to your door. Tonight, for the first time, you don't know who the living are.
