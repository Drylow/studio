On day 331, nobody talks about anything else.

The sun comes up over the hill, and the long room is already full of people pretending they slept. Ruth sits by the window with the radio in her lap, the way she sat all night. Kit is asleep against her shoulder, still wearing his white helmet. The radio says nothing at all.

Your name is Patch. Three hundred and thirty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot, and then above it, on a hill, looking down at a yard so full of the dead that you can't see the ground. You put them there yourself, two weeks ago, so nineteen people in the city could have their bridge back. And last night, a man called Tam spoke out of the radio from a rock in the sea, and said the gate at the end of his road is leaning.

"Five weeks," says Ruth. "We walked here in nineteen days. We can walk back."

"And arrive with two weeks to spare," says Wes, "and three pairs of feet, and no way to carry anything."

"Then what?"

Vera is standing at the big window, looking down at the depot. At the yard, the canopy, the sign. At the dark rectangle in the middle of all those heads, where something big and beige used to stand.

She takes the travel alarm clock out of her pocket and listens to it tick.

"Then we drive," she says. "In a bus."

Everybody looks at you. They always do, at this point. And you realize that the question isn't really about buses at all. It's the question Ruth asked on day 285, by the fire, when you said "When the roads are clear," and Nora smiled at the first half of a yes.

You look down at the yard. There's no bunker to go back to now. No bench. No lantern. Only a sign over a street, and a gate with the dead behind it.

"Then we'd better go and get it," you say.

From day 332 to day 334, you go back to the pit.

Four of you. You, Vera, Wes and Bram, in the white truck, the long way round, along the farm tracks, between fields that have finally started to turn green.

You hear it before you see it. Nothing. Just wind going around inside a hole in the ground.

The gravel pit looks different now. One whole side of it is gone, slumped into a long brown slope, cracked and dry in the sun, from the bottom to the rim. The other side is the way you left it. Steep. Grey. The single road curving down along the wall. And across the top of the road, four sections of motorway barrier, still bolted end to end, still holding a gate across a place that hasn't needed one since the rain.

At the bottom, where the frozen puddle used to be, there is a wide crust of dried mud. And in the middle of it, still standing on its wheels, mud up to its hubcaps, is the coach.

Around it, like fence posts, stand the last of the dead. Forty, maybe. Sunk to the knee in the mud, facing the bus, the way they faced it on the night the horn died. They haven't moved in weeks. They don't look like they ever plan to.

"They never follow anything the first time," says Vera softly.

Wes looks at her. "Then we'll have to give them a second time."

On day 335, the clock does one more job.

The plan is Vera's. She winds the travel alarm clock all the way, sets it, and ties it into the end of the rope. Then she walks around the rim, to the far side of the pit, the steep side, opposite the coach.

You lower the clock down the wall, slowly, hand over hand, until it hangs just above the mud at the bottom.

And it rings.

A thin little sound, tinny and brave, rattling around the bottom of a gravel pit. Down by the coach, forty heads lift at once. Slowly, very slowly, the first one pulls one foot out of the mud. Then the other. And then they are walking, all of them, sucking and squelching across the floor of the pit, away from the coach, toward the sound.

It takes them the whole afternoon to cross. When the bell runs down, they're standing at the bottom of the far wall, in a little crowd, looking up at a clock they can't reach. They won't climb. You know that. And there is nothing on that side to walk up.

Vera ties off the rope and leaves the clock hanging there.

"Twice," she says. "It's done its job twice now. Most people don't get that."

On day 336, you unbolt the gate.

Bram and you, with Ansel's spanners, at the top of the road. The bolts squeal like gulls again. One by one, the four sections of barrier come up, and the road down into the pit is open.

Bram looks at the barrier lying in the grass. Then he picks up the first section and puts it on the back of the truck. Then the second.

"We're not going to need a gate," says Wes.

Bram picks up the third section, and looks at him, and whistles two notes. Then a third.

"Maybe," Wes translates. "Fine. Maybe."

On day 337, the coach wakes up a second time.

You drive the truck down the long curving road, slowly, into the pit, with the forty dead watching you from the far wall. Nobody breathes much. They watch you the whole way down, but they don't come. They are waiting for their clock.

You park nose to nose with the coach, the way you did on the motorway. Bram opens the coach's engine flap and stares into it for a long time, without a word. There is dried mud in places mud should never be. He takes things out. He wipes them. He puts them back. It takes him until noon.

Then Wes clips on the jump leads. Red to red. Black to black. Three times, with hand movements.

Vera climbs into the driver's seat. She puts both hands on the big wheel, and turns the key.

The starter turns. Slowly. Once. Twice. Then nothing.

At the far wall, forty heads turn toward the sound.

"Again," says Bram. It is the first word you have ever heard him say.

Vera turns the key again. The coach coughs. Coughs. Coughs again, a great rattling cough, the kind of cough that makes everybody in a waiting room look up. And then it catches. A deep diesel rumble fills the pit, and the forty start across the mud toward you, slowly, one foot after the other.

"Go," says Wes.

The wheels spin in the mud. Spin. Then grip. The coach climbs out of its own tracks, the truck reversing ahead of it, up the long curving road, past the place where it slid on the ice, all the way to the top. Behind you, the forty reach the bottom of the road, and stop, because the engines are already too far away to follow.

At the rim, Vera switches off and sits there with her hands on the wheel.

"Good bus," she says.

From day 338 to day 342, the city fills the tank.

You drive the coach slowly along the farm tracks, with the truck behind, and across the bridge at the junction, which is empty except for frost-cracked tarmac and one girl with a handcart, who drops it and runs ahead to tell everybody.

All nineteen of them come out of the tall, narrow house. Ansel shakes your hand with the yellow mitten and then the green one, as if one hand is not enough.

"Sea," he says. "I heard."

They fill the tank with the last of their diesel. Every can. The girl holds the funnel. When you try to say they should keep some, Ansel just shakes his head.

"Everything we have, we have because of a bus," he says. "Feels right to give it back to one."

On day 341, he asks the four of you up to the top floor, and takes the lamp out of the window. The lamp that burned every night for Vera, back when she was on the roof of this same coach, alone on a motorway.

He gives it to her.

"So somebody knows," he says, "that somebody has seen you."

Vera holds it for a long time. She doesn't say thank you. She doesn't need to. Ansel can see her face.

"Come with us," says Nora, on the radio, that night. "All of you. There's room on the bus."

"Somebody has to keep the bridge," says Ansel. "And somebody has to answer when you call."

From day 343 to day 347, the coach gets ready.

It sits on the hill road below the long room, beige and patient, with the sun drying the last of the mud off its sides. The depot's name is still painted down one of them. Bram lives under it, mostly. Wes lives in the driver's seat, mostly, pretending to drive, until Vera tells him to get out.

The four sections of barrier go up on the roof, strapped down tight, in case anything needs a gate.

Wes's list grows on the wall of the long room. Things we'd take. The orange bag. The turquoise bag. The flashlight. The clock, which is still hanging in the gravel pit, so Vera crosses it out. The lamp. The cardboard with the totals on it. Jump leads. Tape. The radio. Bram's oil can. Kit adds "my helmet," although he has never once taken it off. Nora adds "the sign."

"We can't take the sign," says Wes.

"I know," says Nora. "That's why I'm going to change it."

That evening, you find her sitting on the coach steps, with the postcard in her hands. Blue sea. Yellow sand. The tiny white lighthouse in the corner.

"You said when the roads are clear," she says, without looking up.

"I know."

"They're not clear."

"I know."

She turns the postcard over. On the back, there is nothing written at all. She bought it, and never sent it, and never went.

"I'm going anyway," she says. "I wanted you to hear me say it."

You sit down next to her on the step.

"I'm driving the truck," you say. "Somebody has to stay behind the bus."

On day 348, Nora paints the sign.

She does it at dawn, when the dead are slowest. Bram carries the ladder down the hill and props it against the wall under the sign, out in the street, outside the gate. On the other side of that gate, the yard is packed wall to wall. Thousands of them, quiet, facing the canopy, waiting for one more announcement.

Nora climbs the ladder with the little pot of white paint and the brush. You hold the bottom. Wes holds his breath.

Every rung creaks. Every creak turns a few heads in the yard. Not many. Just enough.

At the top, Nora stops. And for a long moment, she doesn't paint anything at all. She just looks at the sign. At TWO ALIVE, crossed out. THREE ALIVE, crossed out. FOUR ALIVE, in her own white paint. And the frequency, the one that carried her voice all the way to the sea.

Then she crosses out FOUR. And underneath, in big white letters, she paints: SEVEN ALIVE. GONE TO THE SEA.

When she comes down, there is somebody standing just inside the gate.

The Doorman. Both hands flat on the bars, from the inside, for the first time in his life. Looking up at the sound of the ladder, the way he has looked at that gate since day 67.

Nora stands in front of him for a moment, with the bars between them. She doesn't flinch. She doesn't step back.

"Look after it," she tells him quietly.

Then she picks up the paint pot, and walks back up the hill without looking back.

From day 349 to day 354, the radio goes quiet.

Every night, Ruth calls the Point. Every night, nothing.

Then, on day 351, very late, a voice in the hiss.

"Ruth. It held. Last night it held. Barely." A crackle. "There are more of them. They come down the beach at every low tide, and they don't all go back when the sea comes in. Some of them just stand in the water on the road and wait."

"We're coming," says Ruth. "We've got a bus."

A pause, full of static.

"Course you have," says Tam, and for a second, he sounds like he's laughing. Then he's gone.

After that, nothing. Not on day 352. Not on day 353. Kit stops sleeping. On day 354, Nora sits down beside him at the radio, and says Still here into it, on the Point's frequency, slowly, so it will arrive safely.

Nobody answers. But Kit falls asleep against her shoulder.

From day 355 to day 357, you say goodbye.

The hill people give you potatoes, apples, blankets, and the boy with the blue bandage. Only for the morning. He spends it on the coach steps with Kit, and when it's time to go, the two of them do their secret knock on the coach door, the one nobody else is allowed to know, and then the hill boy takes off his blue bandage and gives it to Kit, who ties it around the strap of his white helmet.

Ansel comes on the radio every sunset. "Bridge is ours. Come home if it's bad." And every sunset, the answer.

On day 357, you stand at the big window with Nora for the last time, looking down at the depot. The yard, full. The canopy. The sign. SEVEN ALIVE. GONE TO THE SEA. And the frequency, so anyone who reads it knows where to call.

"It's a door you can't close," you say. "A sign."

"I know," says Nora. "That's why I didn't paint the address."

On day 358, the coach leaves the hill.

At first light. Vera drives the coach, with Wes beside her, Ruth and Kit behind, and Bram at the back with the jump leads across his knees. You drive the truck behind them, with Nora beside you, the turquoise bag on her knees and the orange bag between your feet.

You go down the hill road slowly, past the depot. Nobody uses a horn. Nobody speaks. In the yard, a few heads turn toward the engines, and then turn back.

At the gate, the Doorman stands with his hands on the bars, from the inside, and watches you go.

Nora flashes the flashlight at the hill, where the hill people stand in a row. Once. Twice. Still here.

And then the depot is behind you, getting smaller in the mirror, until it's just a roof, and a canopy, and a sign you can't read anymore.

You have lived in that place for three hundred and fifty-eight days. You don't look back. Nora does it for both of you.

On day 359, you follow the pencil line.

Ruth's thin line from the edge of the city to the circle by the sea. Back roads. Empty villages. A sky that gets bigger and paler as you drive, until the trees start to lean all the same way, as if somebody is always blowing on them.

"Wind off the sea," says Ruth, on the radio from the coach. "We're close."

You see a few of the dead in the fields. Small groups, never more than a dozen, standing in hedges, turning slowly to watch the coach go past. Vera never slows down. Nobody follows for long.

In one village, the road runs between two rows of little houses with their curtains still drawn, and a single figure stands in the middle of the street, in a postman's cap, holding nothing. He turns to watch the coach come. Vera goes round him, two wheels on the pavement, without touching him, the way you would walk around somebody in a corridor. In the mirror, you watch him turn, very slowly, to watch you go.

That night, you stop on a hilltop, the coach and the truck side by side, the engines off. You sleep in the cab. Vera sleeps in the driver's seat, lying back, with Ansel's lamp unlit on the dashboard.

And, at midnight, Kit hears the radio.

On day 360, the bridge is broken.

You reach it at noon. The bridge Ruth wasn't sure about. A long concrete road over a wide brown river, where the land flattens out and the air starts to taste of salt.

It's still standing. But halfway across, a piece of it has fallen away into the water, a long jagged gap down one side, wider than a car. What's left of the road next to it is barely wider than the coach.

Vera gets out and looks at it, with her hands in the ends of her scarf.

"Is there another way?" you ask.

"Two days round," says Ruth. Her voice is very flat. "Through the town upriver."

"We don't have two days," says Kit.

Nobody answers. Because at midnight, Kit heard the radio, and woke everybody up, and you all sat in the dark and listened to Tam's voice coming in and out of the hiss, saying the same thing again and again, as if he wasn't sure anybody was there.

"The gate's gone. It came down at low tide. We're all on the rock, at the lighthouse. Everybody's safe, for now. But in five days, the spring tide. The road will be dry for half a day. And there's nothing between them and us anymore."

You stand at the edge of the broken bridge, and look across the river, toward a line of pale light where the land ends.

The sea.

Three hundred and sixty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 361. The river is locked in your old yard, the coach is running, and the sign over the street says SEVEN ALIVE. GONE TO THE SEA. The sea is one day away. The only road to it has a hole in the middle. And in five days, the lowest tide of the year will open a door that nobody is holding anymore.
