On day 181, the loudspeaker gets one more job.

It has called passengers for Platform 1 for months. It called the river into your yard. It called the river's tail down from the hill. Now, for the first time, Nora wants it to say what it was built to say, and have somebody actually listen.

Your name is Patch. A hundred and eighty days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, Wes, who used to live on a roof, and since the winter began, a woman from a truck who still won't tell you her name. The yard is full of the dead. So is the street. In the middle of the yard stands a coach with a full battery, its keys hanging on a nail in your bunker, and eleven passengers standing between you and the driver's seat.

On day one, that coach called the whole street into this yard with its alarm. It seems only fair that it should take them all out again.

"We open the door," Nora says. "We turn on the speaker. And we ask them to get off."

"Will they?" asks Wes.

"They always did," says Nora. "Eventually."

The woman looks at the nearly empty racks. "Eventually is about four days from now."

Days 182 to 184 are spent sharing out the sunlight.

All winter, the solar panel has been feeding the coach. The loudspeaker has gone hungry. On day 182, Wes unclips the jump leads from the control box and opens the lid. The little battery light glows. Barely. A thin sliver of green.

"How long will it shout?" you ask.

"One announcement," says Wes. "Maybe two. Then it's a very expensive box."

So nothing can go wrong with the first one.

Wes says he should be the one on the coach roof, because he made the hook. The woman says she should, because she is going to drive the thing. Nora says it will be you, because you have done it twice and come back both times. Nobody argues with Nora about the coach.

The folding door of the coach has an outside lever, low on the front corner, by the steps. You used it on day 55 to lock eleven passengers in. Now you need to let them out. It is right where the crowd is thickest. But the hole in the canopy roof is right above the front of the coach. And Wes still has the polisher handle.

On day 183, you eat the last tin. On day 184, you eat the crackers the woman was hiding for an emergency. She says this counts.

That night, the stars come out sharp and close again. Nora looks up at them from the gantry. You know what that means now.

Day 185 is white from the gate to the canopy.

You go down through the hole onto the coach roof with the rope around your waist. The dead stand frozen around you like a garden of statues, frost on their shoulders, heads turning so slowly that you can't see them move, only that they have moved.

You crawl to the front corner of the roof and lie flat. Below you, the folding door. Below that, the lever. Between you and the lever, a few frozen faces, tilting up. One frozen hand begins to rise toward the polisher handle, as slowly as a plant growing toward a window.

You lower the polisher handle past them. The wire hook touches the lever. Slips off. Touches again. Catches. You pull up, slowly, the way you would lift a sleeping cat. The lever moves. With a hiss and a clunk, the folding door of the coach opens for the first time in a hundred and thirty days.

Up on the gantry, Nora flashes the light once. Wes flips the switch.

"Passengers for Platform 1."

Inside the coach, eleven heads turn toward the sound. Ten passengers start down the aisle. One step at a time. Down the steps. Out of the door. Into the frozen crowd, toward the voice under the canopy, like people who have finally heard their bus being called. Very orderly. Better behaved than most passengers you have ever traveled with.

"Mind the step," Wes whispers from the hole, to nobody.

"Passengers for Plat—"

The speaker dies in the middle of the word. It doesn't matter. Ten are off.

But the eleventh is still standing at the top of the aisle, behind the driver's seat, as he always has. The night driver never got off first. He waited until everybody else was gone.

Nora crawls across the canopy roof to the front edge, right above the open door. She doesn't shout. She just leans out over the edge and says, very softly, the thing that he used to say to her every night for nine years.

"Last stop. All yours."

The man in the blue depot vest turns toward her voice. Down the aisle. Down the steps. Out of the coach that he drove every night, into the crowd, his face turned up toward her, until the crowd closes around him.

Nora stays on the edge of the canopy roof for a long time after that. You don't call her back in. Nobody does.

On day 186, the woman goes down through the hatch.

She lands in the empty aisle, steps over the place where the night driver always stood, and sits down in the driver's seat. She closes the folding door with a button. Hiss. Clunk. Then she sits very still with both hands on the big wheel.

"Well?" says Wes, through the hatch.

"It smells of old jackets," she says. "It's perfect."

She checks everything, the way some people check a sleeping baby. The lights on the dashboard. The mirrors. The fuel gauge. A quarter of a tank.

"Is that enough?" you ask.

"Enough to get them out of town," she says. "Not enough to get them far away. We'll see."

Then she walks to the back of the empty coach, kneels down and reaches under the last seat. When she climbs back up through the hatch, she is holding a small wind-up travel alarm clock with two bells on top.

"Somebody left this," she says.

"That was me," you say. "Day 55."

She looks at it, winds it once, and tucks it into the folds of her scarf. "Every bus needs a clock."

On day 187, she climbs back up and tells you the plan for the hundredth time. You open the gate. She drives out, slowly, with the horn going. The yard follows the coach. The street follows the yard. Then she leads them over the flyover and keeps going until there is a long downhill road, and she gives them a last sound somewhere she isn't.

On day 188, the hill's radio says they are ready. As soon as the street is empty, they will walk down the hill with sacks of food.

Nobody in the bunker has eaten since yesterday. Wes says he can no longer remember what food is for. The woman says that is the first sensible thing he has said all winter. You find that the plan sounds even better on an empty stomach.

On the night of day 189, everyone takes their place.

The gate bolt has a short loop of rope tied to its handle, run up to the top of the canopy ladder, where you will be. When the coach comes, you pull the bolt and let the crowd do the pushing. Nora will be at the far end of the gantry with the flashlight. One flash means close it. Wes will lie at the hole in the canopy roof, where the woman can see him through the windshield.

And the woman will sleep in the coach.

"It's got two doors," she says, climbing down through the hatch with a blanket under her arm. "The front one and the roof. I haven't slept anywhere with two doors since day four."

You hear her through the hatch, a long time after the lantern is out, humming.

On the bench, Nora turns the lantern down low.

"The rule," she says.

"If they come toward the gate, you close it."

"Even if she's outside," says Nora.

You both know that this time, she will be.

At dawn on day 190, the woman turns the key.

The engine coughs. Coughs again. You think of the truck on day 141, grinding three streets away, and every walker in the street turning toward it.

Then the engine catches. Through the windshield, you see the woman pull her goggles down over her eyes. Up at the hole, Wes gives her a thumbs up with his mitten. A deep diesel rumble rolls out under the canopy and fills the whole yard.

Every head in the depot turns toward the coach.

She leans on the horn. Not a short blast. A long one, the one that empties a flyover.

The coach starts to move.

It moves at the speed of a very slow queue. The dead in front of it don't fall. They turn to face it and walk backward, their faces against the windshield, their hands on the glass, as if the coach is something they have been waiting for all their lives. The crowd around it presses in, walks with it, follows it. The whole yard begins to turn, like water around a plughole.

From the top of the ladder, you watch the crowd at the gate lean the other way. Toward the horn. Away from the gate. A gap opens along the inside of the wall.

You pull the rope.

The bolt lifts out of its hole. And the gate, oiled all summer by Wes, pushed all winter by the street, slides open with a polite little whisper. A hand's width. Then a body's width. Then wide open.

The street pours into the gateway, toward the horn, and meets the yard coming the other way. For one long minute, nothing moves at all. You hang on to the top of the ladder with the rope in your fist, and a few hundred faces press past the foot of it, close enough to touch your shoes, none of them looking up. Then the coach noses into the gateway, slow and enormous, and the crowd in front of it gives way into the street, turns, and starts to walk.

The coach rolls out of the gate. It turns downhill, toward the lower town, toward the flyover. Behind it, the yard follows. Behind the yard, the street. Above them all, the sign over the street swings in the wind of a few thousand bodies passing underneath it.

It takes all day.

They pour out of the yard like sand out of a bag. The ones from under the canopy. The ones from around the bay door. The ones who have been standing against your ceiling since the end of summer. Hour after hour, they walk out of the gate and down the street, after the sound of a horn that is getting farther away. The bay door stops creaking. The canopy stops echoing. Bit by bit, the depot remembers what it sounds like when it is empty.

Late in the afternoon, the yard is almost empty. A few stragglers. Then two. Then one.

The last one out of the yard wears a blue depot vest with two pockets.

He walks slowly through the gateway, following his bus. And in the street, standing beside the gate with his hands at his sides, there is a walker in an olive vest, who waits for him to pass, like a doorman, and then follows him down the hill.

Up on the gantry, a small light flashes once.

You climb down the ladder into an empty yard, slide the gate shut and drop the bolt. It is the quietest sound you have ever made.

On day 191, Nora walks into the yard.

Not to the coach. There is no coach. Not to the gate. Just into the middle of the concrete, where the sun is.

She sits down on the ground, in the daylight, with her back against a canopy pillar, and closes her eyes.

You sit down a little way off. You don't talk. Nothing turns toward you. Nobody watches. There is a dark rectangle on the concrete where the coach stood for a hundred and ninety days, and the only sound in the yard is the wind.

After a long time, without opening her eyes, Nora says, "It's warmer than I remembered."

At sunset, the woman's voice comes on the radio, faint and crackling.

"Over the flyover. Still coming. All of them."

In the morning, Wes climbs down the ladder, walks once around the whole empty yard, touches the gate, touches every canopy pillar, and then runs in a big slow circle with his arms out, laughing without a sound, like a dog that has finally been let off the lead.

On day 192, the same. On day 193, she says the tail was still climbing the ramp when she reached the top. She couldn't stop. She is following the motorway toward the city, looking for a long downhill road.

"I'm taking them back where they came from," she says. "Return to sender."

On day 194, there are footsteps in the street. Living ones.

From the gantry, you watch a little line of people walk down the hill road, through the split and into your street, carrying sacks on their shoulders. Every one of them has a blue bandage somewhere.

They stop at the gate. One of them knocks. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer. Somebody has been talking on the radio.

Nora looks at you. You look at Nora.

Then you open the gate, all the way, for the living.

That night, in the middle of the yard, you build a fire out of an old wooden pallet, and the hill people bake potatoes in it. It is the first hot food you have eaten since you were waiting for a bus. Nobody whispers. The fire crackles, and nothing comes to see why.

On days 195 and 196, the hill people sleep in the corridor and the store, and walk around the depot as if it is a museum. Wes shows them the bay door latch. Twice. Then he takes the pencil and adds a whole row of marks to the people met column, one at a time, very slowly, enjoying every single one.

On day 197, the woman's voice is fainter. The motorway is long and flat. No downhill yet.

Every sunset, Nora says, "Still here." Every sunset, the woman says it back. Each time, it is a little quieter, as if she is talking from the bottom of a well that keeps getting deeper.

On day 199, fainter still. "The fuel light's on," she says. "Don't tell anybody."

On day 201, you can barely hear her at all. Something about a bridge. Something about the city's first houses in the distance. Then a long hiss.

On day 203, nothing.

From day 204 to day 209, the radio says nothing at all.

You lie awake in the bunker, under a ceiling with no footsteps on it, and think about a woman sitting in a bus on a motorway, in the middle of the whole river, with a clock in her scarf and a quarter of nothing in her tank.

You leave them on at sunset anyway. Both of them. The depot's and the hill's, side by side on the rack, hissing at the same empty channel.

The hill people go back up the hill. The yard stays empty. The gate stays shut. Every night, Nora climbs to the end of the gantry and flashes the light toward the motorway. Twice. Once for still here. Once for her.

On day 206, Wes walks the three streets to where the truck died on day 141. It is still there, white with frost, with its cab door open. Its battery is dead. Its tank is half full. He comes back and doesn't say anything, but he looks at the jump leads for a long time.

Up on the gantry, the little battery light in the control box creeps back toward green, a sliver at a time. The loudspeaker is eating again.

On day 208, Nora opens the tin of white paint. She looks at the sign for a while. Then she closes it again.

"Not until she tells us," she says.

On day 210, at sunset, the radio crackles.

Everyone in the bunker stops breathing.

"Depot." It is the woman, so faint that it is mostly hiss. "The coach is out of fuel. I'm inside. They're all around it. All of them."

Nora grabs the radio. "Where are you?"

"Motorway. Past the bridge. Before the city." A long crackle. "The battery's almost gone. Nora. You asked me for my name."

"Yes."

"It's—"

The hiss swallows the rest. Then nothing.

"Say it again," Nora whispers into the radio. "Say it again."

The channel hisses back at her, empty. Wes picks up the flashlight and goes up to the gantry without a word, and you hear him on the metal steps, climbing toward the motorway side of the depot to flash it twice into the dark.

A hundred and ninety days in the dark, and twenty days in daylight. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the radio. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 211. Your yard is empty. Your gate is your own. And somewhere down the motorway, the whole river is standing around one bus, with a woman inside who has finally found a place with one way out, and who just told you her name a second too late.
