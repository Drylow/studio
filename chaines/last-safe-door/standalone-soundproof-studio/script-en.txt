At 2:14 in the morning, you open the door of the studio, and the city is screaming.

Not a little. All of it. Car alarms, sirens, glass, a hundred voices on the street above, all at once, like somebody turned the volume of the whole world up to the top. And behind you, on the other side of two doors, a woman is still singing a love song into a microphone, because in there, she can't hear any of it.

Your name is Patch. You are the night engineer at Glasshouse Sound, a small recording studio in the basement of an old building downtown. The room you work in was built for one job: to keep sound out. Thick walls. A floor that floats on rubber. Two heavy doors with a little corridor between them, so that no noise from the street can ever reach the microphones.

In about ten minutes, you are going to find out that the things on the street hunt by sound.

And that you are standing in the only room in the city they can't hear.

Six hours earlier, it was a normal night. The cheapest booking of the week: ten at night to six in the morning. A singer called Nora, with a purple band on her right forearm and a turquoise bag full of lyrics, paying for eight hours with money she had saved for a year. Her first real demo. Three songs.

You fixed her headphones. You lowered the lights in the vocal booth. You put the little orange bandage back on your forehead, the one you always wear because you always hit your head on the low pipe by the coffee machine. And you pressed the big red button that lights the sign over the door: RECORDING.

For four hours, nothing happened. Take after take. She laughed when she got it wrong. She closed her eyes when she got it right.

At 2:10, your phone buzzed in your pocket. Then again. Then it didn't stop. You didn't look. In a session, you never look.

At 2:14, you went out for coffee.

Now you are standing in the stairwell, holding an empty cup. At the top of the stairs, the street door is open. Someone is coming down. Slowly. One step at a time. A man in a delivery jacket, with his head hanging to one side, and a face the color of old green paint. His eyes don't move. His mouth is open.

Behind him, on the street, people are running. And other people are following them. Not running. Walking. Very fast.

The man on the stairs stops.

Your cup has slipped out of your hand. It bounces down three steps. Plastic on concrete. A tiny sound.

The man's head turns toward it. All the way. And he comes down the stairs at you, faster than anything that walks like that should move.

You don't think. You turn, you pull the first studio door open, you get inside the little corridor, you pull it shut. Then the second door. You throw your whole weight against it until the handle clicks.

And then there is silence. Total silence. The kind you only get in a room like this.

Through the small glass window in the inner door, Nora is still singing, eyes closed. She opens them, sees your face, and stops.

You press the talkback button. Your voice comes out in her headphones.

"Don't make a sound," you say. "Something is outside."

She takes off the headphones. She comes out of the booth. You stand together in the control room, in front of the big mixing desk with its hundreds of little lights, staring at the inner door.

You can't hear anything. Not a knock. Not a step. Nothing.

That's the worst part. He could be right there, on the other side, with his face against the steel. And you would never know.

"What was it?" Nora whispers.

You don't know what to tell her. So you tell her the truth.

"I think it was a dead man."

Day 1 comes up gray, but you only know that because the clock on the desk says 7:00. There are no windows in Glasshouse Sound. You have not slept. Neither has she.

Your phones say the same thing, over and over, until the network goes down at 7:40. Stay inside. Lock your doors. Do not help the injured. They are attracted by noise.

Attracted by noise.

Nora reads it twice. Then she looks around the room. At the walls covered in thick gray foam. At the double doors. At the red sign that still says RECORDING.

"We're in a soundproof room," she says.

"Yeah."

"They can't hear us."

"No," you say. "And we can't hear them."

That's the problem you spend the whole first day on. You can't live behind a door you can't hear through. You need ears outside.

So you build some.

In the cupboard, there is an old microphone nobody uses anymore, and a long cable. Very slowly, very quietly, you open the inner door. The little corridor is empty. You put your ear against the outer door. Nothing. You open it one finger wide. The stairwell is dark and still. The delivery man is gone.

You tape the microphone to the wall at the bottom of the stairs, pointing up toward the street. You run the cable back under both doors, through the rubber seals, into the desk. Then you shut everything and sit down, and you and Nora each put on a pair of headphones, and you push up one slider.

And the world comes in.

Wind. A dog barking far away. A car alarm that has been crying for so long that its battery is dying, and every scream is a little weaker than the one before. And under it all, a sound you will learn to know better than your own name. A dry shuffling. Feet that drag. Dozens of them, up on the street. And now and then, a low sound in a throat that isn't breathing.

Nora's hand finds yours on the desk. Neither of you says anything for an hour.

On day 2, you count what you have.

The green room is the little kitchen where bands wait between takes. A fridge with the food a rock band ordered for a session that was cancelled: two crates of bananas, crackers, cheese, forty cans of energy drink and a cake with "Good luck Tuesday" written on it in blue icing. A water cooler with four full bottles, big ones, about nineteen liters each. A coffee machine. A box of tea. Nora's thermos.

"Seventy-six liters of water," Nora says. "Two of us. If we're careful, that's a month."

"And if we're not careful?"

"Then we're not careful for a lot less than a month."

The studio's little radio still works. One station, between the hiss. A calm man reading the same message every hour. The city is closed. The bridges are closed. Do not go outside. Rescue will be announced on this frequency.

You write the frequency on a strip of tape and stick it on the desk.

On day 3, you learn the first rule.

Through the stairwell microphone, at noon, you hear a phone ringing. Not yours. Somewhere up on the street, a phone in a pocket, or on the ground, playing a happy little song, over and over.

And the shuffling changes. All of it. You can hear it turn. Dozens of feet, all at once, all going the same way. Toward the song. The ringing stops. The shuffling stays there for a long time. Then it slowly spreads out again.

"They don't see very well," Nora says. "They follow noise. Whatever's loudest."

"So the quiet ones live."

"Then we're the quietest people in the city," she says, and for the first time since the night started, she almost smiles.

On day 4, the power goes out.

It happens at 9:12 in the evening. The desk goes black. The lights go black. The fridge stops humming. In the dark, the silence of the room is so thick you can hear your own heart.

Then a little green light comes on in the corner. Then a beep.

The studio has a backup battery for the desk, a big gray box under the rack, so a recording is never lost if the power fails. You know exactly how long it lasts, because you have read the label a hundred times on boring nights: about six hours, with the desk on.

Six hours of ears. That's all you have left.

You and Nora sit in the dark and decide. You turn off everything except one channel of the desk: the stairwell microphone. You only switch it on four times a day, ten minutes each. Morning. Noon. Evening. Night. The rest of the time, you live blind. And deaf.

That night, Nora lies on the old leather couch in the control room, and you lie on the floor under the desk, and neither of you can sleep, because every time you close your eyes, you imagine that face, right on the other side of the door.

"Patch?" she says, in the dark.

"Yeah."

"If they were out there. Right outside. Would we know?"

You don't answer. Because the answer is no.

On day 6, at the evening listening, you hear knocking.

Not on your door. Up in the building. Three knocks. A pause. Three knocks. Careful. Gentle. Like somebody who has been knocking on doors for days, and is trying not to be heard by anything else.

The dead don't knock in threes.

You take the flashlight from the drawer. Nora takes the fire extinguisher. You open the inner door, then the outer one, and you go up the stairs one step at a time, on the outside edges, where the old wood doesn't creak.

On the ground floor, behind the glass door of the guitar repair shop, there is an old man in a brown cap and round glasses, holding a tuning fork in each hand.

His name is Gus. He has fixed guitars in this building for thirty-one years. He has been hiding in his back room for six days, eating peanuts and drinking the water from the radiator. He has heard you. Not you: your silence. Every night, he said, the dead walked past the door of Glasshouse Sound and didn't stop. Not once. He wanted to know why.

You bring him down. You give him a banana and a piece of the Tuesday cake. He cries a little, quietly, the way old men do when they're surprised to still be alive.

Now there are three of you. And seventy-six liters of water is no longer a month.

On day 9, Nora does the math again, on the back of a lyric sheet.

"Twelve days," she says. "Maybe thirteen."

Across the street, there is a small corner grocery. You can see it on the security camera in the lobby, when the battery lets you look. Its door is broken. Its shelves are half full. And between you and it, in the middle of the road, there are eleven of them, standing still, swaying, the way they do when there is nothing to follow.

Eleven.

"They follow noise," Gus says, rubbing his glasses. "Whatever's loudest."

He goes up to his shop and comes back with a small amplifier, a guitar cable, and the old battery from his van. It takes him two hours, in the dark, with a tiny screwdriver. When he's done, the amplifier works, with no wall power at all.

The plan is simple. That's what makes it so frightening.

Gus puts the amplifier on the roof of the building next door, at the end of the alley, as far from the grocery as possible. He runs a long cable back to the stairwell. Nora holds the other end, with a little music player. You stand by the street door with three empty backpacks.

You nod.

Nora presses play.

At the end of the alley, a drummer from one of your old recordings starts hitting his drums as hard as he can. It's enormous. It bounces off every wall on the street.

And all eleven turn their heads at once.

They go. Shuffling, then faster, all of them, down the street, toward the alley, toward the drums.

You run. Across the road, into the grocery, past the broken door. You fill the bags without looking. Bottles. Cans. Rice. Batteries. A box of candles. Bandages. Peanut butter. You're shaking so hard that you drop a can, and it rolls across the floor, and you freeze.

But the drums are louder.

You're back in the stairwell in four minutes. Nora pulls the cable. The drums stop. And at the end of the alley, eleven dead people stand in a circle under the roof, staring up at a little amplifier that has gone quiet.

Three bags. Thirty more liters of water. And a lesson.

"They don't want us," Nora says, that night. "They want the sound. We just have to be quieter than something else."

On day 13, it rains.

It rains for two days without stopping. You can't hear it inside, of course. You only know because the stairwell microphone is full of it, a long gray hiss. And because, on the second morning, there is water under the outer door.

Not a lot. A thin dark line on the floor of the little corridor. But the rubber seal on the bottom of the outer door is old, and wet rubber swells, and swollen rubber doesn't close.

At the noon listening, you hear something new in the stairwell. Not shuffling. Closer. A slow scraping. Like fingernails on a wall. And then, very clearly, right next to the microphone, a long, low breath that doesn't breathe.

One of them is at the bottom of the stairs.

Nora takes off her headphones very slowly. Gus has stopped moving. None of you dares to look at the inner door.

Then Gus coughs.

He doesn't mean to. He's old, he's been cold for two weeks, and it comes out of him before he can stop it. Not loud. In a normal room, nobody would even notice.

But the outer door isn't closing all the way anymore.

In your headphones, the scraping stops. Then you hear the breath turn. Toward the door. Toward the gap at the bottom. And then, through the steel, faint, for the first time since day 1, you hear it with your own ears, without any microphone at all.

A hand. Flat against the outer door. Pushing.

You put your hand over Gus's mouth. Nora puts both hands over her own. For forty minutes, the three of you sit on the floor of the control room without moving anything but your eyes, while on the other side of two doors, something leans its weight against the steel, again and again, waiting for one more sound.

It doesn't get one.

At last, the breathing moves away, up the stairs, into the rain.

That night, you and Nora pull the foam off a whole wall of the vocal booth, the thick gray kind, and you stuff it into the corridor between the two doors, floor to ceiling. Gus tapes every edge. You have just made your door thicker than any door in the city.

"Good," Gus whispers, when it's done. "Now I'll cough into a pillow."

On day 17, the radio speaks.

Not the calm man. A woman, a soldier, reading a new message. Boats. Rescue boats, on the river, at the old pier on Harbor Street, at dawn on day 30. They will wait thirty minutes. Then they will go. And they won't come back to the city again.

The old pier is two kilometers away. You know because Gus draws it on the back of a lyric sheet, with every street, every crossing, every bridge. Two kilometers of open road, in a city full of things that hear everything.

"Two kilometers," Nora says. "We can't run two kilometers."

"We don't have to," Gus says. "We just have to be quieter than something else."

Then all three of you turn and look at the same thing.

Over the mixing desk, there is a shelf with two big speakers on it. The loudest things in the building. The ones you used to play finished songs to the bands, so loud that the floor shook.

On days 18 to 24, you build the loudest thing in the city.

It takes everything you have. The two big speakers. Gus's amplifier, and a second one he finds in the back of his shop. Three car batteries, which Gus takes out of the dead cars in the alley at night, with a wrench wrapped in a towel so it doesn't clink. Every meter of cable in the studio. And the music player, with a little kitchen timer taped to the front, so it can start by itself while nobody is near it.

You carry it up to the roof of Gus's building piece by piece, at night, on the outside edges of the stairs. It takes six trips. On the fourth, a dog barks two streets away, and the whole street below you turns toward it, and you lie flat on the roof with a speaker in your arms for twenty minutes, not breathing, while they shuffle past the building.

On day 25, there is only one question left.

What do you play?

It has to be long. Loud. It has to keep them coming for an hour, from all over the city, so that the road to the river is empty.

You go through every recording on the drives. Rock bands. Drums. A choir. Nothing feels right.

Then Nora says, very quietly, "Play mine."

You look at her.

"My songs," she says. "The demo. We have three songs. If you loop them, that's an hour." She looks down at her hands. "I saved for a year to come here and get them recorded. I wanted somebody to hear them." She almost laughs. "I didn't think it would be the whole city."

So for three days, with the last of the backup battery, you finish her demo. You mix it. You make it as loud as it can possibly go. Gus listens to the last take with his eyes closed, and says only, "Yes. That's the one."

On day 29, nobody sleeps.

You pack the bags. Water, a little food, the flashlight, the radio. Nora packs her lyric sheets in the turquoise bag. Gus puts on his brown cap and polishes his glasses about forty times. You stand in front of the inner door for a long time, with your hand on the handle.

For twenty-nine days, this door kept you alive. Two inches of steel, and rubber, and foam. The only safe door you've ever had.

"Ready?" Nora asks.

You aren't. You open it anyway.

Day 30. 5:40 in the morning.

The three of you stand in the stairwell in the dark, with your backs against the wall. In your hand is the little remote button Gus wired to the timer. In your ear is one headphone, connected to the stairwell microphone. Up on the street, you can hear them. More than ever. Hundreds of feet. Shuffling. Waiting.

You look at Nora. She nods.

You press the button.

Two seconds of nothing.

And then, from the roof of Gus's building, at the end of the street, Nora's voice comes out over the city.

It's enormous. It's beautiful. It's a love song, about a girl waiting for a bus in the rain, and it bounces off every building, every window, every empty car, all the way to the end of the street and back.

And the dead turn.

All of them. From every direction. You can hear it in your headphone: the shuffling turning, all at once, like a crowd at a concert, and starting to move. Toward the roof. Toward the song. Away from the river.

You wait three minutes, until the street outside the door goes quiet. Then you go.

You walk. You don't run. Running is loud. You go the way Gus drew it, down the middle of the road, past cars with their doors open, past the corner grocery, past a city bus still waiting at its stop with its doors open. Behind you, Nora's voice fills the whole sky. Nora walks beside you with her eyes wet and doesn't look back once.

One kilometer. Then a kilometer and a half.

At the last crossing before the river, one of them is standing in the middle of the road.

He's not moving toward the song. He's not moving at all. He's just standing there, facing you, swaying, with a broken earphone still hanging from one ear, and you realize, with a cold feeling in your stomach, that he can't hear the music. He can't hear anything. He's deaf. He was deaf before.

He sees you.

He starts toward you. Fast.

Gus freezes. Nora grabs your arm. There is nowhere to go, and nothing louder than you on this street.

So you make something louder.

You pull the flashlight out of your bag and throw it as hard as you can, past him, through the window of a parked car. Glass explodes. And he can't hear that either, but he sees it, the bright beam spinning, flashing across the road. He turns toward the light.

You go. All three of you, behind him, around him, across the crossing and down the last long street to the river, where the sky is getting pale, and the water is gray, and at the end of the old pier, with its engine running very quietly, there is a gray boat with a soldier standing on the front of it, waving one arm.

You run the last fifty meters. You don't care how loud it is anymore.

Gus first. Then Nora. Then you, last, jumping the gap between the pier and the boat, and a soldier's hand catches the strap of your bag, and you're on.

The boat pulls away from the city.

And from the water, as the sun comes up behind the buildings, you can still hear it. Far away, small now, over the rooftops. A girl singing a love song to a city full of the dead, and every one of them standing still under the roof, listening.

Nora sits on the floor of the boat, with her knees up, and her turquoise bag in her arms, and she listens to her own song until the city is too far away to hear.

"Somebody heard it," you say.

She laughs, and cries at the same time, and leans her head on your shoulder.

Thirty days. The same orange bandage. A singer, an old guitar man, and a night engineer, out of a room that was built to keep the whole world out. You spent a month learning to be the quietest people in the city. And in the end, what saved your life was the loudest song it ever heard.
