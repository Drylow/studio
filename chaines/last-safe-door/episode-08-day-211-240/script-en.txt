On day 211, nobody in the bunker has slept.

The radio sits on the bench between the three of you, hissing. You have taken turns holding it all night, as if holding it harder might bring her voice back. It didn't. The last thing it said was the first sound of a name.

Your name is Patch. Two hundred and ten days ago, you were waiting for a bus. Since then, you have lived under a bus depot with Nora, who used to clean it, and Wes, who used to live on a roof. Twenty days ago, the woman from the truck drove the last bus out of your yard with the whole horde following her horn. Your yard is empty now. Your street is empty. And she is sitting in that bus on the motorway, out of fuel, with every one of them standing around her.

At dawn, Nora puts the radio down and stands up.

"We're going to get her," she says.

You think of the rule. If they come toward the gate, you close it. Even if somebody is outside. You have kept that rule for two hundred days.

"The rule is about the gate," Nora says, as if she heard you think it. "It doesn't say anything about a truck."

Nobody asks who we means. Wes is already looking at the jump leads. You are already thinking about the truck.

"It's got half a tank," says Wes. "And no battery to speak of."

You remember a long ramp at night, and an engine switched off at the top of it. She taught you how to stop an engine on a hill.

"Then we'll need a hill to start one," you say.

Days 212 and 213 are for the truck.

It sits three streets away, where it died on day 141, white with frost, its cab door hanging open. Up close, it looks smaller than you remember. Most things do, once you have stopped being afraid of them.

On the radio, you ask the hill for help. By midday, a line of people with blue bandages walks down the hill road. A dozen of them. They have never pushed a truck before. Neither have you.

The street where the truck stands slopes gently down toward the lower town. You climb into the driver's seat. You have not driven anything since before day one, and never anything this big. Wes leans in through the window and explains the clutch to you in a whisper, three times, with hand movements.

On day 212, the first try goes wrong. The truck rolls halfway down the slope, you let the clutch out too early, and it stops dead with a jolt that bounces your head off the roof. A dozen people with blue bandages have to push it back up the hill. None of them complain. One of them pats the truck, as if it is a horse that has had a fright.

On the morning of day 213, everybody pushes. The truck creaks. Then rolls. Then rolls faster, the tyres crunching on frost, the hill people falling behind, waving. At the bottom of the slope, you let the clutch out the way Wes showed you.

The engine coughs. Coughs again. Catches.

For two hundred days, an engine starting has meant every dead head in town turning toward you. This time, the only heads that turn are a dozen living ones, at the top of the street, cheering without a sound, out of habit.

On day 214, Nora walks out of the gate.

Not into the yard. Out. Into the street. She has not been outside the depot walls since the morning it all started. She stops on the pavement, looks up and down the empty street, and holds the flashlight a little tighter.

"It's bigger out here," she says.

She turns and looks back at the depot from the outside. The wall. The gate. The sign over the street, its yellow paint run by the rain. THREE ALIVE. Only one of them is staying behind it.

"Everything is," says Wes, who is staying behind. Somebody has to keep the depot, and he says he has always wanted to be the one waiting at the gate. He has the radio, the rope on the bolt and the canopy ladder.

"If anything follows you home," he says, "I'll still be here."

Nora climbs into the cab beside you. You put the orange bag between your feet.

And for the first time in two hundred and fourteen days, the only one left behind the last safe door is a man in a red hat.

You drive down through the lower town, past the bus stop where a walker used to wait, past the dark petrol station, and up the long ramp of the flyover. At the top, you look down at the town. The hill. The tanks. The depot, very small, with one tiny figure on the gantry, flashing a mirror in the morning sun.

Then the motorway.

It is long and flat and grey. Abandoned cars stand in the lanes like toys somebody stopped playing with. Nora stares at all of it through the windshield, as if she is reading a book she always meant to read. Every few kilometers, you see a few stragglers wandering on the hard shoulder, walking toward the city. You don't stop. You don't use the horn. Their heads turn slowly to follow the truck as it passes, and Nora counts them under her breath, the way she used to count walkers gone.

By afternoon, you pass under a bridge. Beyond it, far ahead, the first houses of the city show against the sky. And in front of them, across every lane, there is something that looks like a dark lake.

It isn't a lake.

It is the river. Thousands of them, standing shoulder to shoulder across the whole width of the motorway, packed so tight that they look like one thing. All of them facing the middle. And in the middle, like a stone in a stream, a beige coach.

On the roof of the coach, beside the open hatch, a small figure sits cross-legged with a grey scarf around her neck. Her goggles catch the light.

"Two doors," Nora whispers. "She went out the second one."

You stop the truck well back and turn off the engine. On the radio, you try her channel. Nothing. Her battery is gone. But as you watch, the figure on the coach roof slowly raises one arm and waves. Once.

Still here.

On day 215, you make your plan.

The motorway has two sides, with a long metal barrier down the middle. The coach is on the city side. The other side is almost empty. The dead do not climb. A waist-high barrier is as good as a wall to them.

So at first light, you cross over to the empty side and drive slowly toward the coach. Nora holds the flashlight in her lap like a promise. When you are level with the edge of the river, you put your hand on the horn.

One short blast.

Every head on the motorway turns.

It is like watching a field of wheat turn in the wind. Thousands of faces swing away from the coach and toward the truck. Then they start to move. They come up against the barrier and press there, and as you roll forward, very slowly, they follow you along it, a whole motorway of the dead walking beside a truck, toward the city.

You keep going. One short blast. Another. Behind you, the river pulls away from the coach. Slowly, the coach comes out of the crowd, like a rock when the tide goes out.

The barrier creaks. Then it groans. A whole section of it leans toward you under the weight of a few thousand people pressing against it. You don't speed up. Speeding up is how you lose them. You just keep rolling, one short blast at every lamppost, and you try not to look at the barrier at all.

Two kilometers on, there is a junction. A long slip road curves up onto a bridge and down the far side.

You remember a woman holding a steering wheel with two fingers, like a teacup.

At the top of the bridge, you stop and lean on the horn. Not short. Long. It rolls out over the motorway and the fields and the first houses of the city.

Then you turn the key, and the engine dies.

The truck begins to roll. Down the far side of the slip road, silent, no lights, faster and faster. Nora grips the dashboard. You hold the wheel with two fingers and try very hard to look like someone who has done this before.

At the bottom, you coast on along an empty road, slow down, and stop behind a row of trees. Nora lets go of the dashboard one finger at a time. And you wait. Two hours. By then, a kilometer is a wall.

When you look back, the bridge at the top of the slip road is black from one railing to the other. The river is standing exactly where the last sound was, facing nowhere, waiting for a horn that is never going to blast again.

You drive back to the coach on the empty side of the motorway with your heart in your mouth.

It sits alone in the middle of its lanes. Almost alone. A handful of stragglers still stand around it, the slow ones and the stubborn ones, the ones who never follow anything the first time.

On the coach roof, the woman stands up. She reaches into the folds of her scarf, pulls out a small travel alarm clock with two bells on top, winds it, and throws it as far as she can, over the hard shoulder and into the frozen field beside the motorway.

A few seconds later, it starts to ring. A tiny, furious sound.

The stragglers turn toward it, one by one, and wander off into the field after a clock. They do not leave when the noise stops. They never do.

Every bus needs a clock.

She climbs down through the hatch, out through the folding door, across the lanes and over the barrier, without hurrying once. She opens the door of the truck, climbs into the cab beside Nora, and sits there for a moment, with her goggles pushed up, looking straight ahead.

Nora doesn't wait.

"Say it again," she says.

The woman looks at her for a long time. Then she smiles, which you have never seen her do.

"Vera," she says.

Nora nods slowly, as if she is writing it down somewhere inside. Then she knocks on Vera's shoulder. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

"It was a good bus," says Vera, looking back at the coach standing empty in its lanes. "Tell the night driver I said so."

From day 216 to day 218, you drive home the long way, and nobody is in a hurry.

You stop for the night on the flyover, at the very top, because from there you can see anything coming for kilometers. Vera sleeps sitting up in the cab, facing the door, out of habit. But she sleeps.

Nora stays awake looking at the sky. She has been under a ceiling for so long that she has forgotten how many stars there are.

On day 216, Vera tells you about the coach. When the fuel ran out, she climbed onto the roof and stayed there, because from up there she could see everything coming. She drank the frost that formed on the hatch every morning. She wound the clock every day at dawn, so she would know she was still here.

On day 217, you ask Vera what the city looked like up close.

"Empty streets," she says. "Shut windows. And one night, on the edge of it, one window with a light in it. Just one."

"Living?"

"Or very tidy dead," she says. "I wasn't in a position to knock."

On day 218, you drive down off the flyover into the lower town, and the gantry of the depot comes into view over the rooftops. A small light blinks at the end of it, in broad daylight, twice. Once for still here. Once for all of you.

On day 219, you stop the truck in front of your gate and turn the engine off.

Nobody inside needs to be told who it is. But Nora gets out anyway, walks up to the gate and knocks. Three times. Then a fourth time, softer.

The bolt lifts. The gate slides open with a polite little whisper. And there is Wes, in his red hat, with his arms out, as if he has been standing there for five days. He probably has.

"Welcome to the depot," he says. "Management apologizes for the delay."

Vera walks in through the gate. For a while, she just stands in the middle of the empty yard and turns around slowly, looking at the walls, the canopy, the bay door, the gantry. A place with one way out. Her eyes stop on the dark rectangle on the concrete, where the coach stood for a hundred and ninety days.

"Wes," she says. "My name's Vera."

Wes takes off his hat, the way he did once before, on day 77, and puts it on his chest.

"I know," he says. "Nora told me on the radio. Four times."

From day 220 to day 225, the depot changes its sign.

Nora fetches the tin of white paint from the rack. She climbs to the far end of the gantry, leans out over the street, crosses out THREE, and paints a big, careful FOUR. She takes her time. When she comes back, she has white paint on her purple band and does not care at all.

FOUR ALIVE.

Wes goes to the chart on the bunker wall. People met. He adds a mark, then stops, and looks at Vera.

"Do you count twice?"

"I count once," says Vera. "Like everybody else."

Nora takes the pencil from him. She rubs out the fourth column, the one that counted down to the river, the only column you ever kept that went backward. And at the top, she writes a new one. Days in daylight.

It already has twenty-nine marks.

From day 226 to day 232, the depot opens its doors, one at a time.

You take the desk and the trolley away from the bay door. The suitcases go back where they came from, mostly. The bay door swings open onto an empty yard, and it stays open all afternoon, for no reason except that it can.

On day 228, Nora walks to the far end of the depot building, to a narrow room you have never been in. A row of tall metal lockers. She stops at one of them and opens it with a small key from the bottom of her teal bag.

Inside, there is a folded cleaning jacket. A pair of gloves. And taped to the inside of the door, a postcard of a beach, bright blue sea and yellow sand.

"I always meant to go," she says.

Wes leans in to look at it. "That's the one," he says. "That's the beach. I promised."

She doesn't take the postcard down. She just leaves the locker open.

On day 230, you drive the truck to the hill with Vera, and bring back sacks of potatoes and apples and jars of beans, and you cook them on a fire in the middle of the yard, and nobody whispers.

From day 233 to day 239, the four of you learn to live in daylight.

Every morning, the truck goes out, one short blast at every corner, and leads whatever wandered into town overnight back out of it. Some mornings Vera drives. Some mornings, you do. The hill sends a boy with a blue bandage to learn the code.

Wes oils the gate every three days, although it doesn't need it. Nora sits in the yard in the sun every afternoon, against the same canopy pillar. Vera still sleeps sitting up, facing a door. But now she leaves it open.

At night, you all go back down to the bunker anyway. Up there, the sky is too big. Down here, there is a bench, a lantern, a wall chart, and a ceiling with nothing on it. You have never slept so well under anything.

The loudspeaker battery is full again. Wes checks that the switch says OFF, then tapes it down with a strip of tape, very neatly. Some promises are worth keeping, even to a loudspeaker.

On day 240, at sunset, the radio crackles.

It isn't Vera. She is sitting right next to you. It isn't the hill. You know every voice up there now. It is a new voice, a man's, on the exact channel painted on your sign. Careful. Tired. Polite.

"Depot. Depot. Do you read?"

The four of you look at each other.

You pick up the radio. "This is the depot."

A long pause.

"This is the city," says the voice. "A month ago, somebody drove a bus up the motorway with the biggest horde we have ever seen behind it, and left it standing on our doorstep. Then a white truck came along and parked the whole thing on our bridge. The bus had your depot's name painted on the side."

Vera closes her eyes. Her goggles are in her hand, and she turns them over and over, the way Nora held the keys on day 167.

"We'd like to talk to the driver," says the voice.

Two hundred and forty days. The same orange bandage. Nora is holding the flashlight. You are holding the orange bag. Tomorrow is day 241. Your yard is empty, your gate is your own, and the sign over the street says FOUR ALIVE. But the river you sent away didn't disappear. It went to somebody else's door. And now, for the first time, the living are knocking on yours to ask why.
