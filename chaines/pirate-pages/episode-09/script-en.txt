You have cooked every meal in this series. Nobody has thanked you once. Most of them do not even know your name.

So far in this series, you have been Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon, the carpenter, the navigator, the gunner and a frightened young captive. Every one of them ate your food. This time, you are the man behind the smoke: the ship's cook.

Imagine you are a cook of about fifty-five, aboard the same modest Caribbean sloop around seventeen seventeen. You are round and red-faced, with a bushy white mustache, thick white eyebrows and no beard, a faded red knitted cap, a blue-and-white checked shirt and a stained cream canvas apron with a wooden ladle tucked into its string. Your left leg has been stiff since a fall from the rigging in the last war, so you walk with a stick. You came to this crew of your own free will. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts, archaeology and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. Every barrel you open was taken from somebody. The sailors who loaded it, and the people who went hungry when it was gone, are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

You learned your trade in the navy, more or less. Royal Museums Greenwich, describing a print of a navy cook from seventeen ninety-nine, says ship's cooks were usually disabled seamen. They ranked as warrant officers, but they were not appointed for their cooking.

The historian Bruno Pappalardo, writing for History Hit, adds that until eighteen-oh-six, the only qualification for a navy ship's cook was a pension from the navy's fund for injured seamen. Such men, he writes, were often missing limbs. Their skill came from experience, if it came at all.

So the galley was where the navy put a sailor it could no longer send aloft. You did not choose cooking. Your leg chose it for you.

Pirates had their own answer for injured men. In the surgeon episode, we met the clause in Bartholomew Roberts's articles, as Captain Charles Johnson printed them: eight hundred dollars from the common stock for any man who lost a limb or became a cripple in the company's service. Johnson is a pen name, and his book of seventeen twenty-four mixes fact with invention, but the economist Peter Leeson reproduces the same articles.

And your share? The same articles give two shares to the captain and the quartermaster, one and a half to the master, the boatswain and the gunner, and one and a quarter to "other officers." Whether a cook counted as an officer, the articles do not say. On your sloop, the crew voted on it. You got one share. You have been thinking about that vote for two years.

Your kitchen is a small iron kettle on a box of bricks and sand near the mast, under a scrap of sailcloth. Your pantry is whatever the last prize was carrying.

Most days, that means the same few things sailors ate everywhere. Pappalardo describes the navy's victualling contract of sixteen seventy-seven: a pound of biscuit and a gallon of beer a day, and each week eight pounds of beef, or four of beef and two of pork, with two pints of peas. Royal Museums Greenwich sums up the result as boiled beef or pork and the ubiquitous ship's biscuit.

That is your menu too, minus the reliable delivery. The beef comes out of the cask the color of an old saddle. You boil it until it surrenders. You boil the peas until they forget they were ever peas. Tom calls it stew. You let him.

We know a little about what really sat in a pirate kettle. Conservators working on Blackbeard's Queen Anne's Revenge, which ran aground at Beaufort Inlet in seventeen eighteen, once opened a lump of concretion from the wreck.

Inside, according to their monthly report, were bone fragments and a tooth, layered together with pieces of cast iron the thickness of a cooking kettle. Some bones looked like pig or goat, others like cow, and the tooth closely resembled a pig's. Of twenty bones recovered earlier, nearly all had been identified as pig or cow.

The conservators asked whether it could be the remains of the last meal cooked aboard before she ran aground. They also admitted it might be scrap loaded into a cannon. The researcher Michaela Hoots, at East Carolina University, has since studied the ship's iron cauldron fragments, and how they might show who aboard was fed, and how.

Your own kettle is less mysterious. Everybody knows exactly what is in it. That is the problem.

Here is the part of your job that nobody puts in a song. On a pirate sloop, the food belonged to everyone.

The very first of Roberts's articles, as Johnson printed them, gave every man an equal title to the fresh provisions and strong liquors seized, unless a scarcity, which Johnson calls "no uncommon thing among them," made it necessary to vote a cut for the good of all.

That was a real change. Leeson quotes a sailor's complaint from a merchant ship in seventeen-oh-one: the crew "were att short allowance and wanted bread," while the officers kept their full allowance, as if there were no scarcity at all. On a pirate ship, Leeson writes, the power to allocate provisions went to the quartermaster, whom the crew elected.

So you do not decide who eats. The quartermaster does, and the crew watches him decide. You only decide how it tastes. Somehow, that is always the part they complain about.

Remember the young captive's first night aboard. Tom gave him half his biscuit, and the young man has thanked Tom for it ever since. Tom has never mentioned that the biscuit was yours, and that you had already given Tom two.

The captain gets no special plate either. Johnson says Roberts drank tea constantly, and that his crew would still walk into his cabin and seize part of his food and drink whenever they liked. On your sloop, the captain once asked for the last piece of fresh pork. Tom ate it while the vote was still being counted.

Fresh food, when you can find it, comes from the islands. Johnson writes that the small uninhabited islands and keys of the West Indies gave pirates what they often wanted: water, sea birds, turtle, shellfish and other fish.

He tells of Captain Thomas Anstis's crew, hiding at a little island off the southwest end of Cuba with provisions for only two months. They stayed about nine. For the whole time, Johnson says, they ate no meat but turtle, and no bread at all. Their rice was boiled, squeezed dry and eaten with the turtle.

Johnson even passes on their review. Some parts, he says, ate like chicken, and some like veal. It is the book's only restaurant review.

He also explains how they caught them. In May, June and July, he writes, the turtles came ashore at night to lay their eggs in the sand. The men waited in silence, without a light, and turned each turtle onto its back as soon as it landed. A turtle on its back cannot leave. Remember that. Tom will not.

At another island, Blanco, Johnson describes great numbers of iguanas, a lizard with a body as thick as a man's leg, "very good to eat," and "much used by the Pyrates that come here."

Your crew anchors off a sandy key, and you go ashore with a sack and a low opinion of lizards. Tom spends the whole night chasing a turtle up the beach. The turtle wins. In the morning, you serve iguana stew. Nobody asks what it is. Nobody asks for more, either.

Plenty is the exception. The rule is the cask, and the day the cask runs dry.

Johnson tells how Roberts's crew, sailing back across the Atlantic toward Surinam, had only one large cask of water left for one hundred and twenty-four people, and hundreds of leagues still to sail. They came down to one mouthful of water a day. Some drank seawater, which only made their thirst worse. Men died. When a boat finally came back from the coast with water, they had drunk nothing for two days.

Even then, Johnson adds, their food allowance stayed very small. A crew that voted on everything could still vote itself into famine.

On your sloop, it never gets that bad, but it gets bad enough. One dry week, the water ration is your job. You stand at the cask with a ladle and the quartermaster stands beside you with his book. Every man gets exactly one ladle. The captain gets one ladle. Tom gets one ladle, and asks whether rain counts as extra. You tell him rain is free. He spends the afternoon on his back with his mouth open.

Your fire is the one flame the crew cannot do without, and the one it fears most. Roberts's articles, as Johnson printed them, put out lights and candles at eight in the evening, and anyone still thirsty had to drink on the open deck. Your galley fire goes out at eight too. You bank it with sand and check it twice, then once more, because the gunner sleeps on the powder barrels a few steps away and has opinions about sparks.

Then a sail appears, and the crew remembers why they became pirates.

Prizes meant food, and the stories of pirate feasts are not all legend. In seventeen nineteen, at the Sierra Leone River, pirates under Thomas Cocklyn captured the slave ship of William Snelgrave.

Snelgrave wrote that their captain ordered a great meal from his stores: geese, turkeys, fowls and ducks, several Westphalia hams and a whole sow. All of it went into the ship's great furnace, which, he wrote, would boil food for five hundred enslaved Africans. Snelgrave's cook was ordered to boil the lot.

Nobody waited for it to be done properly. Snelgrave's sailors were made to cut off the birds' heads and pull out the big wing feathers, but the pirates would not wait for the rest to be plucked. The sow went in with her hair still on. Pirate cooking, as one eyewitness saw it, was mostly impatience.

Remember why that furnace was so large. It was built to feed the captives Snelgrave's ship had been sent to carry. A pirate feast was cooked in a pot made for captives.

Drink was the other prize. Royal Museums Greenwich says that in February seventeen twenty-two, Roberts's crew celebrated a fresh capture at Cape Lopez by drinking their way through its liquor. When the warship Swallow arrived the next morning, many of them were still drunk or badly hungover. Johnson agrees that the prize's liquor left them confused.

Your crew takes a merchant brig loaded with rum. That night, nobody is sober enough to stand watch except you, because somebody has to stop a drunk crew from relighting the galley fire at midnight to fry a ham. You sit by the kettle until dawn, with a bucket of sand and your stick across your knees. In the morning, the gunner thanks you. It is the first thanks you have had in two years.

Cooks also appear on the other side of the story, aboard the ships pirates robbed.

In April seventeen seventeen, pirates from Samuel Bellamy's Whydah took a small Dublin ship called the Mary Anne. A modern account in History Is Now magazine says she carried about seven thousand gallons of Madeira wine. Her own crew's sworn statement, printed in J. Franklin Jameson's collection of court records, says the pirates sent men into her hold for the wine, but a cable coiled in the hatchway blocked the way. They left with five bottles from the cabin.

Seven pirates stayed aboard to sail her. That night, in fog and a storm, the Mary Anne ran aground on Cape Cod. The Whydah was wrecked the same night.

Among the men left aboard was the Mary Anne's cook, Alexander Mackconachy, aged fifty-five, who signed his statement with a mark. When a local man named John Cole asked who these strangers were, Mackconachy answered that they were pirates, and had taken the ship. The seven were arrested and taken to Boston. Six of them were hanged that November.

A cook who signed with a mark had brought down seven pirates with one sentence.

Pirate crews needed cooks of their own, and they took them the same way they took surgeons and carpenters.

At the Cape Coast Castle trial of Roberts's crews in seventeen twenty-two, a witness described how one prisoner was taken. He had rowed his own captain over to the pirate ship. Roberts asked him whether he was a sailor. He said no, hoping that would get him sent home. Roberts answered, "What are you then? I want a Cook's Mate, and you shall go."

That man told the court he had served through the last war in the king's service, and showed an affidavit from his captain that he had been forced. He was acquitted.

Another cook saved somebody else. In our captive episode, we heard a witness accuse a prisoner of showing the pirates where a ship's cook had hidden his rings and buckles. Then the cook himself, Roger Shelton, took the oath. The rings, he said, were the prisoner's own: the prisoner had bought them from the cook with a written note, a promise to pay. The prisoner told the court that when the pirates took the rings, he told the cook to cancel the note. He was acquitted too.

And one cook stood before that court as a witness to something far darker. Edward Evans was the cook of the Porcupine, a slave ship that Roberts's men burned at Whydah in seventeen twenty-two, with enslaved Africans still aboard. Evans himself was acquitted. The captives aboard his ship were never given a hearing at all.

Back on your sloop, you get a mate of your own. The young sailor from the captive episode spends his evenings at your kettle, peeling, stirring and listening. You never asked for him. You just told the quartermaster you needed help, and nobody wanted to argue with the man who decides what goes in the stew.

You teach him the only rule that matters: the fire is never left alone. He learns it faster than Tom ever did.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You came willingly, so you have a vote. You look at the empty casks, at the bricks and the kettle, and at your stiff leg. You raise your hand for the pardon. It wins, narrowly.

The night before they sail in to surrender, you cook one last meal. There is no turtle and no ham. There is boiled beef, tired peas and the last of the biscuit, the same as always. For once, nobody complains. The captain eats exactly what Tom eats. The quartermaster does not write anything down.

In the harbor, the clerk takes down every name. When he reaches yours, he asks what you did aboard. You tell him you were the cook. He writes it down without looking up. You suspect that is the most dangerous thing anyone has ever believed about you.

So why was being a pirate cook worse than you think? Because the galley was where sailors went when their bodies could no longer climb. Because you fed a crew that owned every crumb and voted on every cut, and complained about the taste. Because the feasts were rare, the casks ran dry, and a single spark from your fire could end everyone's story.

And because every barrel you opened had been loaded by someone else, and some pots were built to feed people held as cargo. The legend remembers the rum. It forgets who carried it, and who went without.
