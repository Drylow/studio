You have cooked every meal in this series, and nobody has thanked you once. Step into the stained apron of an imagined pirate cook in the Caribbean around 1717, feeding Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon, the carpenter, the navigator, the gunner and the young captive from our earlier episodes, and discover what the galley really meant: a job the navy gave to injured seamen, a crew that owned every crumb and voted on every cut, turtle for nine months, one mouthful of water a day, a feast boiled in a slave ship's furnace, and the cooks who turned up as witnesses when pirates finally stood trial. This illustrated story draws on the 1722 Cape Coast Castle trial record, the Boston court records of 1717, William Snelgrave's eyewitness account, a conservation report from Blackbeard's Queen Anne's Revenge, Royal Museums Greenwich, History Hit, History Is Now, Captain Charles Johnson's General History of the Pyrates (used critically), and research by the economist Peter Leeson. The cook and crew are fictional, but piracy harmed real people, including the sailors whose stores filled the pirates' kettle and the captives the law treated as cargo.
