You are the frightened young sailor who handed the quartermaster the ship's papers with both hands. You thought that was the worst day of your life. It was only the first one.

So far in this series, you have been Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon, the carpenter, the navigator and the gunner. Every one of them belonged to the crew, or was made to. This time, you are on the other side of the rail. You are what the crew takes.

Imagine you are a merchant sailor of nineteen, in the Caribbean around seventeen seventeen. You are thin, with straight black hair, a first wispy mustache, a faded red-and-white striped shirt and canvas trousers rolled to the knee, and you work barefoot like most sailors. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts, a captive's own memoir and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember who this story is about. Not the legend. The people the legend robbed.

A few weeks after that first robbery, you sign on to a new sloop carrying flour and timber between the islands. The pay is poor, but at least the ship is still yours to sail. Then, on the third morning out, a familiar sail comes over the horizon.

Same sloop. Same crew. The same quartermaster comes over the rail first, looks at you for a long moment, and says, "Again?"

This time, they do not just take the cargo. Since they captured a second sloop, the crew has two vessels and not enough hands to sail them. The captain looks over your crew the way a buyer looks at barrels. He points at you.

Real pirate crews filled their ranks from the ships they robbed. Over the last four episodes, we met men taken for their skills: surgeons, carpenters, navigators and gunners. But ordinary sailors and fishermen were taken too.

In June seventeen twenty-two, Edward Low's pirates captured a young fisherman from Marblehead, Massachusetts, named Philip Ashton, off the coast of Nova Scotia. His story survives because he told it himself, in a memoir published in seventeen twenty-five.

According to Ashton's own account, Low asked the captives whether they were married. In the Boston court files, the pirates who kept the carpenter Thomas Davis in seventeen sixteen said they kept him because he was "a carpenter and a single man." A man with nobody waiting at home was easier to keep.

The captain asks you the same question. You say yes, very quickly. You have never been married in your life. It does not help. You are taken across to the pirate sloop with nothing but the striped shirt you are wearing. From the flour sloop, an old gray-bearded shipmate raises one hand slowly, the way people wave at a funeral. You wave back. Nobody stops either of you.

Your first night aboard, Tom gives you half his biscuit. He tells you he came aboard of his own free will. He says it as if it were a confession. You notice he does not look at you while he says it.

The next evening, the quartermaster brings out the articles and a pen.

You have heard what happened to men who refused. Ashton refused to join Low's crew. In his memoir, he wrote that Low "cocked his pistol and clapped it to my head." He was threatened again and again, especially by Low's quartermaster, John Russell, who by Ashton's account once tried to shoot him with a pistol that misfired, again and again. Ashton still refused.

At the Cape Coast Castle trial of Bartholomew Roberts's crews in seventeen twenty-two, prisoner after prisoner said he had been forced to sign. Some described being threatened by Roberts himself.

And here is the cruel part. Captain Charles Johnson's version of that trial, printed in his General History of the Pyrates in seventeen twenty-four, records the court's suspicion. A pistol and the articles, "served up in a dish together," the court observed, was often a performance arranged between the pirates and the new man, so that he would look forced if he were ever caught. Johnson is a pen name, and his book mixes fact with invention, but the trial record shows the court weighing that doubt case by case.

So even a pistol to your head might not be enough to prove that you were forced. The court would ask whether you meant it.

You do not sign. The captain shrugs. The quartermaster writes something in his book. For once, you are certain what the word is, because you watch him write it: "forced." It is the most important word anyone has written about you in your life. Beside it, he adds the date and the name of your flour sloop. Nobody has ever bothered to write down where you came from before.

Forced men knew they would need proof, and they looked for it everywhere.

At Cape Coast, two musicians from the ship Cornwall said they had given all their wages to their own captain, Captain Rolls, begging him to put them in the Gazette as forced men. A newspaper notice that a man had been taken by force could be evidence later. Both musicians were acquitted.

A witness said the eager surgeon Peter Scudamore had asked the same captain to put him in the Gazette. The captain refused, and the witness believed no force had been used on him at all. Scudamore was hanged.

Newspapers of the time were full of captives' stories. Leeson notes that eighteenth-century papers published pirate victims' accounts, sometimes describing the pirates' flag, their numbers and even their captain's temper. For a family at home, a line of print might be the only sign that a son was still alive.

Johnson's version of the trial explains why the court mistrusted those notices: they were meant "to answer the end of being put in a newspaper or affidavit." The proof could be faked. So the court looked for something harder to fake: witnesses.

Months ago, a ship's surgeon taken from your old sloop asked you to tell anyone who asked that he had been taken by force. You nodded so hard he was not sure you heard him. You heard him. Now you understand exactly what he was asking for.

The merchant sloop you were taken from is about to sail away. You run to the rail and shout your name to her master, and the name of your mother's village. Tell them I was forced, you shout. He nods. You hope he is a better witness than you were.

Life as a forced man is mostly work. You pump, haul, scrub and stand watch like everyone else. The carpenter shows you how to caulk a seam. The navigator lets you hold the sandglass. The gunner makes you sponge out a gun, and is very serious about it.

But nobody quite trusts you. In Boston, in seventeen seventeen, a shipmate of the forced carpenter Thomas Davis testified that he kept his distance from Davis aboard Bellamy's ship, because the pirates suspected forced men of planning to run away together.

And escape was dangerous. Johnson describes Phillips's crew killing men who tried to leave, and says those severities made it dangerous for anyone even to talk about escaping. A Boston deposition confirms that two of Phillips's men had been shot by their captain.

One night at the tiller, the navigator tells you about Harry Glasby, Roberts's forced master, who tried once to slip away and was never allowed off the ship again. He tells it quietly, as a warning and as a kindness.

So you keep your head down, and you keep your eyes on the horizon.

On a long night watch, Tom finally tells you why he joined. A man in a tavern described a share of captured wealth, and described it more carefully than the work. Tom imagined gold and a magnificent coat. He got a bucket. He tells you this as if it were funny. Then he says, quietly, that at least he chose the bucket. You did not even get to choose that.

You think about your mother's village, about the flour sloop, and about the merchant captain who promised to tell people your name. You wonder if anyone at home knows you are alive. Then the bell rings for the next watch, and you go back to the pump.

Then comes the order you have been dreading. A sail appears, the chase ends in surrender, and the quartermaster puts a cutlass in your hand. "Boarding list," he says. "Your turn."

You climb over the rail with the others. You hold the cutlass the way Tom holds a bucket, as far from your body as possible. You do not raise it. You do not take anything. But the merchant sailors staring at you do not know that you are forced. To them, you are just another pirate with a blade.

This mattered more than any oath. In the quartermaster episode, we heard how witnesses in Boston said that John Rose Archer went armed aboard every vessel his crew took. At Cape Coast, Johnson's version of the trial puts the court's rule plainly: "it is not so material how a man comes among pirates, as how he acts when he is there." A forced man seen armed on a prize could be hanged as a volunteer.

That night, you ask the quartermaster to write it in his book: armed by order, took nothing. He looks at you for a long time. Then he writes it. You do not know if that is kindness, or simply very good bookkeeping.

Being a captive was not only about being forced to join. It was about watching everything that happened to the other captives.

The economist Peter Leeson argues that pirates worked hard to build a reputation for punishing captives who hid valuables, and rewarding those who cooperated. At Cape Coast, a witness said that one captured sailor had shown the pirates where his own ship's cook had hidden some rings and buckles, and had them taken. Captives could be turned against each other.

You watch it happen on your first prize as a forced man. The pirates ask a frightened passenger where the money is. A sailor from his own ship points before the passenger can answer. The quartermaster takes the purse. Nobody looks at anybody.

Some captives were held for weeks. William Snelgrave, the slave-trading captain taken by pirates at the Sierra Leone River in seventeen nineteen, wrote that by the time the pirates sailed away, he had been in their hands a month, "and many others much longer."

Some captives were held as bargaining pieces. When Blackbeard blockaded Charleston in seventeen eighteen, Royal Museums Greenwich says he took the crews of ships as hostages and threatened to kill them unless the town paid his ransom of medicine.

And some captives never had any hope of a hearing at all. After Roberts's crews were captured in seventeen twenty-two, the historian David Wilson writes that the remaining captives, described only as "black men," were sold into slavery without trial. The Canadian Encyclopedia puts their number at seventy-seven.

The Whydah's pilot, John Julian, survived the wreck of seventeen seventeen. Court records describe him as Native American. He was never tried, and History Is Now magazine says he was sold into slavery.

For them, there was no newspaper notice to buy, no captain to plead, no court to persuade. The law did not ask whether they had been forced. It treated them as property.

Weeks pass. Your crew anchors off a quiet island to take on water, and you are sent ashore with the water barrels for the first time. The trees come right down to the beach. You think about running.

Philip Ashton did run. In March seventeen twenty-three, after about nine months as Low's captive, he went ashore with a watering party at Roatán, an island off the coast of Central America. He slipped into the woods and hid until the pirates gave up looking and sailed away.

He was free, and for a long time completely alone. He survived on the island for about sixteen months, through heat, insects and hunger, with no tools to hunt or fish. He was finally carried home aboard a ship to Salem, and reached Massachusetts in seventeen twenty-five.

That same year, his minister, John Barnard, published his story in Boston. The title is longer than most chapters: Ashton's Memorial, an History of the Strange Adventures, and Signal Deliverances, of Mr. Philip Ashton.

You look at the trees for a long time. Then you look at your bare feet. Then you think about sixteen months. You carry the water barrels back to the boat. You are not proud of it. You are alive.

Other captives found a different way out. In April seventeen twenty-four, the men held prisoner aboard John Phillips's pirate sloop rose up together, led by captured men like the fisherman John Fillmore and the forced carpenter Edward Cheeseman, and overpowered the crew within minutes.

In Boston that May, the court acquitted all seven of the men who had planned the rising together. Their own testimony, and each other's, proved what they were. Fillmore later said the court even gave him the dead pirate captain's sword and gun.

Witnesses were everything. At Cape Coast, more than seventy of Roberts's men were acquitted, most of them men who had been forced aboard, often because somebody stood up and said so. Fifty-two were hanged.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You never signed, so you have no vote. You listen to the arguments from the hatch, holding your breath. The pardon wins, narrowly. You discover that you are crying, and so, for some reason, is Tom.

The sloop sails into a colonial harbor under a white flag, past a stone fort with its guns run out, just in case. Soldiers line the quay. The whole crew is marched to a long table under an awning, where a clerk in a wig sits with a ledger even bigger than the quartermaster's. Somewhere behind you, the gunner mutters that the fort's guns are pointed at the wrong ship.

The clerk takes down every name. He reaches yours and asks the question you have rehearsed for months. Did you come willingly?

You do not have a newspaper notice. You do not know whether the merchant captain ever told anyone. But you have three things.

The quartermaster opens his book and shows the clerk the word next to your name: "forced." The surgeon in his leather apron steps forward and says he saw you taken. You once promised to speak for him. Today, he speaks for you. And Tom, who has never once said the right thing at the right time, says it now. "He never wanted to be here. I gave him half my biscuit."

The clerk writes something down. You very much hope the word is "forced."

A week later, a small trading boat drops you at the jetty below your mother's village. You walk up the path barefoot, in the same striped shirt, now mended twice by a pirate carpenter. Your mother is at the door. She has heard nothing at all. No newspaper ever printed your name. The merchant captain never came. For her, you were simply gone, and now you are simply back.

You do not tell her everything. You tell her about Tom's biscuit.

So why was being a pirate captive worse than you think? Because the crew needed your hands, and asked whether anybody would miss you. Because even a pistol to your head might not prove you were forced. Because escape could mean a bullet, or sixteen months alone on an island.

And because, for the people the law already treated as property, there was no notice to buy and no witness who could save them. The legend calls it freedom. For a captive, piracy was something that happened to you.
