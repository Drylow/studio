They robbed your ship once. The second time, they kept you. Step into the striped shirt of an imagined young merchant sailor taken by the same crew as Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon, the carpenter, the navigator and the gunner from our earlier episodes, in the Caribbean around 1717, and see piracy from the other side of the rail: being chosen because nobody would miss you, refusing to sign with a pistol in the room, paying for a newspaper notice to prove you were forced, being sent aboard a prize with a cutlass you never wanted, watching other captives turned against each other, and deciding whether to run. This illustrated story draws on the 1722 Cape Coast Castle trial record, the Boston court records of 1717 and 1724, Philip Ashton's memoir of his captivity with Edward Low's crew, William Snelgrave's eyewitness account, Captain Charles Johnson's General History of the Pyrates (used critically), Royal Museums Greenwich, and research by the economist Peter Leeson and the historian David Wilson. The young sailor and crew are fictional, but piracy harmed real people, above all those the law treated as property, who were never given a hearing at all.
