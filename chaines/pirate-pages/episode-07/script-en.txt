You are in charge of the most frightening thing aboard. Two small cannons. And the whole point of your job is to never have to use them.

So far in this series, you have been Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon, the carpenter and the navigator. This time, you are the man who sleeps nearest the gunpowder: the gunner.

Imagine you are a gunner of about forty-five, aboard the same modest Caribbean sloop around seventeen seventeen. You are short and stocky, with a shaved head, a thick black beard going gray, a sleeveless leather jerkin over a gray shirt, a powder horn on a cord and a coil of slow match at your belt. You served on merchant ships before this one, and you came to the pirates of your own free will. Years beside the guns have left you hearing about half of what the crew says. You consider it the better half. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts, archaeology and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. Every gun you load exists to frighten, or to hurt, people who were only trying to do their work at sea. The sailors aboard the ships you threaten are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

Pirate crews valued gunners. In the articles that Captain Charles Johnson printed for Bartholomew Roberts's crew, the gunner received one and a half shares of a prize, the same as the master and the boatswain. The economist Peter Leeson reproduces the same clause. Johnson is a pen name, and his book of seventeen twenty-four mixes fact with invention, but on this point the two agree.

Your day is mostly housekeeping. You check the two guns, their carriages and their ropes. You keep the powder dry, the shot stacked and the match coiled. You inspect every pistol aboard, because Roberts's articles, as Johnson printed them, required every man to keep his piece, pistols and cutlass clean and fit for service.

You also run the most important rule aboard: no fire near the powder. John Phillips's articles, which only Johnson prints, banned smoking in the hold without a cap on the pipe, and any candle without a lantern. A wooden ship full of powder, rum and tired men is not forgiving.

Tom asks if he can light his pipe near the guns. You do not answer. You just look at him until he goes away. That is about half of gunnery.

The other half is a routine you could do asleep. Sponge out the barrel, so no spark survives from the last shot. Powder charge in, then a wad of old rope, then the ball, then another wad to hold it. Run the gun out through its port. Prick the cartridge through the touch-hole, prime it with fine powder, stand clear of the wheels, and touch it off with the glowing match. Then do it all again, faster, while somebody is shooting back.

You offer to teach Tom. The crew votes against it. Powder costs money, practice shots hit nothing worth selling, and nobody wants to explain to the quartermaster where the ball went. So Tom learns to sponge the barrel, and is very proud of it. It is, you admit, the most important step.

Here is the secret nobody puts on the poster. On a pirate sloop, a good gunner almost never fires.

Pirates wanted prizes, not battles. Leeson argues that crews promised mercy to merchant ships that surrendered without a fight, and threatened much worse for those that resisted. The legal scholar Magne Frostad told ScienceNorway that pirates hoped their flag alone would be frightening enough to make victims surrender.

A fight risked wounded men, a damaged ship and a damaged prize. Every shot you fire into a merchant ship is a hole the carpenter has to fix later, in a ship you were planning to rob.

So when a sail appears, and the chase begins, your moment is small. You fire one gun, aimed well ahead of the merchant's bow, to tell her to stop. That was a real tactic. At the Cape Coast Castle trial in seventeen twenty-two, a Dutch captain said that two pirate ships "fired and brought him to," which means they forced him to stop.

For one bad moment, the merchant answers. A puff of smoke from her stern, a bang, and a ball skips across the water well short of your bow. Her captain is brave, or frightened, or both. The crew looks at you. You keep your second gun quiet and wait, because a fight is exactly what nobody aboard either ship actually wants.

The merchant lowers her sails. Nobody on either deck is hurt. The crew cheers the captain. You swab out the barrel and say nothing. A shot that did not need a second shot is the best shot there is.

Later, on her deck, you meet the man who fired that shot: the merchant's own gunner, gray-haired and shaking. He tells you his owners would have asked why he never fired at all. You tell him it was a good shot, just short, and that short was the smartest place to put it. If he had hit your sloop, your crew would not have been so calm. He looks at you as if he does not know whether to thank you. Neither do you.

Then you go down into her hold, with the boarders, to collect your share of the shopping list.

On Roberts's ships, a gunner went straight for the prize's gunner's stores: powder, shot, match, flints. At Cape Coast, two Dutch captains told the court that one of Roberts's men, Thomas Sutton, had come aboard their ships armed and taken their gunner's stores. The court record later lists Sutton as a gunner.

In Johnson's version of the trial, one of the Dutch captains said Sutton had ordered all their gunner's stores out. Sutton interrupted him from the dock. The man was lying, he said. He had not taken half.

You take powder in its barrels, a few bags of small shot and all the flints you can find. You leave the merchant one small gun and enough powder to fire a warning of his own, in case he meets somebody worse than you. There is always somebody worse than you.

Gunners themselves were often taken by force. The court heard about one gunner taken from a Liverpool ship in seventeen twenty-one, whose own captain had begged the pirates to let him stay, "because he was his gunner, and very much wanted by him." The pirates refused.

Witnesses said that same gunner had tried to escape several times. Once he slipped back aboard his own ship and hid, and the pirates sent for him by name. The court acquitted him.

Another witness said the gunner of the Bristol galley Mercy had been forced out of his ship with Roberts himself beating him aboard the pirate ship. Being good with a gun was not something you could hide for long.

Others were simply put to work near the powder. A witness said that one man, Hugh Menzies, was forced off his ship and beaten twice because he cried and begged to get away, and that the pirates mockingly called him "Dead Hugh Menzies." In the fight with the Swallow, he was stationed in the powder room, and the same witness said he did much to save the ship from being blown up.

Another prisoner, a witness said, became gunner's mate only because Roberts's gunner swore that he should accept it. On a pirate ship, even a promotion could be an order.

Back on your sloop, the new powder needs a home. You stow the barrels deep in the hold, in the driest corner, as far from the galley fire as a small sloop allows, which is not very far. You sleep on top of them. The crew considers this brave. You consider it supervision.

We know what a real pirate gunner kept loaded, because Blackbeard's flagship sank with her guns still aboard. When the Queen Anne's Revenge ran aground at Beaufort Inlet, North Carolina, in June seventeen eighteen, she went down armed.

A nineteen ninety-nine report from the North Carolina conservation laboratory explains that La Concorde had carried fourteen to sixteen guns as a French slave ship. Blackbeard's crew increased that to as many as forty. Royal Museums Greenwich also says Blackbeard equipped her with forty guns.

Archaeologists have found the guns. The nineteen ninety-nine report counted twenty-one cannons on the site so far. By two thousand thirteen, the Maritime Executive reported, twenty-nine had been located and twenty raised. Both describe most of them as cast-iron six-pounders, about eight feet long.

Some were still loaded. In two thousand eleven, the project's chief conservator, Sarah Watkins-Kenney, said that four cannons had been found loaded, "with cannon shot and wads in place ready to be fired."

The nineteen ninety-nine report describes one small gun, cast in Sweden and dated seventeen thirteen. Inside, behind its wooden plug, conservators found a wad of rope, then three iron drift pins, which are heavy spikes used in shipbuilding, then a second wad, a solid round shot, a third wad and finally the powder charge. Somebody had loaded spare iron along with the ball.

Even three centuries later, the report treats that powder as live. The conservators pulled the wads out with a copper wire hook, made a scoop from plastic pipe shaped like an old powder scoop, and filled the barrel with fresh water in case a metal tool struck a spark. A gunner would have approved.

The same report describes two cast-iron hand grenades from the wreck, hollow spheres packed with gunpowder, with a wooden fuse.

And in twenty eighteen, conservators found sixteen scraps of paper wedged inside the chamber of a breech-loading cannon from the wreck. The Associated Press reported that the fabric and plug around them had protected the paper for three hundred years.

Researchers identified the scraps. They came from a first edition, printed in seventeen twelve, of Captain Edward Cooke's A Voyage to the South Sea, the story of a privateering voyage around the world. Cooke had sailed on that voyage under Woodes Rogers.

Yes, that Woodes Rogers. The same man who arrived at Nassau as governor in seventeen eighteen, with the king's pardon and warships behind him. Blackbeard's gunners may have used a book about his voyage to pack a cannon. History has a nasty sense of humor.

Most pirate voyages never needed those loaded guns. But when a real battle came, it was the gunners who decided it.

In November seventeen eighteen, at Ocracoke Inlet, Lieutenant Robert Maynard's sloops rowed toward Blackbeard's. Johnson writes that Blackbeard's men fired a broadside "charged with all manner of small shot," and that the lieutenant's sloop had twenty men killed and wounded. The Smithsonian puts it at twenty-one, in seconds.

Both accounts say Blackbeard's crew then threw grenades, which Johnson describes as case bottles filled with powder and small shot. Maynard had hidden most of his remaining men below. When Blackbeard boarded, they came up, and Blackbeard was killed in the fight that followed.

Johnson's book alone lists Blackbeard's gunner, Philip Morton, among the pirates killed that day. Most of Blackbeard's surviving men were later tried and hanged in Virginia, as the South Carolina Encyclopedia and Johnson both record. The broadside had won the first minute. It did not win the day.

Three years later, off West Africa, gunnery decided another fight. Royal Museums Greenwich says that on the tenth of February, seventeen twenty-two, a series of broadsides from Captain Chaloner Ogle's ship, the Swallow, left Bartholomew Roberts's ship nearly unable to move, her masts and rigging shattered.

Grapeshot from the Swallow, lead balls fired together to strike men rather than wood, killed Roberts at once. Johnson describes the same shot. His crew fought on for a time, and then surrendered.

That is the uncomfortable math of your trade. Your guns were made to win without firing. When they did fire, they could win the first minute and still lose the fight, or meet a navy warship with far more guns than any pirate sloop.

You think about that during a quiet week of rain. Rain is your real enemy. Damp powder will not fire, and a gun that will not fire is just a heavy bucket.

So you spend the whole week opening barrels, spreading powder on sailcloth under a tarpaulin whenever the sun comes out, and covering it again when it does not. The crew watches you guard black dust as if it were gold. In a way, it is. Without it, your two little guns are furniture.

Then, one evening, a spark jumps from the galley fire and lands near a loose powder bag on the deck. Everyone freezes. Tom, who has never moved quickly in this entire series, steps on it with his boot before anyone else can breathe.

You thank him. He says he has been practicing with his pipe. You decide not to ask what that means.

The men who ran the guns did not do well in court. At Cape Coast, Thomas Sutton, the gunner who had not taken half, was sentenced to death, and he was among the first six men hanged outside the castle.

Johnson's book alone gives Sutton a last joke. In irons, Sutton is said to have mocked a prisoner who prayed for heaven. Did he ever hear of pirates going there, Sutton asked. He would rather go to the other place, where it was merrier, and he would give Roberts a salute of thirteen guns when he arrived.

Whether he said it or not, that is a gunner's joke. Even facing the gallows, he was still thinking in salutes.

On John Phillips's crew, the gunner, James Sparks, did not survive the day the captives rose up in April seventeen twenty-four. The Boston News-Letter reported that the captives fell upon the gunner, and the pirates were overpowered within minutes.

In all, fifty-two of Roberts's men were hanged after the Cape Coast trial. More than seventy were acquitted, most of them men who had been forced aboard. The gunner from the Liverpool ship walked free. Thomas Sutton did not.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You read the reward list. As the London Gazette printed it, a pirate commander was worth one hundred pounds, and "every lieutenant, master, boatswain, carpenter, and gunner" forty. Your name is on it, beside the carpenter and the navigator.

You came willingly, so you have a vote. You look at your two little guns, and at the warships the dealer says are coming. You raise your hand for the pardon. It wins, narrowly.

The captain asks whether you will fire a salute as they sail in to surrender. You say no. You have a better idea.

That evening, you draw the charges from both guns: the wad, the shot, the powder, one careful piece at a time, the way the conservators would do it three hundred years later on Blackbeard's cannons. You lay it all out on the deck in a neat row. An unloaded gun cannot start anything by accident. Neither, you hope, can you.

Tom watches the whole thing, sponge in hand, and asks what happens to the guns now. You tell him they will probably end up on somebody else's ship, aimed at somebody else's problems. He asks if he can keep the sponge. You let him. He has earned it.

So why was being a pirate gunner worse than you think? Because your best day was the day you never fired. Because you slept on the one cargo that could kill everyone aboard. Because when a real battle came, your guns could win the first minute and still lose you everything.

And because every gun you aimed was aimed at people whose only crime was being in the way. A court did not care how good a shot you were. It cared who you had aimed at.
