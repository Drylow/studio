You are the reason the sloop is still floating. Nobody has thanked you for it in four episodes. They have been busy with coats, votes and medicine chests, while you were down in the hold with a hammer, keeping the sea outside.

So far in this series, you have been Tom, the captain, the quartermaster and the surgeon the crew kept. This time, you are the man who was below the whole time: the carpenter.

Imagine you are a ship's carpenter of about fifty, aboard the same modest Caribbean sloop around seventeen seventeen. You are broad and bald, with a short gray beard, rolled sleeves and a brown canvas apron full of tools. You joined this crew of your own free will, long before Tom arrived. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. Every repair you make keeps a crew of robbers at sea. The sailors on the ships they board are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

Your day starts at the pump. A wooden hull always lets some water in, and yours lets in more than most. Half the water Tom carried in his famous bucket was, strictly speaking, your department.

You sound the well with a weighted line, read the wet mark, and decide how worried to be. Then you go looking for the leak with a lantern, a mallet, a bag of oakum and a pot of hot pitch. Oakum is old rope picked apart into loose fibers. You hammer it into the seams between the planks, seal it with pitch, and the sea comes in a little more slowly. That is the whole glamorous secret of your trade.

You know why you left merchant ships. The economist Peter Leeson quotes the maritime historian Ralph Davis: on a merchant ship, the captain "had absolute authority over the mates, the carpenters and boatswain, and the seamen." On this sloop, the captain cannot even keep his own coat.

Buccaneer crews knew what carpenters were worth. Alexander Exquemelin, who sailed with them in the sixteen sixties and seventies, wrote that before any prize was shared, the carpenter who careened, mended and rigged the vessel received one hundred or one hundred and fifty pieces of eight.

That sounds generous until you remember the surgeon. His medicine chest alone was rated at two hundred or more. As the surgeon heard last time, a box of jars was rated above the man who keeps the ship afloat. You noticed. You said nothing. You are a carpenter. You measure twice.

Pirates valued carpenters so highly that many never got the chance to volunteer.

In December seventeen sixteen, two pirate sloops under Samuel Bellamy and Louis Lebous took the Bristol ship Saint Michael, bound for Jamaica. According to the depositions in the Boston court files, they kept her carpenter, Thomas Davis, because he was "a carpenter and a single man."

Davis begged to be released. Bellamy promised to let him go when they took the next ship. When the moment came, he asked the company. A witness said the crew answered violently: no, they would shoot him or whip him to death at the mast first. Another witness said they refused "by reason he was a carpenter."

So Davis stayed. In April seventeen seventeen, Bellamy's flagship, the Whydah, was wrecked off Cape Cod. Davis was one of only two men known to have survived that wreck.

Even before the wreck, being wanted did not make him trusted. A shipmate testified in Boston that he had kept his distance from Davis aboard the Whydah. The pirates suspected Davis because he was a forced man, and the shipmate did not want them to think the two of them were planning to run away together.

Blackbeard's crew did the same thing with La Concorde, the slave ship they captured in November seventeen seventeen. The Queen Anne's Revenge project lists carpenters among the ten Frenchmen forced to stay. Johnson's book alone tells of Edward Low's crew forcing away the carpenter of a captured ship.

A few weeks ago, when your crew took the second sloop, the faster one, somebody suggested keeping her carpenter as your mate. You looked at a frightened man holding his tool bag with both hands. You argued that a forced man with a saw is not a man you want behind you. The vote went your way. You still feel good about that, and slightly less good about why you said it.

Then a sail appears, the chase begins, and the merchant ship surrenders. When the boarders climb aboard, you go with them. Not for the money. For the nails.

On Roberts's ships, this was a known routine. At the Cape Coast Castle trial in seventeen twenty-two, a witness said one carpenter's mate went aboard prizes to fetch carpenter's stores, "as was the custom among them."

The mate of the King Solomon remembered that same man coming over in the pirates' boat for carpenter's stores, and threatening the ship's own carpenter for bringing up only one bucket of rosin. Johnson's version of the record lists the robbery job by job: the boatswain took the cables, the cooper the casks, and this man "the carpenter's stores."

His name was John Griffin, a ship's carpenter from Blackwall, near London. A witness said it was common talk aboard that all the men taken from his ship, the Mercy, had been forced. It did not save him. Griffin was condemned, and then became one of the twenty men whose sentence was changed to seven years of forced labour on the West African coast. Being valuable to pirates did not make a man safe from the court.

A shipmate said another carpenter, taken from a different ship, had been forced, then "voted carpenter" by the pirates, a post he accepted "very unwillingly," often lamenting his condition. Another witness said Roberts used oaths and foul language to send him aboard prizes for whatever stores they wanted, "saying he was the best judge."

That carpenter told the court about a captured brigantine. Her own carpenter warned him she was leaky. So he went back to Roberts to object to taking her pump. Roberts swore that he would have not only the pump, but the mainmast too if he wanted it. A second witness said the carpenter had gone to Roberts twice about it.

Robbing a leaking ship of her pump is about the cruellest thing one carpenter can do to another. You think about that while you choose. Oakum, for caulking seams. Tar. Nails. A coil of line. One good spare spar that will not fit anywhere, and that you take anyway.

The merchant ship's own carpenter watches you from beside her mainmast. He is younger than you, with a tool bag he clearly paid for himself. He does not say a word. He does not have to. You both know exactly what a missing auger means three weeks from the nearest harbor.

You leave him his tools. You leave the merchant his pump. Neither of them thanks you. From their side of the rail, a polite robber is still a robber.

One eyewitness did remember a decent pirate carpenter. In seventeen nineteen, William Snelgrave, the slave-trading captain held by pirates in West Africa, found a pirate carpenter asleep on his own bed in his cabin.

When the carpenter woke, Snelgrave wrote, he politely told him to go and get some rest. Later that night, a drunk boatswain came in and abused Snelgrave, and the carpenter called the boatswain a base villain and threw him out.

When a gust of wind blew their candle out, the boatswain accused Snelgrave of trying to sneak into the powder room and blow up the ship. The carpenter told him it was an accident, and that Snelgrave was still sitting beside him. In Snelgrave's story, the most reasonable man aboard a pirate ship was the one with the saw.

The second sloop is now yours to fix. Taking a ship was one thing. Turning her into a pirate ship was another, and that was carpenters' work.

Real crews did it constantly. Most of La Concorde's cannons had been left in France, the Smithsonian notes, to make room for her captives. Yet Royal Museums Greenwich says Blackbeard equipped her with an imposing forty guns as his flagship.

Someone had to make room for those guns, fit them and keep that ship sound under their weight. Ships do not arm themselves.

At Whydah in seventeen twenty-two, as Captain Chaloner Ogle reported, Roberts's men carried off a French ship planning to fit her for their own service. Every plan like that ended at a carpenter's bench.

Your bench is the deck. You open up space below for more men, mend the rails and patch the boat. The crew calls this "making her ready." You call it three weeks of splinters.

Then a squall hits both sloops at night. By morning, the old sloop's mast has a long crack near the top. Nobody can buy a new mast at sea. So you bind the cracked mast between two lengths of spare timber, lashed tight with rope, the way you were taught as an apprentice. That spare spar you stole is suddenly the most popular object aboard.

The captain asks whether it will hold. You say yes, in the voice carpenters use when they mean "probably". The crew hears "yes". The mast hears "probably". You spend the next week looking up.

In a chase, your place is not with the boarders. You stand below with wooden plugs, oakum and a mallet, ready for the moment a shot comes through the side. On this voyage, it never does. Nobody thanks you for that either. That is the job: when you do it well, nothing happens.

Then comes careening. As our earlier episodes showed, crews had to haul a ship onto her side on a hidden beach and scrape away weed, barnacles and the worms that bore into the wood.

Everyone scrapes. Only you decide which planks are still sound. When you find a soft one, the whole crew looks at you as if you put the worm there yourself.

Being the man with the saw has a darker side. If there is no surgeon aboard, the crew starts looking at you whenever someone is badly hurt.

In seventeen twenty-four, a Boston court heard that one of John Phillips's pirates, William Phillips, had lost a leg. Johnson's book alone claims who took it off. There was no surgeon aboard, so the crew chose the carpenter as "the most proper man." Johnson says the carpenter did it with the tools of his trade, and that, against the odds, the man survived.

The court records add a strange detail. When a captured fisherman offered to take William Phillips home, he refused. If he went, he said, they would hang him.

Captain John Phillips himself, Johnson says, had been bred a carpenter. Pirates had taken him from a ship bound for Newfoundland, and he soon took to the life. A Newfoundland history article agrees: Phillips was a ship's carpenter by trade.

On your sloop, at least, the surgeon is right there, still in his leather apron. You consider him the best thing this crew ever stole.

Carpenters were valued, but valued is not the same as free.

Only Johnson's book prints the articles of John Phillips's crew. They gave the master, the carpenter, the boatswain and the gunner one share and a quarter each, almost as much as the captain's one and a half. The same articles said that any man who tried to run away would be marooned.

Johnson says Phillips's carpenter, a man named Fern, was offended when the new arrival John Rose Archer was made quartermaster over the older hands. You may remember Archer from the quartermaster episode.

Fern tried to sail off with a captured prize, and failed. Later he tried again, and Johnson says Phillips killed him for it, according to their articles. A deposition in the Boston court files confirms that a man named Ferne had been shot by Captain Phillips.

Phillips needed a new carpenter. In March seventeen twenty-four, his crew took one from a ship bound from Virginia to London: Edward Cheeseman. The indictment in Boston says plainly that the pirates forced him.

Johnson calls Cheeseman "a modest sober young man, very averse to their unlawful practice." The man ordered to row him to the pirate ship was a captured fisherman, John Fillmore. Johnson says Fillmore saw how unwilling Cheeseman was, and offered to help him overthrow the pirates.

In April seventeen twenty-four, they did. The captives rose up. According to the Boston News-Letter, Cheeseman threw the pirates' sailing master overboard, and the crew was overpowered within minutes.

In May, Cheeseman and Fillmore stood trial in Boston. The court found them not guilty. The carpenter the pirates had forced aboard was the carpenter who ended their voyage.

History has a sense of humor about Fillmore, too. The historian J. Franklin Jameson, who published the Boston court records, notes that John Fillmore was the great-grandfather of Millard Fillmore, a future President of the United States. The fisherman who rowed the pirates' new carpenter across ended up in the family tree of a president.

Thomas Davis, the Whydah survivor, had been tried in Boston too, in October seventeen seventeen. He told the court he had asked again and again to be released. He was found not guilty.

That is the uncomfortable thing about your story. The courts were kindest to carpenters who could show they never wanted to be there. You came willingly. Nobody has your name written down as a forced man.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You read the reward list, and for the first time in this series, the job is on it. As the London Gazette printed it, a pirate commander was worth one hundred pounds, and "every lieutenant, master, boatswain, carpenter, and gunner" forty pounds.

The quartermaster was not on that list. The surgeon was not on it. You are. Forty pounds. Twice what Tom is worth. You were hoping for a different kind of recognition.

The crew votes on the pardon. You came willingly, so you have a vote. You raise your hand for the pardon. You know exactly how much a ship can take before she sinks, and you think this crew is close.

The pardon wins, narrowly. Before you go ashore, you walk the hold one last time and check the seams by lantern light. Tom asks why you bother. You tell him that somebody will sail this sloop next, and the sea does not care who.

Ashore, the waterfront is full of boats that need mending, and nobody asks a carpenter too many questions while he is fixing their hull. You find work within a day. The pay is worse. Nobody threatens to shoot you if you ask to leave. You decide that is a fair trade.

Not everyone made it. Johnson writes that the greatest part of the pirates who surrendered soon returned to the trade, and the historian who edited Woodes Rogers's voyage agrees that many resumed it. A carpenter can always find a ship. Choosing which one was always the hard part.

Tom comes by now and then with a bucket, out of habit. You let him bail out whatever you are working on. Some partnerships are bigger than piracy.

So why was being a pirate carpenter worse than you think? Because the crew needed you too much to ever let you go. Because you were the plumber, the shipwright and sometimes the surgeon. Because you robbed other carpenters of the tools that kept their ships afloat.

And because a court would ask the same question the crew never did: did you come willingly? Some carpenters who could prove they were forced went home. The ones who came willingly had a price on the king's list.
