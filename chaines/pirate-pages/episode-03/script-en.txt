You are the man who took the captain's coat. You held out your hand, he handed it over, and it went into the common chest. Then you ran the auction where he bought it back. He has almost forgiven you.

Two episodes ago, you were Tom, holding a bucket. Last time, you were the captain, losing that coat. This time, you are the quartermaster: the steady man who signed Tom aboard, and the one the crew brings its complaints to.

Imagine you are an experienced seaman on the same modest Caribbean sloop, around seventeen seventeen. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. A quartermaster helped organize robbery, then kept the accounts. The sailors on the ships you board are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

Your story starts with a vote. When the crew removed the last captain, every office went back to a show of hands, including yours. On a pirate ship, a job was a loan from the majority.

Our old friend Captain Charles Johnson, whose General History of the Pyrates appeared in seventeen twenty-four, describes the quartermaster as a principal officer "of the men's own choosing." Johnson is a pen name, and his book mixes fact with invention. So we will check him against other witnesses, and when a story rests on that one book from seventeen twenty-four alone, we will say so.

One of those witnesses is William Snelgrave, a slave-trading captain held by pirates in seventeen nineteen. Crews, he wrote, chose a captain to fight, and then chose "another principal officer, whom they call quartermaster."

Court records agree. In Boston in seventeen twenty-four, a captured quartermaster named John Rose Archer was asked how he got the job. The company, he said, "thought him the fittest man for a quartermaster, and so chose him."

The economist Peter Leeson calls this a system of checks and balances. The captain had absolute command in a fight. The quartermaster held much of everything else: provisions, plunder, quarrels and punishment.

Johnson calls the quartermaster "a sort of civil magistrate." In plain words, you are the crew's treasurer, judge, storekeeper and complaints office, all in one waistcoat.

The vote goes your way. The new captain shakes your hand, a little stiffly. He has just remembered that the hands that elected him can also remove him. Then he notices they are the same hands that elected you.

A new captain means new articles, and the articles are your department. Leeson notes that crews agreed on their articles before a voyage, at the same time as they elected their officers, and that every man had to consent.

Then everyone swears to them. Johnson says Bartholomew Roberts's crew kept a Bible reserved for that purpose only. But only that one book from seventeen twenty-four claims that John Phillips's crew, lacking a Bible, swore "upon a hatchet."

Your crew owns one Bible, two hatchets and very strong opinions. The Bible wins the vote, narrowly.

Then you read out the clauses that make you the most watched man aboard. In the articles Johnson printed for Roberts's crew, the quartermaster received two shares of a prize, exactly like the captain.

Another clause called every man "fairly in turn, by list" to board prizes, because boarders were allowed a change of clothes. On your sloop, guess who keeps the list.

The same articles banned gambling for money. Lights and candles went out at eight in the evening, and anyone still thirsty had to drink on the open deck. Every man kept his pistols and cutlass clean. Anyone who cheated the company of a dollar could be marooned.

Phillips's articles worried about fire. No smoking in the hold without a cap on your pipe, and no candle without a lantern. A wooden ship carrying gunpowder does not forgive careless smokers.

Only Johnson's book prints the articles of George Lowther's crew, and they include a rule that might as well carry your name. Any gold, jewels or silver found on a prize, worth a piece of eight, had to be handed to the quartermaster within twenty-four hours.

So now every man aboard assumes you are hiding something. Being trusted with everything is the fastest way to be suspected of everything.

Leeson points out that the articles fenced you in too. Written shares and written punishments limited how far you could favor friends. And when an article's meaning was in doubt, Johnson says, a jury of the crew decided. Even the judge had a judge.

Then a sail appears. During the chase, the captain commands absolutely, as the crew's own rules allow. You check the boat, the oars and the men. Your power begins later, and it begins in the most dangerous place on the water.

Snelgrave wrote that the quartermaster was "to be the first man in boarding any ship they shall attack, or go in the boat on any desperate enterprise." Johnson agrees: the quartermaster was first on board any prize.

Pirates hoped a crew would surrender before anyone climbed aboard. Leeson describes a deliberate bargain: mercy for crews that gave up, and something much worse for those that fought.

Real trial records show quartermasters in front. In January seventeen twenty-two, off West Africa, Roberts's quartermaster, William Magnes, commanded the boat that took the King Solomon, a Royal African Company ship.

Witnesses said Magnes ordered her provisions and stores out. The boatswain went straight for two new cables and coils of rope, and beat the merchant sailors for not working fast enough. Robbery, it turns out, is mostly inventory, with threats.

Your own boarding is the merchant sloop from last time. You go over the rail first, with a pistol in your belt that you very much hope stays there. Their crew has already surrendered. A frightened young sailor hands you the ship's papers with both hands.

Surrender spared lives, but it did not make the afternoon gentle. Snelgrave and the victims who testified in seventeen twenty-two describe threats, blows and cutlasses waved in faces. The people on that deck are afraid of you, and they are right to be.

Then you do what Johnson says quartermasters did. You separate what the company will keep, and you return what you think fit to the owners. Gold and silver are not returnable. Your crew voted on that long ago.

The legend imagines a treasure hunter. The reality is a nervous warehouse manager on a very small boat. Leeson notes there was rarely room aboard a pirate ship to take everything a prize carried.

You take flour, rum, rope, sailcloth and a few bolts of cloth. You leave the merchant his salt fish and enough bread to reach a harbor. You call that returning what you think fit. He calls it something else.

Real crews could be wasteful. When pirates decided to keep Snelgrave's own ship, he watched them throw bales of woollen goods and cases of Indian cloth into the sea to clear her. Witnesses at the seventeen twenty-two trial described Roberts's quartermasters ordering a captured ship's goods thrown overboard too.

Money and necessities, Snelgrave wrote, were what they chiefly wanted. Plunder that would not fit simply stopped existing.

Whatever cannot be split goes into the chest, to be "sold at the mast," as Snelgrave put it. Leeson explains that crews auctioned awkward items and shared the money. That is how the captain bought back his coat. You ran the auction and kept a straight face, which was the hardest part.

The rest has to become money ashore. Royal Museums Greenwich notes that stolen goods were sold in nearby islands and European colonies. Johnson describes colonial officials sharing plunder and trading with pirates. Your dealer's prices drop the moment he sees where your cloth came from.

And some prizes carried people the law treated as cargo. Royal Museums Greenwich says pirates rarely freed enslaved people. They often treated them as badly as their captors did, and usually sold them for profit once they reached land.

At Whydah, on the West African coast, in January seventeen twenty-two, Roberts's crew demanded ransoms from the ships at anchor. Captain Chaloner Ogle of the Royal Navy reported that one English ship, whose captain refused to pay, was burned with "a considerable number" of enslaved Africans aboard. Johnson's book alone puts the number at about eighty.

No column in a quartermaster's book tells the truth about that. The people aboard those ships were never anyone's share.

Back at sea, a quarrel arrives on schedule. Your crewmate says Tom pocketed a silver buckle from the prize instead of handing it in. Tom says he found it in his own shoe. Neither story improves as it gets louder.

This is your court. Johnson says the quartermaster punished small offences not covered by the articles: men who were quarrelsome, who misused prisoners, who plundered beyond his orders, or who neglected their weapons.

He punished them, Johnson says, with "drubbing or whipping," and anyone else who tried it risked a lashing from the whole company. You are the only man aboard allowed to hand out a beating. That is not a privilege. It is a target.

The articles set limits. Roberts's crew banned striking one another on board. Phillips's articles punished a blow with "Moses's law": forty stripes lacking one, on the bare back.

So when your crewmate shoves Tom, the case becomes more serious than a buckle.

Roberts's articles said quarrels had to be ended on shore, at sword and pistol. Johnson describes the routine. The quartermaster first tried to reconcile the two men. If that failed, he took them ashore, stood them back to back, and gave the word to turn and fire.

If both pistols missed, Johnson adds, they took up cutlasses, and the first man to draw blood was declared the winner. You would very much prefer the reconciliation. A duel costs you one man for certain, and possibly two.

So you do what the job mostly is. You listen. The buckle, it turns out, belongs to the merchant's mate. It goes into the chest, which is not the same as going home. Tom apologizes for his shoe. Your crewmate apologizes for his hands. You write both apologies in the book, because next week they will remember them differently.

Some quarrels broke whole companies apart. Edward Low's quartermaster, Francis Spriggs, was given command of a captured vessel, and after bad blood with Low he sailed off with one of the ships and became a captain himself.

Only Johnson's book explains the quarrel, and it claims it was about justice. Spriggs wanted a crewman hanged for killing a man in cold blood, and Low did not. A quartermaster's verdict could decide more than a buckle. It could decide who stayed.

A few weeks later, your crew takes a second sloop, newer and faster than your own. The vote takes about four seconds. Keep her.

Real crews upgraded whenever they could. Bartholomew Roberts's flagship, the Royal Fortune, had been the Onslow, a merchant ship his crew captured. Blackbeard kept the French slave ship La Concorde and gave her captain a small sloop instead.

At Whydah, Ogle reported, Roberts's men even carried off a fast French ship after her ransom had been paid, planning to fit her out for themselves and quit one of their own.

Two vessels need two commanders. The captain moves to the new sloop. The crew votes you command of the old one, with eight men and a pump that complains louder than they do.

There were real precedents. When Charles Vane's crew voted him out, his quartermaster, John Rackham, became captain. And Low gave his quartermaster, Spriggs, a captured vessel to command before they parted.

For one week, you are captain and quartermaster at once. You give the orders, and then you receive the complaints about the orders. Your eight men elect one of themselves to keep an eye on you. It is Tom. He takes it very seriously.

Splitting a company had a cost. In February seventeen twenty-two, one of Roberts's ships, the Ranger, left Cape Lopez alone to chase what her crew took for a merchant ship. It was the warship Swallow. The Ranger was taken first. Five days later, the Swallow caught Roberts too.

Your own week ends quietly. You rejoin the captain and hand back the command. You discover you enjoyed it slightly too much. Somewhere aboard, someone is already discussing that.

Then the dealer brings the news you heard last time: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You read the reward list carefully, because reading carefully is your job. As the London Gazette printed it, catching a pirate commander was worth one hundred pounds. Every lieutenant, master, boatswain, carpenter and gunner was worth forty. Inferior officers were thirty, and private men twenty.

Quartermasters are not on the list. The officer who ran a pirate ship's money and discipline did not get a line of his own. You are not sure whether to feel insulted or relieved.

Relief would be a mistake. Courts did not need a price list to understand what a quartermaster was.

In the spring of seventeen twenty-two, Roberts's captured men were tried at Cape Coast Castle, on the West African coast. Prisoners said they had only obeyed their captain and their quartermaster. The court answered that orders from "officers of your own election" were still their own acts.

The vote that made you could also convict your voters.

One prisoner protested that he had never voted for the new quartermaster, William Magnes. He had voted for David Simpson, the old quartermaster, because Magnes was "too honest a man for the business."

It did not help Magnes. He and Simpson, the new quartermaster and the old one, were among the first six men hanged outside Cape Coast Castle.

In all, fifty-two of Roberts's men were hanged. Twenty were sentenced to seven years of forced labour on the West African coast. More than seventy were acquitted, most of them men who had been forced aboard.

And around seventy Black men captured with Roberts's crews were never tried at all. They were sold into slavery.

Two years later came John Rose Archer, the man his company called the fittest for the job. Witnesses at his Boston trial said that once he became quartermaster of John Phillips's crew, he went armed aboard every vessel they took.

That career ended in April seventeen twenty-four, when captives aboard rose up and overpowered the crew. Johnson's book alone claims Archer was spared that day because he held all the plunder, which made him useful evidence.

He was hanged in Boston that June, beside a young shipmate. Afterwards, the shipmate was buried. The quartermaster's body was hung in chains on an island in the harbor, as the Boston Gazette put it, "to be a spectacle, and so a warning to others."

There was one late mercy for some. In December seventeen eighteen, a new proclamation extended the pardon to pirates who surrendered by the first of July, seventeen nineteen, because some had not heard of the first one in time.

The pardon got a second deadline. The gallows never needed one.

Which brings you back to the deck, where we left the crew last time with their hands in the air. Pardon, or keep sailing? The captain speaks first. Then Tom. Then everybody else, at length.

Counting is your job. You count once. You count again, because whatever number you say, somebody will call it fixed.

The pardon wins, narrowly. A few of the losing hands stay up a moment too long, as if the count might change its mind.

Your last duty is the common chest. You divide it share by share, in front of everyone, the way you were elected to do. The captain gets two shares. You get two shares. Tom checks your arithmetic twice.

And history keeps its own count. Many who took the pardon went back to sea. Quartermasters who stayed in the trade went on boarding first and holding the plunder, which was exactly the record a court would read aloud.

So why was being a pirate quartermaster worse than you think? Because you went first into every boat. Because you held everyone's money and nobody's trust for long. Because you judged your friends, and then a court judged you by the votes they gave you.

And because the fairness you guarded so carefully stopped at your own rail. The sailors you robbed had no vote. The enslaved people aboard those prizes were never anyone's share. A pardon could forgive a crime against the king. It could not give back what was taken.
