You finally own the magnificent coat. You bought it at an auction run by your own crew, with most of your own share, after a vote you were not allowed to skip. Congratulations, Captain. The coat is the best part of the job.

Last time, you were Tom, scrubbing below deck and looking up at the captain's boots. This time, you are wearing those boots. From down there, the job looked like freedom with a hat. From up here, it looks like everybody's complaints with a hat.

Imagine you are an experienced seaman on the same modest Caribbean sloop, around seventeen seventeen, newly elected captain. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts and museum research about real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. A pirate captain organized robbery at sea. The people on the ships you chase are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

Tom is still aboard, still attached to his bucket. He raised his hand for you in the vote, which is touching, until you remember that he also raised it to remove the last captain.

The last captain was not overthrown in a sword fight. He was voted out. A heavily armed ship appeared offshore, he chose caution, and the crew decided that caution looked suspiciously like cowardice.

That was not unusual. The economist Peter Leeson, in a peer-reviewed study of pirate governance, finds numerous examples of crews deposing captains by majority vote.

In November seventeen eighteen, Charles Vane declined to attack a French warship. The next day, his crew voted him out and branded him a coward.

Our main source for that story is Captain Charles Johnson's General History of the Pyrates, published in seventeen twenty-four. It needs a warning label. Johnson is a pen name, the author is still unidentified, and parts of the book are exaggerated.

Johnson claims Blackbeard had fourteen wives, for example; Royal Museums Greenwich notes that the records show one legal marriage. Historians still use the book, carefully, alongside court records and eyewitnesses. So will we.

Vane's replacement was his own quartermaster, John Rackham. That detail should worry you. Crews could promote the officer they trusted most, which means your successor may already be standing beside you, helpfully checking the water barrels.

Your quartermaster is the same steady man who signed Tom aboard. You get along. You also notice that when the crew has a complaint, they do not bring it to you. They bring it to him.

So why did they choose you? Johnson says crews wanted a captain "superior for knowledge and boldness, pistol proof, as they call it." In plain words: someone who can navigate and will not panic. Nobody asked about your management style.

Elections could be quick. Johnson and the Canadian Encyclopedia both note that Bartholomew Roberts had been with his crew for only about six weeks when they made him captain. Your own campaign lasted one afternoon and one argument about the weather.

Then you read the job description. Johnson records that a pirate captain's power was "absolute and uncontrollable" in three situations: "fighting, chasing, or being chased." Otherwise, "in all other matters whatsoever, he is governed by a majority."

So you are a commander for the length of a chase. The rest of the time, you are a man in a good coat making suggestions.

Where to sail, when to rest, how to share the food and whom to pursue can all end in a show of hands. Your orders are more like opening statements.

William Snelgrave, the captain of a slave-trading ship, was captured by pirates on the West African coast and saw this system up close. The captain, he wrote, is "chiefly chosen to fight."

The quartermaster, Snelgrave added, "has the general inspection of all affairs, and often controls the captain's orders." You read that sentence twice. It does not improve.

Johnson describes the quartermaster as the men's own choice. He punished small offenses, settled quarrels, kept the common stores, and was first aboard any captured ship, deciding what the company would keep. On your sloop, he also decides who gets the last biscuit.

Then there is your cabin. Johnson says crews gave the captain the great cabin, after which every man, "as the humour takes him," could walk in, use his silver and china, swear at him, and help himself to his food and drink.

Your sloop is far too small for a great cabin. You have a cramped corner at the stern that everyone politely calls yours. They visit it constantly. The swearing fits in perfectly.

Snelgrave noticed that on one pirate ship everybody slept rough on deck, "the captain himself not being allowed a bed." You had imagined a carved four-poster. You receive a patch of damp planks with an excellent view of Tom's feet.

Next comes money. In the articles Johnson printed for Bartholomew Roberts's crew, the captain and the quartermaster each received two shares of a prize. The master, boatswain and gunner received one and a half, other officers one and a quarter, and everyone else one.

Two shares sounds wonderful until you remember what one share of a modest prize looks like. You have doubled a disappointment. Leeson concludes that a pirate captain's lodging, food and even pay were nearly the same as an ordinary crewman's.

Roberts's articles held other surprises. Every man had a vote in affairs of moment. Every man had equal title to fresh food and strong liquor, unless a scarcity, which Johnson calls "no uncommon thing among them," made the company vote to cut rations.

Another clause said nobody could even talk of breaking up their way of living until each man had shared a thousand pounds. That is an ambitious retirement plan for a crew that mostly captures cloth.

The musicians, meanwhile, were promised rest on the Sabbath. Any other day required special favor.

Johnson admits these were only some of Roberts's articles, and no two crews wrote exactly the same rules. Your imagined company has its own agreement, but the spirit is familiar: the captain leads, and the crew keeps the receipts.

Your first real test is not a battle. It is water. The barrels are low, the biscuit is tired, and the crew votes on where to find more.

You suggest a quiet bay with a stream. Someone suggests a busy trade route. Thirst is persuasive, but treasure campaigns better.

A pirate captain could not sail into most ports and order supplies with a polite letter. Food, rope, sailcloth and timber came from captured ships, or from harbors and traders willing to deal with pirates, for a price and for a while.

You try the waterfront dealer from Tom's last visit. His prices have climbed, and so has his curiosity about your plans.

Then there is the hull. Below the waterline, weed and barnacles grow on every wooden ship, and Royal Museums Greenwich notes that this growth could severely affect a sailing ship's performance. Britannica and the Smithsonian add another guest: shipworms, which bore into wooden hulls.

For a pirate, speed is not a luxury. Johnson calls a clean, quick sloop "a light pair of heels," useful "either to take, or escape being taken."

So you must careen: find a hidden beach, unload the guns and stores, haul the ship onto her side, and scrape.

Careening takes days. For that time, your armed vessel is a helpless wooden whale lying on the sand, and your crew camps in tents beside everything it owns. Johnson describes crews being surprised in exactly that position.

Stede Bonnet's whole career is a lesson for captains. A wealthy Barbados planter, he bought his own sloop and, unusually, paid his crew himself, according to both Johnson and North Carolina's Dictionary of Biography.

Owning the ship did not keep him in charge. When Bonnet met Blackbeard, as Royal Museums Greenwich and the North Carolina dictionary both describe, Blackbeard put one of his own men in command of Bonnet's sloop.

Bonnet himself stayed aboard Blackbeard's ship, somewhere between a guest and a prisoner. It is a very specific way to lose a job you paid for.

In seventeen eighteen, Bonnet took a royal pardon, soon went back to piracy, and found his sloop leaking badly. He took her into the Cape Fear River to repair and scrape her hull.

Released prisoners spread the news. Colonel William Rhett arrived with armed sloops from South Carolina. Bonnet surrendered and was hanged in Charleston that December.

Your own careening goes well, mostly because nothing happens. You scrape beside Tom for three long days. He seems deeply suspicious of a captain holding a scraper. You are deeply suspicious of anything that might be a sail.

Back at sea, you hunt, which mostly means waiting. Days pass. The crew counts biscuits, then counts your decisions. A captain without prizes is still technically employed, but his contract is renewed by show of hands.

Then a sail appears. For the length of a chase, you finally have absolute power. You order the sails trimmed and the course changed, and for one glorious hour nobody votes on anything.

The merchant sloop surrenders without a fight. That is the best outcome for everyone's survival, but it is still robbery. The sailors watching your crew climb aboard did nothing to deserve this afternoon.

The quartermaster goes aboard first, just as Johnson and Snelgrave describe. He decides what the company takes. Your absolute power expired the moment the other crew gave up, and you are back to standing on deck in a good coat.

Most of the prize is flour, rum and cloth. And one magnificent coat. You try it on. It fits. For one beautiful minute, you look exactly like the poster version of yourself.

Snelgrave watched this moment happen for real. Several pirate captains took fine clothes from the plunder without the quartermaster's leave. The crew took "great offence," fearing that their captains would start to "take whatever they liked for themselves."

The next morning, Snelgrave writes, the coats were taken back and put into the common chest, "to be sold at the mast." Leeson treats the episode as evidence of how tightly crews limited their captains.

Your quartermaster has never read Snelgrave. He does not need to. He holds out his hand. The coat goes into the chest, then up for auction.

You outbid two crewmates and, for some reason, Tom. You did not seize the coat. You bought it back from the voters.

The captured sailors watch all this from their own deck. Some may be invited to join, some pressured. Whatever your crew decides, they do not get a vote. The fairness aboard a pirate ship stopped at its own rail.

Enslaved people stood even further outside it. Royal Museums Greenwich says that, despite some stories of pirates freeing enslaved people, this rarely happened, and pirates often treated enslaved people as badly as their captors did.

Blackbeard's famous flagship, Queen Anne's Revenge, began as La Concorde, a French slave ship taken in November seventeen seventeen.

According to the Smithsonian and North Carolina's Department of Natural and Cultural Resources, most of the captives aboard were carried on to Martinique to be sold. A change of flag was not freedom.

Some men of African descent did serve as pirate crew. But how pirates decided who counted as crew and who was treated as cargo, the Smithsonian notes, remains unclear. Your crew talks a great deal about fairness. History asks who was never allowed into that conversation.

Back aboard your sloop, morale is excellent for exactly as long as the rum lasts. Then someone asks loudly why the captain always chooses the slow route. Someone else asks why the coat sold so cheaply.

You begin to understand how quickly a vote can become a verdict.

Removal was not always polite. Snelgrave heard that Christopher Moody's crew forced him and twelve others into an open boat, and they were never heard of again.

One pirate's advice, according to Johnson, was simple: if a captain ever exceeded his limits, "why, down with him!"

So you do what sensible pirate captains did: you consult. About the course, the rations, the weather and the dinner. You become the most democratic man in the Caribbean, mainly out of a strong preference for not being placed in an open boat.

Then the dealer brings news from the harbor. On the fifth of September, seventeen seventeen, King George the First issued a proclamation against pirates.

Any pirate who surrendered to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, would be pardoned for piracies committed before the fifth of January, seventeen eighteen.

Johnson prints the proclamation in full, and the Calendar of State Papers records the planning behind it. After the deadline, it promised rewards for capturing those who refused.

One hundred pounds for a commander. Forty for officers such as the master or gunner. Thirty for lesser officers. And twenty for an ordinary man.

There was one more clause. Any pirate who seized his own commander and delivered him to justice would receive two hundred pounds. The State Papers sum it up as "double the reward."

You do the arithmetic, and so does Tom. Tom is worth twenty pounds. You are worth one hundred. If Tom hands you over himself, he earns two hundred. Your promotion made you five times more valuable, and the money goes to everybody except you.

Tom notices you noticing. He returns to his bucket with great dignity. You decide to sleep facing the hatch.

At Nassau, the pirates' favorite base, the news caused uproar. The London Gazette reported that the proclamation was received "with great demonstration of joy and thankfulness." Johnson describes a pirate council so noisy that nothing could be agreed. Both may be true.

Some captains surrendered, including Benjamin Hornigold. In the summer of seventeen eighteen, Woodes Rogers arrived as governor, with the pardon and naval escorts. Most of the pirates there accepted.

Charles Vane did not. He set fire to a captured ship, sent it toward the warships, and escaped in the confusion.

The pardon was not a magic door. Johnson says many who took it soon returned to the trade. Blackbeard accepted a pardon from North Carolina's governor and did not stay retired.

At Nassau, when some pardoned men went back to robbery, Rogers had them tried and hanged in front of their old companions.

Meanwhile, the hunters were improving. On the twenty-second of November, seventeen eighteen, an expedition sent by Virginia's governor and led by Lieutenant Robert Maynard caught Blackbeard at Ocracoke Inlet. Blackbeard did not survive the fight.

Bartholomew Roberts, Royal Museums Greenwich says, took more than four hundred vessels in about three years. On the tenth of February, seventeen twenty-two, the warship Swallow, under Captain Chaloner Ogle, caught him off West Africa. Roberts was killed in the battle.

Capture did not mean a long voyage to London. Admiralty courts sat in the colonies and trading forts, close to where pirates were caught.

After Roberts's death, fifty-two of his men were hanged following a trial at Cape Coast Castle, on the West African coast. Vane, too, was eventually caught and hanged in Jamaica.

The famous careers were short. Royal Museums Greenwich says people who entered piracy usually did so only for a short period. Roberts lasted about three years. Blackbeard's career as a pirate leader lasted only a few years.

Johnson gives Roberts a motto: "A merry life and a short one." Whatever else in that book was exaggerated, the short part was reliable.

Now comes the vote. Before the deadline, your crew gathers on deck to decide: take the pardon, or keep sailing. As captain, you get to speak first. Then the hands go up. You have one vote. So does Tom.

The quartermaster counts slowly. The real crews of seventeen eighteen split every possible way: some surrendered, some went back, some never had a real choice. Your imagined count can wait. Whatever it says, you will hear it at the same moment as everybody else.

So why was being a pirate captain worse than you think? Because your power lasted as long as a chase, your cabin belonged to everyone, and your share was two pieces of not very much.

Because your crew could vote you out, and your own king priced you at five times your friends. But the coat is yours. You paid for it. Twice, if you count the job.
