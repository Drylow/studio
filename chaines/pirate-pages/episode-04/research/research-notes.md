# Pirate Pages — episode 04 research notes

**Title:** Why Being a Pirate Surgeon Was Worse Than You Think
**Script:** `chaines/pirate-pages/episode-04/script-en.txt`: 2,752 words (`wc -w`), 73 paragraphs, about 19:50 at episode 1's measured voice pace (2,744 words = 1,185 s). Measure the real duration on the final voice.
**Setting:** An imagined ship's surgeon ("you"), travelling as a passenger on the merchant sloop that Tom's crew took in episodes 2 and 3, Caribbean, around 1717 to 1718. The crew is the same as in episodes 1 to 3. The surgeon is a NEW character: a thin, clean-shaven man of about forty, gray hair tied back with a black ribbon, plain black coat over a white shirt, long brown leather apron, wooden medicine chest with brass corners (described in §3 so the images can draw him). The capture, the refusal to sign, the quarter share, the crewmate's rope burn, the chase spent below deck, the pardon vote without a vote and the returned chest are fictional devices; the narration says so in §3. Real events (Snelgrave 1719, La Concorde 1717, Charleston 1718, the Queen Anne's Revenge wreck, Cape Coast Castle 1722) are narrator asides, not events in the fictional present.

## How sources were weighed

- **Johnson, *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724)** was read in the full Project Gutenberg text and searched locally. "Captain Charles Johnson" is a pen name and the book mixes fact with invention (episode 2 and 3 notes: LEE fn. 6, MHS). Where a claim rests on Johnson alone, the narration says so ("Johnson's book alone", "Johnson's version adds"). The digits "1724" are spelled out for the voice.
- **The 1723 trial pamphlet (TRY)** is the printed record of the Cape Coast Castle Admiralty court, read in the archive.org OCR text. Johnson's Roberts chapter abridges the same record. They are two printings, not two independent investigations; this is flagged where it matters, and wording found only in Johnson's printing is narrated as "Johnson's version".
- **Snelgrave (SNE)** is an eyewitness held by pirates at the Sierra Leone River in 1719, and a slave-trading captain; the script says so.
- **Exquemelin (EXQ)** served the buccaneers of the 1660s and 1670s. His book (Dutch, 1678) is read in the 1684 English translation reprinted as *The Pirates of Panama* (Gutenberg 26690) and checked against Leeson's quotation of the 2000 Dover translation. The two translations agree on every figure used.
- **Queen Anne's Revenge archaeology**: Linda Carnes-McNaughton presented the medical finds at the Society for Historical Archaeology meeting in January 2015. Live Science reported it (republished by NBC News and Fox News); *Archaeology* magazine reported it separately twice. Claims found only in the Live Science report (French maker's marks; the French surgeons supplying their own instruments; their names) were **cut**.
- **Leeson (LEE)** is a peer-reviewed economics paper; it relies on Johnson and Exquemelin, so it confirms what those books say, not what happened.
- Quotations are spoken with modernized spelling ("Pyrates" becomes "pirates", "proportionably" is kept as printed). The wording is otherwise unchanged.

### Fictional elements (not historical claims)

The surgeon himself, his post on another island, his capture from the merchant sloop, the request to the young sailor, the unsigned articles and the word in the quartermaster's book, the extra quarter share, the contents of his chest, the crewmate's rope burn and the "assessment" scene, Tom's question about fingers, the chase spent below deck, the captain's fever, the pardon vote he cannot join, and the silver needle kept back. None is presented as a real event. Who assessed injuries aboard real pirate ships is not stated in the sources; the narration limits it to "back on your sloop".

## Sensitivity checks done

- Violence stays off screen. No wound is described beyond "a rope burn", "blistered palms" and "cut on the jaw". The saw in the chest stays "wrapped"; the tourniquet is mentioned only as an object; no amputation is shown.
- Medical objects are drawn as plain objects (chest, jars, mortar, weights, bandages, a small pewter syringe). The syphilis treatment is stated soberly; nothing sexual is shown or implied.
- Slavery is treated gravely: Snelgrave is identified as a slave-trading captain; the captives kept from La Concorde get their own paragraph; Exquemelin's payment in enslaved people is named as showing "whom they counted as payment", with no joke near it; Scudamore's plot is narrated without making the Black men aboard instruments of a joke.
- No real person is a character. "You", the captain, the quartermaster, Tom, the crewmate, the dealer and the young merchant sailor are fictional. Snelgrave, Bleau, Carnes-McNaughton, Scudamore, Wilson, Comrie, Child, Trahern, Sharp, Wragg, Exquemelin, Leeson, Low, Roberts and Blackbeard appear only in narration.
- No parrot, peg leg, treasure map or buried-treasure gag. Every paragraph can be drawn as a flat 2D scene (merchant sloop deck, the medicine chest, the articles and a pen, a harbor seen from the sea, a wreck site with divers, artefacts on a table, a lantern-lit hold, a castle wall seen from the sea, a proclamation).
