You are the item the quartermaster left off his list. Last time, he told you what he took from the merchant sloop: flour, rum, rope, sailcloth and a few bolts of cloth. He never mentioned the passenger with the medicine chest. That passenger was you.

Three episodes ago, you were Tom. Then you were the captain, and then the quartermaster. This time, you are not one of the crew at all. You are the ship's surgeon they took, and nobody asked whether you wanted the job.

Imagine you are a surgeon of about forty, travelling as a passenger to a new post on another island, around seventeen seventeen. You wear a plain black coat over a white shirt, with a long leather apron, and your gray hair is tied back with a black ribbon. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts, archaeology and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the crew did for a living. Every wound you will treat was earned robbing someone. The sailors on the ships they board are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

Your capture takes about a minute. The quartermaster opens your wooden chest, looking for silver. He finds jars, bandages, a small saw and a row of knives, and he stops looking for silver. The captain says the one word you were hoping not to hear: keep.

Real crews did exactly this. Pirates held on to captured men whose skills they needed, and surgeons were high on that list.

In seventeen nineteen, at the Sierra Leone River in West Africa, pirates captured the slave-trading captain William Snelgrave. He later wrote that they intended to take his surgeon, James Bleau, by force.

Snelgrave pleaded for him. Bleau, he told them, grieved so much that if he did not die quickly, he would be of no use to them anyway. It did not work. Bleau was set free only because the surgeon of a French ship joined the pirates that same evening. The crew needed a surgeon. It did not much care which one.

Blackbeard's crew did the same. In November seventeen seventeen, they captured the French slave ship La Concorde. The Queen Anne's Revenge project, which excavates her wreck, writes that ten Frenchmen were forced to join the pirates, among them surgeons, carpenters and a cook. The archaeologist Linda Carnes-McNaughton says all three of the ship's surgeons were forced to stay.

The surgeons were not the only people kept against their will. The Smithsonian says the pirates kept sixty-one of the Africans held captive aboard La Concorde. The Queen Anne's Revenge project says about seventy-five, and notes that sources vary. Most of the others were handed back to the French captain, to be sold. Nobody asked those people anything, and no court would ever ask how they had come aboard.

Before your merchant sloop sails away, you ask her frightened young sailor to tell anyone who asks that you were taken by force. He nods so hard you are not sure he heard a word.

Tom carries your chest across to the pirate sloop. He carries it very carefully, the way people carry anything that contains knives.

That evening, the quartermaster brings out the articles and a pen. You read every clause, looking for the one that sends surgeons home. There isn't one.

Whether a captured surgeon signed mattered a great deal. In seventeen twenty-two, Bartholomew Roberts's captured crews were tried at Cape Coast Castle, on the West African coast. A witness told the court that one surgeon, Peter Scudamore, had signed the pirates' articles "with a great deal of alacrity," and was the first surgeon who had done so.

Our old friend Captain Charles Johnson prints his own version of that trial in his General History of the Pyrates, from seventeen twenty-four. Johnson is a pen name, and his book mixes fact with invention. His version adds that, before Scudamore, the crew had changed surgeons after they served a while, and never obliged them to sign.

Another surgeon, George Wilson, had been a passenger like you when pirates took his ship. You will meet him again. He asked a witness at his own trial to confirm that he had challenged Roberts over why surgeons were now made to sign. The witness said yes. It was not a small question. A signature turned a prisoner into a member.

So you do not sign. The captain shrugs. The quartermaster writes something in his book. You very much hope the word is "forced."

Signed or not, you will be paid. In the articles Johnson printed for Roberts's crew, the master, boatswain and gunner received one and a half shares of a prize, and other officers one and a quarter.

The trial record shows that Roberts's men even voted for a chief surgeon. Scudamore had been elected "in opposition to" two other surgeons. One of those rivals, Adam Comrie, said George Wilson had told him he hoped Comrie would win it, because "a quarter share, which they had more than others, would be worth looking after."

On your sloop, there is no election. You are the only candidate, and you never ran. You receive a quarter share more than Tom. You are a prisoner with a pay rise.

The crew does not admire you. It admires your chest.

They have good reason. A generation earlier, Alexander Exquemelin, who served the buccaneers as a barber-surgeon, described their agreements in a book first published in sixteen seventy-eight. Before any prize was divided, he wrote, the surgeon received a salary for himself and his chest of medicines, usually rated at two hundred or two hundred and fifty pieces of eight.

The carpenter, who repaired and fitted out the whole ship, got one hundred or one hundred and fifty. A box of jars was rated higher than the man who kept the ship afloat.

Medicine could even be worth a town's terror. In May seventeen eighteen, Blackbeard's squadron blockaded the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina, for about a week, seizing ships and taking hostages. The South Carolina Encyclopedia names one of them: Samuel Wragg, a member of the colony's council.

Royal Museums Greenwich says Blackbeard demanded a ransom that included a chest of expensive medicines, and threatened to kill his prisoners if he did not get it. The Smithsonian calls the medicine chest his most urgent demand.

The governor gave in. The chest went out, the prisoners were released, and the squadron sailed away. Johnson's book alone puts the chest's value at three to four hundred pounds.

Think about that. A pirate captain sat outside a colonial port with a powerful squadron, and what he wanted first was medicine. Whatever his crew was suffering from, cutlasses were not curing it.

Usually, pirates simply took medicine from the ships they robbed. At the Cape Coast trial, Captain Joseph Trahern of the King Solomon said he watched Scudamore carry off Trahern's own backgammon tables, his surgeon's main instruments, some medicines, a quilt and a bolster.

Scudamore answered that he had plundered medicine chests on orders from the company and from the chief surgeon of the day. Johnson's version adds that, by the pirates' own laws, the chief surgeon always gave the orders in that department, and that another surgeon had carried off a whole French medicine chest the same way.

Pirate surgeons, it turns out, were also pirates. They simply had their own department.

We know what a pirate surgeon worked with, because one surgeon's kit went to the bottom of the sea. In June seventeen eighteen, Blackbeard's flagship, the Queen Anne's Revenge, which had been La Concorde, ran aground at Beaufort Inlet, North Carolina. Centuries later, archaeologists began bringing her back up, piece by piece.

Among the finds, Carnes-McNaughton identified a surgeon's working tools: a brass mortar and pestle, two sets of nesting weights for measuring out doses, a silver needle and the remains of scissors.

There were two pump clysters, used to give enemas. There was a porringer, a small bowl that may have been used for bloodletting. And two pairs of brass set screws may have belonged to a tourniquet, used during amputations.

Then there is the syringe. It is a pewter urethral syringe, and chemical analysis found traces of mercury inside it. In the pirates' day, mercury was a treatment for syphilis.

"Eventually the mercury kills you," Carnes-McNaughton told Live Science. That was the medicine of the time: a cure that could poison the patient, given by a man who had been kidnapped to give it.

Remember who was aboard that ship. Three surgeons who had never asked to be pirates, treating the men who had taken them.

So when your own crew calls it "our" chest, you know whose chest it was. The quartermaster has already written yours into the common stock, between the rope and the rum.

Inside it are a mortar, a few jars, bandages, a saw you hope to keep wrapped, and a bottle of mercury you trust a little less every day.

Your first patient is the crewmate. A rope ran through his hand while he was hauling, and the palm is badly burned. It is not a battle wound, but injuries like it ended careers at sea. You clean it, bandage it, and tell him to keep it dry. On a ship.

Then he asks what it is worth.

Pirate articles answered that question with a price list. In Roberts's articles, as Johnson printed them, any man who lost a limb or became a cripple in the company's service received eight hundred dollars out of the public stock, "and for lesser hurts, proportionably."

Exquemelin's buccaneers were more exact. Six hundred pieces of eight for a right arm. Five hundred for a left arm or a right leg. Four hundred for a left leg. One hundred for an eye, and the same for a finger.

The economist Peter Leeson calls this social insurance. The money came out of the common stock before any shares were counted, and the articles even priced a right arm differently from a left.

The same buccaneer agreements offered a second way to pay: six enslaved people for a right arm, five for a left. That line is not a curiosity. It shows exactly whom the buccaneers counted as people, and whom they counted as payment.

Back on your sloop, you discover the part of the job nobody mentioned. Somebody has to say how bad a wound really is. The whole crew turns and looks at the man in the leather apron.

You tell the crewmate his hand will heal completely. He takes it as a personal insult. Eight hundred dollars was a much better diagnosis.

Tom asks, very casually, what a finger is worth. You tell him to keep all ten.

A few days later, a sail appears. The captain takes command for the chase, the quartermaster checks the boat, and somebody sends you below with your chest. Nobody explains why. Then you understand. If anything goes wrong up there, it will come down the ladder to you.

You lay out bandages on a sea chest by lantern light and listen to the feet above. The merchant ship surrenders without a shot. Nobody comes down the ladder. You have never been so happy to be useless.

Not every pirate surgeon was a reluctant gentleman, and not every one was sober.

Johnson's book alone tells this story about Edward Low's crew. Low was cut on the jaw by one of his own men's cutlasses. The surgeon stitched the wound while, as Johnson puts it, "tolerably drunk, as it was customary for everybody to be." When Low complained about the work, the surgeon punched him, broke open the stitches, and told him to sew it up himself.

True or not, the story shows how readers pictured a pirate surgeon. You decide to stay sober, which makes you the strangest man aboard.

George Wilson's defence at Cape Coast was strange in its own way. He asked a witness to confirm that he had called the pirates' firing at Whydah good sport, "were it not in an unlawful way," meaning the practice it gave him in his business. The witness said yes. That was his defence.

Wilson's story was tangled from the start. Roberts's crew had already taken him once, and he ended up stranded on the West African coast. At Sestos, the master of the Elizabeth, John Sharp, paid local people a ransom of three pounds five shillings in goods to free him, and took Wilson's written promise to repay it.

Then the pirates took the Elizabeth, with Wilson aboard as a passenger. When Sharp asked about the note, Wilson told him it did not matter, because he believed he would never come to England to pay it. Sharp repeated that remark in court. It did not sound like a man planning to go home.

And Peter Scudamore, the surgeon who signed so eagerly, was not finished. After Roberts's ships were taken, he was left aboard Roberts's captured flagship as she sailed for Cape Coast, and a witness said he was treated not like a prisoner, but "with all humanity."

He used that freedom to plan an escape. According to the trial record, he urged a fellow prisoner to join a rising with the Black men held aboard, take the ship, and sail down the coast to raise a new pirate company. He boasted that he knew how to navigate.

The fellow prisoner was William Child, another surgeon, who had just been acquitted. Child reported the plan to the officer in command.

So among Roberts's surgeons, one signed with enthusiasm, one protested about signing, and one turned in another. Your profession, it seems, held every kind of man that the crew did.

Courts cared about how a man came aboard. They cared even more about what he did once he was there.

Johnson's version of the Cape Coast trial puts the court's view bluntly: "it is not so material how a man comes among pirates, as how he acts when he is there."

That sentence keeps you awake. You never signed. But you have bandaged the crewmate's hand, dressed Tom's blistered palms and dosed the captain's fever. Every bandage makes the crew stronger. A prosecutor could call that help.

Scudamore's actions spoke against him: the eager signature, the plundered chests, the plot. He was sentenced to death and hanged at Cape Coast Castle.

Child was acquitted. In all, fifty-two of Roberts's men were hanged, and more than seventy were acquitted, most of them men who had been forced aboard.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You are not sure a forced man needs a pardon. You are also not sure a court would agree that you were forced. A pardon seems cheaper than an argument.

You read the reward list, too. As the London Gazette printed it, a pirate commander was worth one hundred pounds, and every lieutenant, master, boatswain, carpenter and gunner forty. Surgeons are not named at all. Neither, you remember, were quartermasters. The king's clerks clearly never needed stitches.

The crew votes on it. You never signed, so you have no vote. You hear the result with everyone else: the pardon wins, narrowly.

The quartermaster divides the common chest, share by share. Then he hands you back your medicine chest, lighter than before. The silver needle stays behind. Gold and silver, he reminds you, are not returnable. Your crew voted on that long ago.

Tom carries the chest ashore for you, as carefully as on the day you came aboard. The crewmate shows you his healed hand. He is still slightly offended.

So why was being a pirate surgeon worse than you think? Because the crew wanted your chest more than they wanted you. Because you were a prisoner with a pay rise, and your best medicine was a poison. Because your patients wanted a price list, not a cure.

And because every wound you closed helped a crew that robbed other people. A court would not ask how you came among pirates. It would ask what you did there.
