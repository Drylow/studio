You are the only man aboard who knows where the sloop is. That is exactly why nobody will ever let you leave it.

So far in this series, you have been Tom, the captain, the quartermaster, the surgeon and the carpenter. This time, you are the man the captain looks at whenever the land disappears: the sailing master, the crew's navigator.

Imagine you are a navigator of about thirty-five, aboard the same modest Caribbean sloop around seventeen seventeen. You are tall and thin, with curly black hair to your shoulders, a thin mustache and a long gray coat, and you are never far from a pair of brass dividers. Two years ago, pirates took you from a merchant ship because you could navigate. Since then, this crew has voted you its master. You, this crew and this voyage are imagined. The problems come from trial records, eyewitness accounts, archaeology and historians' research on real pirate crews.

And remember what the job was. Every course you set leads a crew of robbers toward somebody's ship. The sailors aboard those ships are not scenery, even when our story keeps the violence off screen.

On a pirate ship, "master" did not mean owner. It meant the officer who sailed her. In the articles Johnson printed for Bartholomew Roberts's crew, the master received one and a half shares of a prize, the same as the boatswain and the gunner, and less than only the captain and the quartermaster.

Our old friend Captain Charles Johnson is a pen name, and his General History of the Pyrates, from seventeen twenty-four, mixes fact with invention. But the share list matches what the economist Peter Leeson reproduces from the same articles: one and a half shares, for the man who keeps everybody from getting lost.

The crew pays you well because it cannot replace you. Pirates who wanted a navigator usually took one.

When Blackbeard's crew captured the French slave ship La Concorde in November seventeen seventeen, the Queen Anne's Revenge project says that among the ten Frenchmen forced to stay were navigators. The archaeologist Jeremy Borrelli writes that Blackbeard kept the French ship's pilot.

On Roberts's flagship, the Royal Fortune, the master was a forced man named Harry Glasby. At the trial of Roberts's crews at Cape Coast Castle in seventeen twenty-two, witness after witness described him. You will meet him again in this story. He is the closest thing your job has to a patron saint.

So you wake before dawn and go to work. At noon, you measure the height of the sun with a backstaff and work out your latitude: how far north you are.

Royal Museums Greenwich explains why that part was manageable. Latitude could be found from the Sun or the pole star. Longitude, your position east or west, depended on knowing the time at a distant reference point, and at sea, nobody could know that reliably.

The problem was so serious that in July seventeen fourteen, Parliament passed an act promising a public reward, worth thousands of pounds, to whoever could find longitude at sea. In seventeen seventeen, nobody had claimed it. That includes you.

So you guess, carefully. You throw a log line over the stern and count its knots against a sandglass to judge your speed. You read the compass for your heading. You write it all on a slate and add it up, hour after hour. Every small mistake adds up too.

Tom calls this "doing sums at the sea." He is not entirely wrong.

He asks you to teach him the compass. You show him the thirty-two points, north, north by east, north-northeast, and so on all the way round. By the time you reach south, he has forgotten north. He decides he prefers the bucket. The bucket has never asked him for a direction.

At night, when the sky is clear, you take your quadrant to the rail and find the pole star low over the bow. It is the one fixed point in your whole working life. Everything else on this sloop moves, votes or argues. The pole star just sits there, telling you how far north you are, and never asks for a share.

The captain asks you every morning where you are. You give him a position. He nods as if he checked it. Then he tells the crew, who repeat it to each other with growing confidence, until by evening it has become a fact that you never actually claimed.

We know what that work looked like, because Blackbeard's flagship carried the tools. In June seventeen eighteen, the Queen Anne's Revenge ran aground at Beaufort Inlet, North Carolina. Archaeologists later raised her navigation gear.

A twenty twenty-four study from East Carolina University lists lead sounding weights, writing slates, a sector, dividers and parts of compasses. National Geographic photographed a brass chart divider from the wreck in two thousand nine.

The Queen Anne's Revenge lab describes one of the sounding leads: it weighs twenty-one pounds, and remnants of its rope were still in the hole at the top. Several leads have a hollow in the base, filled with something sticky such as tallow or wax. When the lead hit bottom, a sample of the seabed came back up with it.

That is how you find your way when the land is invisible. You drop the lead, haul it in and look at what it brought back. Sand, shells or mud can tell an experienced navigator roughly where he is. Your crew watches you sniff a lump of tallow as if you were reading tea leaves.

Then a sail appears. During the chase, the captain commands. But you choose the angle, the tack and the moment, and when the merchant ship surrenders, the boarders take everything that floats, while you look for things that point.

Even the lookout's eyes could fail. In February seventeen twenty-two, as our quartermaster episode told, the crew of Roberts's ship Ranger chased what they took for a merchant ship off West Africa. It was the warship Swallow. A navigator can find a ship. He cannot always tell you what is on it.

Instruments were loot. Archaeologist David Moore of the North Carolina Maritime Museum told National Geographic that navigational instruments were favorite targets of looting pirates, because they could easily be sold or traded.

But taking a ship's instruments could leave her crew helpless at sea. At Cape Coast, several captains robbed by Roberts said the good treatment they got came chiefly through Glasby. Johnson's version adds that he often argued for leaving enough stores and instruments aboard the ships they robbed.

Charts could be the richest prize of all. In July sixteen eighty-one, buccaneers under Bartholomew Sharp captured the Spanish ship Rosario off what is now Ecuador and took her derrotero, a manuscript book of Spanish sailing directions and coastal charts. Royal Museums Greenwich holds a copy made "from the Spanish original" in sixteen eighty-two by the buccaneer Basil Ringrose. Back in England, Sharp was brought before the High Court of Admiralty, and the captured charts came with him.

Your own prize is smaller. So you take the merchant's spare compass and his charts. You leave his captain the quadrant. The quartermaster raises an eyebrow. You explain that a merchant who gets lost will not be robbed again, and that this is bad for business. Nobody can tell whether you are joking.

Pilots were taken too. A pilot knew one stretch of coast: its shallows, channels and reefs. Bellamy's flagship, the Whydah, carried a pilot named John Julian.

When the Whydah was wrecked off Cape Cod in April seventeen seventeen, Julian was one of only two known survivors, with the carpenter Thomas Davis from our last episode. Court records describe Julian as a Native American, and the Provincetown Independent adds that he came from the Mosquito Coast of Central America. He was not given a trial. According to History Is Now magazine, he was sold into slavery, and the Provincetown Independent says he was probably sold.

The man who knew the way through the shoals was not even given a hearing. He was treated as property.

Back on your sloop, the crew holds a vote about where to sail next. You explain the winds, the currents and the season. They vote for the route with the most ships and the fewest arguments.

On pirate ships, as Johnson describes, almost everything outside a fight could be put to a show of hands. Your knowledge is one opinion. It counts once.

Glasby knew that feeling. The captain of the King Solomon told the court that Glasby once said the pirates had made him master, "but did as they would, never observing him." Johnson's version has him complaining "how hard a condition it was, to be a chief among brutes," and saying he was weary of his life.

You are not quite that unhappy. But you have started counting the days since you last stood on land that did not move.

Then the fog comes. For two days, there is no sun, no star, nothing to measure. Your reckoning says land is somewhere ahead. Your reckoning has said that before.

You order the lead dropped every half hour. Sand. Sand. Then shells. Then the water shallows fast. You turn the sloop away, and an hour later the fog lifts on a line of white water breaking over a reef, exactly where your stomach said it would be.

Nobody thanks you. They vote to have a drink.

A week later, the water barrels are low. The crew wants a small island with a stream that the captain once visited, years ago, and remembers very fondly and very vaguely. You have no chart of it. You have his description, a latitude he swears is correct, and a great deal of open sea.

You do the one thing you can trust. You sail until the sun at noon gives you his latitude, then follow that line west, and watch the horizon for two days. On the third morning, there it is. The crew cheers the captain for remembering it so well. The captain accepts. You go below and lie down.

Real navigators did not always find their reef in time. Royal Museums Greenwich says the Queen Anne's Revenge and the sloop Adventure both ran aground entering Beaufort Inlet.

Was that an accident? Johnson claims Blackbeard grounded his flagship on purpose, to break up the company and keep the best of the plunder for himself and a few friends. In twenty twenty, the East Carolina University archaeologist Jeremy Borrelli suggested, in a preliminary study, that the grounding may have been a deliberate move to get rid of a badly leaking ship.

Either way, somebody had to steer her onto that sandbar. If it was deliberate, the navigator knew. If it was not, the navigator was blamed.

And Johnson's book alone tells what happened to Blackbeard's own master, Israel Hands. One night, Johnson says, Blackbeard fired a pistol under the table and shot Hands in the knee, laming him for life, and explained that if he did not now and then kill one of his men, they would forget who he was.

Every navigator in this story is valuable. That is not the same as being safe.

Being valuable also meant you were watched. Glasby once tried to slip away when the crew was at an island in the West Indies, with two other men. They were caught and brought back.

At Cape Coast, a witness said that Glasby and the two others had been sentenced to death by the pirates themselves for trying to escape, and that the other two were really shot for it. Glasby was spared.

Johnson's book alone tells how. His version says Glasby was suspected because he was a reserved, sober man, in a crew where refusing to get drunk looked like a plot. At the pirates' own trial, one of the judges, Valentine Ashplant, stood up, took his pipe out of his mouth and swore that Glasby should not die. When the others objected, Johnson says, Ashplant pulled out a pair of pistols, and the court changed its mind.

After that, according to a passenger's evidence in Johnson's version of the Cape Coast trial, the pirates never let Glasby off the ship, because he had once tried to escape. The navigator could take them anywhere in the world, except home.

You notice that your crew never sends you ashore for water either.

On that island with the stream, you stand at the rail and look at the trees. You think about the boat, the beach and a night with no moon. Then you think about the two men who went with Glasby. You stay where you are, and you hate how sensible that is.

Volunteering did not make the job safe either. Only Johnson's book prints the articles of John Phillips's crew, which gave their master one share and a quarter. Johnson names that master, "or navigator", as John Nutt. In April seventeen twenty-four, when the captives rose up, the forced carpenter Edward Cheeseman threw Nutt overboard. The navigator who had chosen the life did not survive its last day.

Phillips's crew had its own forced navigator. A Boston court heard in seventeen twenty-four that a captured man named Henry Giles was made to keep the ship's journal, because he was "an artist", an old word for a man with scientific skill. Two witnesses said he was always planning to escape.

When the captives aboard rose up against Phillips's crew in April seventeen twenty-four, Giles was active in the rising. In May, he was acquitted with the others.

At Cape Coast, two years earlier, Glasby's own trial was nearly a celebration. A ship's factor said Glasby had saved his brigantine when the pirates voted to burn her. In Johnson's version, a musician adds that during the fight with the warship Swallow, Glasby never touched the guns, and only trimmed the sails as Roberts commanded.

The Swallow's lieutenant told the court what he had heard when he boarded the beaten pirate ship. Some of the pirates wanted to blow her up rather than surrender. Glasby had taken a lighted match out of the hand of a man heading for the powder, and had ordered the colours struck after Roberts was killed.

Glasby was acquitted. In all, fifty-two of Roberts's men were hanged, and more than seventy were acquitted, most of them men who had been forced aboard.

You think about who would speak for you. The merchant captain you left his quadrant. Tom, who would say something kind and nothing useful. Maybe the surgeon, who knows what it is to be kept. It is a short list, written in a hand you hope a court can read.

Then the dealer brings the news you have heard before: the king's pardon of seventeen seventeen. Surrender to a governor by the fifth of September, seventeen eighteen, and earlier piracies would be forgiven.

You read the reward list. As the London Gazette printed it, a pirate commander was worth one hundred pounds, and every lieutenant, master, boatswain, carpenter and gunner forty. You are on it, beside the carpenter.

Forty pounds, for the only man who knows where the ship is. You have been undervalued before, but never by a king.

The crew votes on the pardon. You were forced, but you were also elected master, and you have a vote. You raise your hand for the pardon. The pardon wins, narrowly.

Then the captain turns to you and asks the question you have waited two years to hear. Which way to the governor?

You set the course. You check it twice. It is the last course you will ever set for this crew, and for once, everyone wants to arrive at the same place.

So why was being a pirate navigator worse than you think? Because the crew needed you too much to ever let you go ashore. Because you did the hardest arithmetic at sea, and they voted on the answer. Because you could guide them anywhere, except away from them.

And because every course you set led to somebody else's ship. A good navigator could get a crew home. A court would ask where he had taken them first.
