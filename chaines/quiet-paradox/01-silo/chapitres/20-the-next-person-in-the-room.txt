Lukas enters the Legacy because Bernard selects him as a shadow. The appointment gives him access to information most residents cannot inspect. We have already considered what that information contains. The choice of an apprentice opens another question: how does a system make sure the next person in charge will continue it, especially when learning more might give that person a reason to object?

Training someone creates a risk for any institution that depends on keeping knowledge restricted. The apprentice has to understand enough to perform the role. Once they understand it, they may notice a consequence their teacher has learned to tolerate. Bernard can decide who comes into the room, but he cannot completely decide what that person will conclude after looking around. That uncertainty makes succession a plausible weakness in the project's arrangements.

A teacher could respond by revealing things gradually. An early lesson might explain a useful practice. A later lesson could disclose why a troubling instruction exists. By then, the apprentice may have accepted obligations that make leaving difficult. I am describing a possible recruitment process, rather than claiming that every stage has been shown as a deliberate plan. It gives us a way to ask what the order of a person's learning does to their judgment.

Imagine a new official learning to handle a routine request. They follow a procedure, approve a document, and receive praise for doing the job carefully. Much later, they discover that the procedure had a consequence the applicant never knew about. Looking back, they have to decide whether the earlier praise reflected good work or cooperation with something they would have questioned if they had understood it. That hypothetical situation could create loyalty, discomfort, or both.

An institution might benefit from the apprentice feeling personally involved before the fuller explanation arrives. Having already taken part, they may find it difficult to criticize the arrangement without also examining their own conduct. That doesn't make changing their mind impossible. It gives the change a personal cost. The story could explore that cost through a specific decision, without requiring a grand confession that instantly settles the person's entire history.

The apprentice's relationships outside the office would matter too. If someone they know is affected by a rule, the consequence has a familiar face. A teacher's account of collective necessity then competes with an ordinary conversation. The person may still accept the explanation, but they have another source of experience against which to test it. A training system that isolates apprentices could therefore be doing more than providing quiet space for study.

We don't have enough television evidence to say every restricted appointment follows that purpose. Privacy could also protect sensitive records or allow a worker to concentrate. The interpretation would become stronger if a new official were discouraged from speaking to people who could challenge the institution's account. I would look at the relationships they are allowed to maintain, along with the lessons they are given. Those details could reveal how the project tries to turn access into loyalty.

Selecting an apprentice raises a practical concern about competence. A leader who chooses only the most obedient apprentice may end up with someone who cannot adapt when an instruction fails. A curious apprentice might handle an unexpected problem better, while also being more likely to question the explanation behind the work. The project may need qualities that make it harder to preserve complete obedience. That tension could create openings even if its selection procedures have worked for a long time.

For Lukas, curiosity has an obvious practical value. A puzzle requires someone willing to consider whether the apparent answer is complete. We should avoid treating that ability as a guarantee that he will make every future decision wisely. Solving a particular problem and governing a community involve different demands. He could understand a coded record and still need help judging what to disclose, whom to consult, or how quickly to act on a discovery.

That gives the people around an apprentice an important role. Someone with technical knowledge could explain a risk they have underestimated. Someone familiar with ordinary residents could point out a consequence their training omitted. The most promising future would give an official ways to hear those objections. Without them, privileged access could leave the new person as dependent on a narrow account as the teacher they replaced.

An especially revealing test would be what happens when an apprentice finds the teacher's mistake. The teacher might correct it and let the discovery stand. They might explain why it must remain private. They might treat the challenge itself as a sign that the apprentice is unsuitable. Those responses would tell us different things about the role. A position that permits correction could develop over time; a position that punishes it could preserve errors through successive appointments.

I would also watch for who is left without a successor. A system can appear orderly while depending on a small number of people whose skills have never been shared. If one disappears, their room may still contain books and equipment, but no resident knows how to use them reliably. That is a possible concern rather than an inventory we have seen completed. It could make opening knowledge to more people a practical necessity as well as a political choice.

Perhaps future residents will try a different arrangement, with several people learning parts of a job and comparing what they understand. That could prevent one person's account from becoming the only available explanation. It would introduce new difficulties: disagreement, uneven experience, and uncertainty about responsibility. The show could make those difficulties visible instead of presenting a shared institution as effortless. A useful reform would still need people who can do the work.

Succession seems a plausible place for the project to lose control of its own knowledge. Teaching someone how a system works gives them tools they may use for a purpose their teacher didn't intend. We already have a concrete appointment from which to examine that possibility. We still need to see how freely the next official can act once their own responsibilities begin pressing on them. A lesson could become consequential when the apprentice asks a question and the teacher has to let another answer into the room.
