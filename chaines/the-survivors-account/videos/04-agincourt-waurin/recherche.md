RESEARCH NOTES: "The 15-Year-Old Who Fought at Agincourt — And Wrote Down Everything"
Event: Battle of Agincourt (French: Azincourt), Friday 25 October 1415, St Crispin's day; Henry V's English army against the French royal army.
Place: the field between the woods of Azincourt and Tramecourt, county of Saint-Pol, Artois (today Pas-de-Calais), on the road from Harfleur to Calais.
Witness: Jean (Jehan) de Waurin, also spelled Wavrin (use WAURIN in the script). Illegitimate son of Robert, lord of Wavrin; about 15, in the French army; his father and his father's legitimate son (his half-brother Robert) were killed. Some 40 years later he wrote a vast French chronicle of England, the "Recueil des croniques et anchiennes istories de la Grant Bretaigne".

VERIFIED QUOTES (Hardy translation, 1887 volume unless stated; printed page)
1. "which gentleman, of whom I speak, who on account of his good sense and probity chosen king of the said order, was at the time of the fight of Azincourt of the age of nineteen, and was in company with the said King of England in all the affairs of this time, and I, the author of this present work, being then of the age of fifteen, was in the French army." — p. 191 note (MS H). Context: the "gentleman" is the herald later called Golden Fleece (Jean Le Fevre de Saint-Remy), who rode with the English. Can start at "and I, the author".
2. "I John de Wavrin, knight, lord of Forestel, illegitimate son of your grandfather, Monseigneur Robert de Wavrin, formerly knight and lord of the lands and seigneuries of Wavrin, Lillers, and Malannoy, who died in the battle of Azincourt (where on that day I was)" — 1864 vol., prologue pp. 2-3. Context: about 1455, dedicating the chronicle to his nephew Waleran (son of his half-sister). His only words on his father's death.
3. "he ordered that each archer in front should be furnished with a stake sharpened at both ends, and thus he rode from day to day till the day of battle." — p. 196. Context: after the Somme crossing (19 Oct.), Henry knows the French will fight; the army rides in armour every day.
4. "so near his enemies that the foremost of his vanguard saw them quite plainly, and heard them call each other by name, and make a great noise; but as for the English, never did people make less noise, for hardly did one hear them utter a word, or speak together." — p. 201. Context: evening of Thursday 24 Oct., Henry lodged at Maisoncelles.
5. "each of the lords sending their servants and harbingers into the neighbouring villages to seek for straw or litter to put under them, that they might sleep in the same place where they were, which was much beaten down by the trampling of the horses. And almost all the night it ceased not to rain" — p. 202. Context: the French camp, night of 24-25 Oct.; ordered to sleep where they stood.
6. "They had few musical instruments, and during this night one hardly heard a single horse neigh throughout the host. I, the author of this work, know the truth about this, for I was in this assemblage on the French side; and on behalf of the English, Golden Fleece, above mentioned, has certified the same thing, whence an evil augury was drawn in the army of the French, and some foretold that which came of it." — p. 205. Context: same night, fires round the banners. HIS OWN EYEWITNESS LINE. Le Fevre names him as the source: "Le sçay, pour vérité, par messire Jehan, le bastar de Wavrin, seigneur de Forestel; car, en cette assemblée, estoit du costé des François, et j'estoye de l'autre costé des Anglois" (Le Fevre, ed. Morand, I, 247).
7. "it was grand to see them; and as far as one could judge by the eye, they were in number fully six times as many as the English." — p. 206. Context: morning of 25 Oct., the French in three divisions. An impression, not a count.
8. "and then were laid aside many old aversions conceived long ago; some kissed and embraced each other, which it was affecting to witness" — p. 206. Context: French lords split by the Burgundian-Armagnac civil war make peace while waiting, until 9 or 10 a.m.
9. "The place was narrow, and very advantageous for the English, and, on the contrary, very ruinous for the French, for the said French had been all night on horseback, and it rained, and the pages, grooms, and others, in leading about the horses, had broken up the ground, which was so soft that the horses could with difficulty step out of the soil." — p. 209. Context: the French drawn up between two small woods.
10. "wherefore this weight of armour, with the softness of the wet ground, as has been said, kept them as if immovable, so that they could raise their clubs only with great difficulty, and with all these mischiefs there was this, that most of them were troubled with hunger and want of sleep." — p. 210. Context: long steel coats to the knee, leg harness, plate, hooded helmets.
11. "They had archers and cross-bowmen enough, but they would not let them shoot, for the plain was so narrow that there was no room except for the men-at-arms." — p. 210. Context: the French order of battle; lances cut shorter for close fighting.
12. "The said archers were for the most part in their doublets, without armour, their stockings rolled up to their knees, and having hatchets and battle-axes or great swords hanging at their girdles; some were barefooted and bare-headed, others had caps of boiled leather, and others of osier, covered with harpoy or leather." — p. 211. Context: the English archers on the wings as they open fire ("osier" = wicker; leave "harpoy" unexplained).
13. "but the French began to hold down their heads, especially those who had no bucklers, for the impetuosity of the English arrows, which fell so heavily that no one durst uncover or look up." — p. 211. Context: the French advance on foot under the arrows.
14. "struck into these English archers, who had their stakes fixed in front of them, but these had little hold in such soft ground. So the said Sir William and his two companions pressed on boldly; but their horses tumbled among the stakes, and they were speedily slain by the archers, which was a great pity." — p. 212. Context: the French cavalry charge on the archers' wings; Sir William de Saveuse rode ahead of about 300 lances.
15. "and when they came quite up to the English, they were, as has been said, so closely pressed one against another that none of them could lift their arms to strike their enemies, except some that were in front" — p. 211. Context: the French vanguard reaching the English line (use after 13 and 14).
16. "When the King of England perceived them coming thus he caused it to be published that every one that had a prisoner should immediately kill him, which those who had any were unwilling to do, for they expected to get great ransoms for them. But when the king was informed of this he appointed a gentleman with two hundred archers whom he commanded to go through the host and kill all the prisoners, whoever they might be." — p. 214. Context: part of the French rear guard and centre (many Bretons, Gascons, Poitevins) had rallied and was advancing. See RULES.
17. "'It is right, then,' said he, 'that this our victory should for ever bear the name of Azincourt, for every battle ought to be named after the fortress nearest to the place where it was fought.'" — p. 215. Context: Henry asks the name of the nearest castle; told "Azincourt".
18. "Next day, which was Saturday, the King of England and his whole army turned out from Maisoncelles, and passed through the scene of slaughter, where they killed all the French that they found still living, except some that they took prisoners; and King Henry stood there, looking on the pitiable condition of those dead bodies, which were quite naked, for during the night they had been stripped as well by the English as by the peasantry." — pp. 215-216. Context: Saturday 26 Oct. 18b (MS H, p. 216 note): "it was a pitiable thing to see the great body of nobles who were there slain for wishing loyally to serve their sovereign lord the King of France, who were all naked as they left their mother's womb".
19. "for so many noblemen and gentle esquires were killed that it was pitiable, as I the author of this work saw with my eyes. Besides, I have inquired of the officers-at-arms and others who were in the two armies, so that I have been well informed of the truth of all that was done then, as well on the English side as the French; likewise I was largely informed by Sir Hues de Lannoy and Gilbert, his brother, which Sir Hues was taken prisoner, but he escaped the same night." — p. 224. Context: after his list of the French dead, in which "the Lord of Wavrin and his son" appear (p. 221). HIS OWN EYEWITNESS LINE, plus his method (heralds of both sides).
20. "I saw the assembly at Azincourt, where there were many more princes and troops, and also that at Crevant, which was a very fine affair, but certainly that at Verneuil was of all the most formidable and the best fought." — 1891 vol., p. 73. Context: Verneuil, 17 Aug. 1424; Waurin now in the English army under the Duke of Bedford.

VERIFIED FACTS: WAURIN
- Born about 1400: A. Marchandisse, "Jean de Wavrin, un chroniqueur entre Bourgogne et Angleterre", Le Moyen Age 112 (2006) ("Né aux environs de 1400"); Biblissima authority file 1400-1474; Oxford DNB (L. Visser-Fuchs) c.1399/1400-1473/4 (index line only). Aged 15 at Agincourt by his own statement (quote 1); Morand (1876) uses it: Le Fevre 19, "Wavrin, lui, en avait quinze". William Hardy's 1864 guess "about the year 1394", made before this note was printed in 1868, is outdated.
- Family: illegitimate son of Robert de Wavrin, lord of Wavrin, Lillers and Malannoy, hereditary seneschal of Flanders and councillor-chamberlain of the Duke of Burgundy (Marchandisse; Hardy 1864); mother Michielle de Croix (Wikipedia citing the ODNB; not seen in the ODNB). Wavrin is a village near Lille.
- Killed at Agincourt: "the Lord of Wavrin and his son" (Waurin p. 221). Medieval Soldier database (Anne Curry's Southampton project): Robert de Wavrin and "Robert de jnr" among the dead (Monstrelet, the English Brut, the Salisbury and Thoresby lists), both buried at the Abbey of Han near Lillers. Hardy (1864, from Anselme's 1730 genealogy): young Robert had married Jeanne de Crequy six weeks before: unconfirmed. The heiress was Robert's daughter Beatrice; her son Waleran is the "nephew" of the dedication.
- His role at Agincourt is unknown: he says only "in the French army". Marchandisse: present "peut-être comme officier d'armes" (perhaps as an officer of arms); Dan Spencer (Agincourt600, 2015): "unclear whether Waurin was a combatant or not".
- Why the Wavrins went: Duke John the Fearless of Burgundy, at war with the Armagnac princes, ordered the lords of Picardy (Wavrin among them) to stay home; "Nevertheless they obeyed the command of the king in this matter" (Waurin p. 188). The duke's brothers Anthony of Brabant and Philip of Nevers died in the battle; his son Philip was kept away (Waurin p. 199). Craig Taylor (French History, 2019): the Burgundian chroniclers Monstrelet, Le Fevre and Waurin all stress that Philip wanted to fight.
- Later life (Marchandisse unless stated): 1417 squire in Philippe de Saveuse's Burgundian company (muster roll "le bastard de Weaurians", Hardy 1864); perhaps the Holy Land 1418; crusade against the Hussites 1421; after Duke John's murder at Montereau (10 Sept. 1419) Burgundy joined England and Waurin fought on the English side: Cravant 1423, Verneuil 1424 (quote 20), Beaugency and Patay 1429 under Sir John Fastolf, against the French army in which Joan of Arc rode (his own account names "the maid Joan" first among its leaders; he never says he saw her); 1436 at Burgundy's siege of English Calais; 1437 left the army, married a well-off widow of a Lille burgess, legitimated by the duke; knight by 1442; legitimated again by Charles VII in 1447; ducal councillor and chamberlain in the 1460s; 1463 embassy to Pope Pius II; 1467 at the English court with Anthony, the Great Bastard of Burgundy; 1469 followed Warwick "the Kingmaker" from Saint-Omer to Calais to gather material. Died between 1472 and 1475. Commissioned about twelve illuminated manuscripts (the "Master of Wavrin" workshop).
- The chronicle: he says he began "about the year 1455" (prologue); Anne Curry (Reading Medieval Studies, 2008) dates Waurin's and Le Fevre's narratives to "the 1450s and 60s". It runs from the legendary Albina to 1471. He wrote "by way of pastime" (backup line); Livia Visser-Fuchs's book on him (2018) is titled "History as Pastime". It stayed in manuscript; the one complete copy (Louis de Gruuthuse's) is in the BnF, Paris (Wikipedia citing McKendrick 2003). Printed by Dupont (Paris 1858-63, parts) and by William and Edward Hardy (Rolls Series, French 1864-91, English 1864, 1887, 1891). The age note was first printed in 1868 (Morand).

WHICH PARTS ARE HIS OWN EYES
- Anne Curry (2008): Monstrelet's account "is otherwise very close to those of Waurin and Le Fevre and has been commonly taken as the source from which they largely borrowed. The exact relationship of the three chronicles to each other remains problematic." Medieval Soldier: the 273-name list of French dead in Monstrelet (finished by 1447) is also in Waurin and Le Fevre, "but who copied whom is still a matter of debate". Morand (1876): Waurin knew Le Fevre's original and copied from it.
- Copied from Monstrelet (checked in Johnes): the commanders, the French vanguard of 8,000 knights and esquires, 4,000 archers and 1,500 crossbowmen, the wait till 9-10 a.m., Erpingham's baton, the archers' kit, Saveuse's charge, Brabant's death, the baggage raid (Robinet de Bournonville, Ysembart d'Azincourt and about 600 peasants), "about sixteen hundred" English dead, the naming of the battle, the list of dead.
- Shared with Le Fevre but not in Monstrelet: English silence at night (Monstrelet: English trumpets and music all night), rain all night, the churned ground, the weight of armour, no room for French archers, Henry's speech, "fifty thousand" French (Monstrelet: 150,000), "about ten thousand" English, prisoners killed because the French rallied (Monstrelet: because of the baggage raid).
- His own first-person testimony, the only lines to present as "he saw": he was 15 in the French army (quote 1); "where on that day I was" (quote 2); hardly a horse neighed that night (quote 6, credited to him by Le Fevre); the noble dead "as I ... saw with my eyes" (quote 19). Hearsay he flags: Blanchetaque from Golden Fleece, the eighteen knights of Croy "heard related by eminent knights", Philip weeping, the Lannoy brothers, the heralds.
- His informant Ghillebert de Lannoy wrote his own memory (Oeuvres, ed. Potvin 1878, pp. 49-50, French): wounded in knee and head, he lay among the dead; taken prisoner while the dead were stripped; put in a house with ten or twelve other wounded prisoners; at a renewed charge by "monseigneur le duc de Brabant" a cry went up to kill the prisoners and the house was set on fire; he crawled out on all fours, was taken again, sold to Sir John Cornwall, ransomed for 1,200 gold écus. (Curry's English version on Agincourt600 is copyrighted: paraphrase.)

VERIFIED FACTS: THE CAMPAIGN (source in each line; disputed numbers as ranges)
- Henry V (born 1386 or 1387; Britannica "September 16?, 1387") claimed the French crown while France was split between Burgundians and Armagnacs; Waurin (p. 178) says he judged there "never was a better time".
- Southampton plot: the Earl of Cambridge, Lord Scrope and Sir Thomas Grey planned to make the Earl of March king; March told Henry (31 July); executed 2 and 5 Aug. (Univ. of Southampton "Agincourt" course, FutureLearn). Waurin names Lord Cobham instead of Grey.
- Fleet sailed 11 Aug.; landing at Chef-de-Caux on 13-14 Aug. (Dan Spencer, Agincourt600, from the Gesta Henrici Quinti); about 650-700 ships (Craig Lambert, Univ. of Southampton); at least 11,248 men, nearly three-quarters archers (Spencer). Waurin: "about twenty thousand".
- Harfleur besieged from about 18 Aug., surrendered 22 Sept. (Medieval Soldier); Henry entered barefoot (Waurin p. 188); garrison 300 men-at-arms and 900 archers (Medieval Soldier). Dysentery: about 1,330 men invalided home and 36 known dead in the siege (Curry, from TNA sick lists, via Wikipedia "Siege of Harfleur"); dead included Richard Courtenay, bishop of Norwich, and Michael de la Pole, 2nd Earl of Suffolk (Westminster Abbey; Wikipedia).
- March: left 8 Oct. (Britannica); the Blanchetaque ford barred; up the Somme short of bread; crossed at Bethencourt and Voyennes on 19 Oct. (Waurin: "the day after St. Luke's"); stakes cut (quote 3). Strength: roughly 6,000 (about 900 men-at-arms, 5,000 archers: Barker, Rogers, Sumption, per Wikipedia) to 8,500-9,000 (Curry). Waurin: 2,000 men-at-arms, 14,000 archers.
- 24 Oct.: the English crossed the Ternoise at Blangy, found the French across the road, lodged at Maisoncelles (Waurin pp. 200-201).
- 25 Oct. 1415, feast of Saints Crispin and Crispinian (Waurin; Britannica). Field "recently plowed ... bounded by woods" (Britannica); Waurin: "young corn" and "land newly sown". Width between the woods: popular histories give about 750-1,000 yards (not checked in scholarship). Rain most of the night (Waurin, Le Fevre; Musée de l'Armée, Paris).
- ARMY SIZES (disputed): English about 6,000 (Britannica; Barker 2005), 8,500-9,000 (Curry, muster rolls and pay records, Univ. of Southampton 2016), about 9,000 (Musée de l'Armée). French about 12,000 (Curry; Musée de l'Armée), 20,000-30,000 for most estimates (Britannica), about 36,000 (Barker, from a French herald, per reviews). Chroniclers: Waurin/Le Fevre 50,000 and "six times"; Monstrelet 150,000. Curry: about four French to three English; Barker (NPR): at least four to one, possibly six.
- Command: constable Charles d'Albret and Marshal Boucicaut; dukes of Orleans and Bourbon in the vanguard; cavalry wings meant to ride down the archers (Clugnet de Brabant's 800, of whom six score reached the English) (Waurin from Monstrelet).
- English archers shot from the wings behind sharpened stakes, an English innovation (Britannica). The charge failed in mud and stakes; wounded horses broke back into their own vanguard; the press; archers joined the melee; the second division followed; much of the rear guard fled (Waurin pp. 211-213).
- Prisoners killed on Henry's order (Waurin; Britannica). Reason: a French rally (Waurin, Le Fevre); the baggage raid (Monstrelet); Britannica: Henry believed the plundering was a rear attack. Numbers unknown: chronicle figures for surviving prisoners 700-2,200; Rémy Ambühl (Agincourt600) traced over 300 by name.
- French dead (disputed): about 6,000 (Britannica); over 5,000 "including the elite of chivalry" (Musée de l'Armée); 5,800 in three pits at Ruisseauville (Monstrelet); 10,000 (Waurin). The Azincourt 1415 museum memorial names over 500 identified dead (Curry's research). Named (Waurin; Medieval Soldier): Charles d'Albret; Anthony duke of Brabant; Philip count of Nevers; Edward duke of Bar and his brother John; John duke of Alençon; Robert count of Marle; the count of Vaudémont; Admiral Jacques de Châtillon; David de Rambures, master of the crossbowmen; Guichard Dauphin; the lord of Azincourt; the lord of Wavrin and his son; the archbishop of Sens (other chronicles).
- Prisoners: dukes of Orleans and Bourbon, counts of Eu, Vendôme and Richemont, Marshal Boucicaut (died a prisoner in England) (Waurin; Medieval Soldier). Charles of Orleans was a prisoner in England for 25 years, freed 1440, a celebrated poet (Britannica).
- English dead (disputed): about 400 (Britannica), 500 (Musée de l'Armée), "about sixteen hundred" (Waurin, Monstrelet). Named: Edward Duke of York (Henry's cousin) and Michael de la Pole, 3rd Earl of Suffolk. Waurin wrongly says the Earl of Oxford (he died in 1417: Johnes' note).
- After: English dead burned in a barn, York's remains taken home (Waurin p. 216); the army crossed the field on Saturday; Henry rested at his castle of Guines, then Calais (Waurin; popular histories give Calais on 29 Oct., unchecked); back in England 16 Nov., triumph in London 23 Nov. (Ian Mortimer 2009, via Wikipedia; Medieval Soldier also ends the campaign on 23 Nov.). Soldiers sold prisoners at Calais to get home (Waurin p. 218; Ambühl).
- Burial: Philip count of Charolais had the abbot of Ruisseauville and the bailiff of Aire bury the dead left on the field: 5,800 in three trenches, consecrated, hedged with thorns against wolves and dogs (Monstrelet, Johnes vol. 4). No burial pits have been found (Medieval Soldier). Lannoy calls it "la bataille de Rousseauville".
- Later: Treaty of Troyes, 21 May 1420, made Henry heir of France; he married Catherine on 2 June 1420 and died at Vincennes on 31 Aug. 1422 (Britannica).

STORY SHAPE (about 38 min, chronological; his voice as the spine)
1. Cold open (0-3): night of 24 Oct. 1415, rain, straw on mud, fires round the banners; a 15-year-old in the French camp; no horse neighs (6); across the field, silence (4). Then the old knight, about 1455: "where on that day I was" (2).
2. The bastard of Wavrin (3-7): a family of Flemish soldiers; father Robert and half-brother Robert; Burgundians against Armagnacs; Duke John forbids the Picard lords to go, they go anyway; quote 1 (15 on one side, the herald Le Fevre 19 on the other).
3. Henry's gamble (7-11): the claim to France, the Southampton plot, 650-700 ships, the landing; Harfleur's siege and dysentery (backup), 1,330 sent home, surrender, the barefoot king.
4. The march (11-15): 8 Oct., 6,000-9,000 men for Calais; Blanchetaque barred; up the Somme short of bread; crossing 19 Oct.; stakes (3). Context: archers and stakes against horses.
5. The French gather (15-19): the royal summons; young Philip locked away weeping (backup); the host across the road; new knights; the numbers fight (chroniclers 50,000-150,000; historians 12,000-36,000 French, 6,000-9,000 English).
6. The night (19-22): straw and rain (5), the English silent and confessing (4), the silent horses, his own memory confirmed by Le Fevre (6).
7. Morning (22-26): Henry's masses, grey horse, young corn (backup); "six times as many" (7); enemies embracing (8); hours of waiting; failed parley; Erpingham's baton (backup).
8. The mud (26-30): narrow sodden field (9), armour and hunger (10), no room for crossbows (11), archers in doublets (12), heads down under the arrows (13), horses among the stakes (14), the press (15), bows thrown down for axes (backup); the rear guard flees.
9. The order (30-33): the rally, the prisoners killed (16); Lannoy's burning house (paraphrase); the battle named (17); rain at dusk, Orleans pulled from under the dead (backup).
10. The morning after (33-36): the naked dead (18, 18b); "saw with my eyes", the heralds (19); "the lord of Wavrin and his son" on the list; pits at Ruisseauville; Calais; London.
11. The boy who wrote it down (36-38): Burgundy changes sides; Verneuil 1424 (20); Patay 1429 beside Fastolf against Joan of Arc's army; legitimated, knighted, diplomat; the chronicle begun about 1455 "by way of pastime" with the herald from the other side; manuscripts, printed 1864-91; the age line in a manuscript note. Close on quote 2 or "old age is approaching".

RULES
- Quote Waurin only from VERIFIED QUOTES or BACKUP LINES, as Hardy's translation; never paraphrase inside quote marks. Modern translations (Curry, Barker, Agincourt600) are copyrighted: paraphrase only. Monstrelet and Le Fevre are context, never quoted as Waurin.
- "Fought" is not proven: he says he "was in the French army". Narrate "he was with the French army"; at most "he may have fought, or served a herald: he never says". Never show him killing, never show him fighting beside his father (an 1864 guess, no evidence).
- "Wrote down everything": he wrote about 40 years later and much of the battle narrative is copied from Monstrelet or shared with Le Fevre. Use "Waurin writes"; keep "he saw / he remembered" for quotes 1, 2, 6 and 19.
- Age 15, born about 1400; never 21. Never call the half-brother "older" or "younger" (unknown).
- No scene of the father's or half-brother's death: Waurin only lists "the Lord of Wavrin and his son". Say they were killed and buried at the Abbey of Han near Lillers.
- Waurin's numbers are claims (French 50,000, English 10,000, English dead 1,600, French dead 10,000, dysentery dead 2,000, prisoners 1,600): always pair with the modern ranges and name who says what.
- Corrections: Suffolk, not Oxford, died; York was Henry's cousin, not "great uncle"; landing 13-14 Aug. (not 15); the estuary is the Seine (his text says Somme); Henry landed at Dover 16 Nov. (he says sailed 11 Nov.); Grey, not Cobham, in the plot.
- No Shakespeare ("band of brothers", St Crispin speech, "once more unto the breach"). No "V sign" origin story: no contemporary evidence (Curry, Univ. of Southampton). The "three fingers" boast is only what Henry is said to have told his men.
- The killing of prisoners: once, plainly (who ordered it, the reasons given, Waurin's "most pitiable thing"); no name for the esquire; no close-up killing, no burning in detail; never "beheaded and inhumanly cut to pieces" (his next words) on screen. Leave out the boiling of the English nobles' bodies.
- Respect both sides: no "arrogant French got what they deserved", no "cowards". Waurin's bias is Burgundian (Taylor 2019).
- Le Fevre was 19, with the English "amongst the heralds rather than the fighting force" (Curry 2008); not a soldier.
- The 200 archers hidden at Tramecourt: Waurin denies it; do not show it as fact. The field's width is an estimate. The Ruisseauville pits have never been found.
- Joan of Arc: same campaign as Waurin in 1429, opposite sides; no meeting, no "he saw her".
- Images: early-15th-century plate armour, bascinets, padded jacks; archers in doublets with hose rolled to the knee, some barefoot; rain, mud, young winter wheat; Waurin as an unarmoured or lightly armed teenager, no coat of arms invented for him; no Tudor or Shakespeare-stage costume.
