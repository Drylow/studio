# The 15-Year-Old Who Survived Agincourt — His Father Didn't

In the wet dark of 24 October 1415, near Agincourt, you stand with 15-year-old Jean de Waurin among French banners. By tomorrow, his father and half-brother will be on the death roll. Decades later, in the prologue to his chronicle, he says his father died there, 'where on that day I was.' The ground is beaten soft by thousands of hooves. Fires burn around the standards. The horses are quiet. The mud is already choosing the battle. In the next hours, this ground will break an army and a family.

## Where He Was
Jean de Waurin is the boy in the rain. About 40 years later, as an old knight, he writes with names, ranks, and losses arranged behind him.

In the prologue printed in the 1864 Rolls Series translation by Edward Hardy, he identifies himself carefully. He later wrote: "I John de Wavrin, knight, lord of Forestel, illegitimate son of your grandfather." Then he explains why he is writing for his nephew, and why Agincourt belongs to his own memory. His father, he says, died "in the battle of Agincourt, where on that day I was."

Those lines put him on the field.

Waurin is one named surviving witness, probably about 15 years old, and he is on the French side. And in the same battle, his father, Robert de Wavrin, and his legitimate half-brother, also Robert, were killed.

But the record is not clean enough to make him into a boy hero with a sword in his hand. Waurin never tells us exactly whether he fought in the line, served near men of arms, moved with officers of arms, or stood with attendants among the army. He says he was there. That is all.

His book was the Recueil des croniques et anchiennes istories de la Grant Bretaigne, begun around 1455. Parts of his Agincourt narrative are copied from, or shared with, Enguerrand de Monstrelet and Jean Le Fevre de Saint-Remy.

But a few lines are his own.

And those lines lead back before the mud, to a divided France and a family that obeyed the king against its duke.

## The Bastard Son
Jean de Waurin was probably born around 1400. Modern scholars, among them Alain Marchandisse, place his birth in that year or very close to it. His own later note gives the harder number.

In a note to his chronicle, he later wrote: "and I, the author of this present work, being then of the age of fifteen, was in the French army."

Waurin was a teenage noble bastard inside a military world, close enough to the army for his memory to matter.

His father was Robert de Wavrin, lord of Wavrin, Lillers, and Malannoy, hereditary seneschal of Flanders. The Wavrins belonged to the Burgundian nobility, in the border country around Lille, where Flanders, Artois, Burgundy, and the French crown all pressed against one another.

That border mattered.

In 1415 France was not a single calm kingdom waiting for an English invasion. It was torn between Burgundian and Armagnac factions. The French king, Charles VI, suffered periods of mental illness. Great princes fought for control of the royal government. Across the Channel, Henry V saw opportunity.

Waurin also gives us a second witness by contact. Jean Le Fevre de Saint-Remy, later known as Toison d'Or, or Golden Fleece, was on the English side, among heralds rather than as a front-rank fighter. Waurin later says he and Golden Fleece compared their accounts "by way of pastime."

That pastime became part of the record.

A boy born outside the lawful line stood close enough to history to lose his lawful family. But why were they there at all, when their own Burgundian duke wanted many of them to stay home?

## The Order Ignored
The answer begins with Duke John the Fearless of Burgundy. In 1415 he was one of the most powerful men in France, and one of the most mistrusted. His feud with the Armagnac princes had already soaked French politics in violence, and he was careful about handing his own strength to a royal army dominated by enemies.

So, in Waurin's account, Duke John ordered the lords of Picardy, the Wavrins among them, to stay home from the campaign against Henry V.

But the English had landed in France. The king's summons still carried weight. Waurin says that, nevertheless, these men obeyed the command of the king in this matter.

That sentence hides a practical dilemma. Burgundian nobles owed obedience to the king of France, but many distrusted the Armagnac leadership that dominated the royal army.

Burgundian blood was all over the coming disaster. Anthony, duke of Brabant, brother of John the Fearless, would die at Agincourt. Philip, count of Nevers, would die there too. But young Philip, count of Charolais, the future Philip the Good, was kept away. Waurin reports the scene as hearsay, not direct sight. He later wrote: "and, as I was afterwards informed, he withdrew at these words to his private chamber weeping."

So the army moving toward Agincourt was not only French against English. It was also a broken French political world trying, for a few days, to act whole.

That is why the later battlefield scene matters, when old quarrels are said to be set aside and lords embrace before the fighting begins. Political pressure was forcing public unity before the killing began.

Robert de Wavrin and his legitimate son rode into a battle their own political world had tried to avoid. Jean, the illegitimate son, survived to write their names.

The decision to obey the royal summons seemed simple at the time. It wasn't.

## Henry Crosses
In England, in 1415, Henry V is preparing a royal invasion.

He is claiming the crown of France.

France is divided between Burgundian and Armagnac power, and Waurin says Henry judged that there "never was a better time" to press his right across the Channel. That is the pressure that forces the French summons. The quarrel has become a royal invasion.

But before the ships leave, Henry's own throne is touched by conspiracy.

At Southampton, Richard, earl of Cambridge, Henry, Lord Scrope of Masham, and Sir Thomas Grey are accused of plotting to place Edmund Mortimer, earl of March, on the throne. Mortimer informs Henry. Grey is executed on 2 August. Cambridge and Scrope are executed on 5 August.

Waurin tells the story, too, but he names Lord Cobham where modern records identify Grey. That is the kind of error a later chronicler can carry while still preserving the larger shape of events.

The sailing itself is immense. Craig Lambert and the Southampton research put the transport fleet at about 650 to 700 ships. It leaves on 11 August and lands near Chef-de-Caux on 13 to 14 August.

The army is serious. The Gesta and the muster evidence used by Dan Spencer and the Southampton project point to at least 11,248 men, nearly three-quarters of them archers. Waurin gives the larger chronicle figure of about 20,000.

So the popular version begins too late.

The field comes after the plot, the ships, the pay records, and the arithmetic of moving thousands of men and horses into enemy country.

Up to this point, Henry has the initiative.

Harfleur is about to spend it.

## Harfleur Spends Him
Harfleur stands at the mouth of the Seine, and from about 18 August 1415 it becomes the first English target.

Before Agincourt, Henry spends more than a month at Harfleur, dealing with walls, ditches, guns, mines, sickness, and time.

The Medieval Soldier project gives the defending garrison as around 300 men-at-arms and 900 archers. That is not enough to stop Henry forever, but it is enough to hold him for more than a month. The town surrenders on 22 September. Waurin says Henry entered barefoot, as a conqueror giving thanks after a hard siege.

But the victory is already damaged.

Waurin writes: "Besides all other mischief, there broke out in the English army the disease of diarrhoea, of which at least two thousand died." That is his larger chronicle figure, and it may compress rumor, memory, and visible misery into one number.

Anne Curry's archival work gives a harder surviving record. About 1,330 men were invalided home, and 36 known siege dead appear in the surviving sick lists. The real total may have been higher than the named documents show, because medieval paperwork does not catch every man who collapses in a camp.

Important men die, too. Richard Courtenay, bishop of Norwich, dies after the siege. Michael de la Pole, the second earl of Suffolk, also dies.

So Harfleur is a success that weakens the army that won it.

Henry has a captured port, but it is late September. His force is reduced, sick, and deep in hostile country. Sailing straight home would preserve the army. Marching to Calais would display defiance and keep the campaign from looking like a siege followed by retreat.

He chooses Calais.

From this point, the English keep moving because stopping is dangerous. A sick army in enemy land must eat, march, and stay ahead of the forces closing around it.

## The Stakes
On 8 October, Henry leaves Harfleur for Calais.

Modern estimates put the marching army somewhere between 6,000 and 9,000 men, and Anne Curry's reading of the records points to around 8,500 to 9,000. Waurin gives the chronicle figure of 2,000 men-at-arms and 14,000 archers, which is much larger than most modern readings of the records.

The difference matters, but the direction matters more.

Henry's army is smaller than the one that landed.

It moves north and east, trying to reach Calais before the French close the road. But the Somme blocks him. At Blanchetaque, the old ford near the mouth of the Somme, the crossing is guarded. So the English march upstream, hungry, short of bread, with French forces shadowing them and the season turning colder.

Waurin dates the crossing of the Somme to "the day after St. Luke's," which is 19 October. The English get across at Bethencourt and Voyennes.

Then comes the small order that will matter more than it looks.

Waurin writes that Henry "ordered that each archer in front should be furnished with a stake sharpened at both ends, and thus he rode from day to day till the day of battle."

These stakes are not decoration.

They are portable fences. English archers are lightly protected compared with men-at-arms. If cavalry reaches them in open ground, they can be ridden down. But a line of sharpened wood, planted before them, can slow horses, break a charge, and give bowmen time to shoot.

The battle is still days away, but one order is already shaping who may live.

The sharpened stakes are a warning sign, but no one yet knows where the mud will put them.

## The Road Closes
On 24 October, the road to Calais closes.

Henry's army crosses the Ternoise at Blangy, wet, hungry, and still moving north. The men have marched for more than 2 weeks since leaving Harfleur. They have crossed the Somme only after being blocked at the old ford of Blanchetaque and forced far upstream. Now, near the woods of Azincourt and Tramecourt, they find the French army drawn across the way.

There is no clean road left.

Waurin says Henry lodged that night at Maisoncelles. The French are so close that distance stops being an idea and becomes sound. He later wrote that the English king was "so near his enemies that the foremost of his vanguard saw them quite plainly, and heard them call each other by name, and make a great noise; but as for the English, never did people make less noise..."

That is the field before Agincourt.

At night, it is still a roadblock, before later memory turns it into a speech scene.

The numbers are hard because the sources are fighting each other. Waurin and Jean Le Fevre say the French had 50,000 men and the English about 10,000. Monstrelet gives the French 150,000, which modern historians do not accept. Anne Curry and the Musee de l'Armee put the French closer to about 12,000. Britannica gives 20,000 to 30,000. Juliet Barker, reading a French herald's account, gives about 36,000.

For the English, modern estimates usually fall between about 6,000 and 9,000.

Whatever the true number, the French have enough confidence to stand in 3 divisions across the road. The English are not coming here for glory. They are trying to get to Calais.

But the road has become a trap.

For a while, no one attacks. The night itself begins the work.

## The Silent Horses
It is the night of 24 to 25 October, and the rain has not finished with them.

Waurin's most valuable memory is practical: bedding, noise, and the strange silence of horses in a crowded army.

He later wrote that the French lords took their positions, "each of the lords sending their servants and harbingers into the neighbouring villages to seek for straw or litter, in order that they might make some lodgings on the ground, to repose themselves on, for almost all the night it ceased not to rain."

That is not the picture in paintings.

If you are in the French camp, you are on churned ground while servants move through dark villages looking for straw. Horses have beaten the earth before the men lie down on it. The army has blocked the road, but now it has to spend the night where it stands.

And Waurin remembers the silence.

He wrote, "They had few musical instruments, and during this night one hardly heard a single horse neigh throughout the host." Then he gives his authority for it. "I, the author of this work, know the truth about this, for I was in this assemblage on the French side..."

That line matters.

Jean Le Fevre de Saint-Remy, the young herald on the English side, put the same quiet night into his own chronicle. One young man near the French banners, another among the English heralds, and decades later their accounts touch.

Monstrelet gives a different soundscape. He says the English had trumpets and music through the night. Waurin and Le Fevre emphasize English quiet and the strange stillness of the French host.

A medieval army can read silence as an omen.

The practical meaning is worse.

Men and horses are cold, wet, hungry, and waiting on ground that is being ruined under them. The important detail is the ground, because rain and hooves are changing the field before dawn.

## Morning Waiting
Friday, 25 October, is the feast of Saints Crispin and Crispinian.

Dawn comes after rain. The 2 armies are still there. The English have not slipped away in the dark, and the French have not broken them before sunrise.

Waurin says Henry "mounted a small grey horse, without spurs," and arranged his troops quietly "in a fine field of young corn." The detail is small, but it cuts against the later heroic picture. This is not yet the moment of trumpets and bright charges. It is a tired king placing a damaged army on ground where it may have one chance.

The English line is simple enough to draw. Men-at-arms stand in the center. Archers are on the wings, protected by the sharpened stakes they have carried since the march after the Somme crossing. Those stakes matter because the archers are not armored like the knights and men-at-arms in the middle. If cavalry reaches them cleanly, they are in danger. If the horses meet a hedge of wood first, the shape of the fight changes.

Across from them, the French stand between the woods of Azincourt and Tramecourt. They are arranged in 3 divisions, with constable Charles d'Albret and Marshal Boucicaut among the commanders. The dukes of Orleans and Bourbon are in the vanguard. There are princes, bannerets, household men-at-arms, archers, crossbowmen, pages, servants, and horses packed into a narrow field.

Waurin's eye sees magnificence before it sees disaster. He wrote, "it was grand to see them; and as far as one could judge by the eye, they were in number fully six times as many as the English."

That is an impression from the field, not a reliable census. But it tells us what the French host looked like to a 15-year-old inside it. To him the host looked huge, noble, and confident enough to wait.

Waurin also says that old quarrels were put aside. He wrote, "and then were laid aside many old aversions conceived long ago; some kissed and embraced each other..." In a kingdom split between Armagnac and Burgundian factions, that detail is not decorative. Men who had distrusted each other now stand together against the English road column.

The embraces may have mattered politically, but they did nothing to change the soaked ground in front of them.

For hours, neither side gives up the advantage. Attempts at parley fail. The French want Henry to attack through the wet ground toward them. Henry cannot wait forever. His men need food, movement, and Calais. By late morning, the battlefield has become a clock.

The French host looks magnificent, but magnificence is already turning into weight.

Henry has only hours before hunger, exposure, and French numbers decide the day without a battle.

## Erpingham Moves
Henry answers with a measured advance.

The English line moves forward into bowshot, and that means the archers have to do the most dangerous work first. They pull up the sharpened stakes they have carried since the march from the Somme, carry them through wet ground, and hammer them down again closer to the French.

If you are watching from the French side, this looks slow. Men are carrying bows, mallets, hatchets, swords, stakes, and bundles of arrows across a field that is already soft underfoot. Every step forward risks breaking the one thing that has kept cavalry away from them.

But Henry needs the battle to begin without giving the French the ground they want.

Waurin's shared chronicle tradition gives the signal to Sir Thomas Erpingham, a veteran English commander. He rides before the archers, throws a baton into the air, and says one word, "Nestrocq." Monstrelet's nineteenth-century editor guessed that it meant "Now strike." That is all the record safely gives us.

Then the arrows begin.

The exact killing range is less important than the effect. Arrows force men in visors to lower their heads. They make horses flinch. They make ranks close up. They make a crowded formation more crowded.

Waurin later wrote, "The said archers were for the most part in their doublets, without armour," and he says many were bareheaded, or wearing caps of boiled leather, wicker, or other poor protection. Some, he says, had bare feet.

The shining battle of paintings misses this.

Many of the men doing the killing are lightly clothed soldiers, standing behind sharpened wood, using discipline like a machine. The English have moved the fence closer and dared mounted men to ride into it.

Now the French horses and French armor meet ground that has already failed them.

## The Ground Wins
Waurin gives the battle's mechanism in a sentence that matters more than any speech.

He wrote, "The place was narrow, and very advantageous for the English, and, on the contrary, very ruinous for the French." Then he explains why. The French, he says, were so crowded that those behind could hardly use their weapons, while horses in the wet soil could not easily step out or recover.

The field between Azincourt and Tramecourt is not an open parade ground. It is a constricted strip between woods, with recently worked land and young winter grain. French numbers do not spread there. They press inward.

This is where the old story changes shape.

The French men-at-arms understand war, but the field gives them a physical problem that courage cannot remove. They wear full harness. Their visors are lowered against arrows. They have marched, waited, stood in rain, and slept badly or not at all. Behind them are more men pushing forward. Beside them is mud. Ahead of them are stakes and arrows.

Waurin wrote of "this weight of armour, with the softness of the wet ground," and says they were burdened also by hunger and want of sleep. They were brave men, and many were very high-ranking. But those facts did not solve the narrow, muddy ground.

He adds another hard detail. "They had archers and cross-bowmen enough, but they would not let them shoot, for the plain was so narrow." French archers and crossbowmen are present, but according to Waurin they are crowded out by their own men-at-arms.

The mud now changes the fight because it slows horses, traps feet, and makes crowded armor harder to move.

On the wings, mounted men under commanders such as Clugnet de Brabant are meant to ride down the English archers. Waurin and Monstrelet both describe mounted men assigned to this work. Only a fraction reach the line.

Everything that made the French host imposing is about to work against it.

## The Press
Waurin says the first effect is on posture.

He wrote that "the French began to hold down their heads" because of "the impetuosity of the English arrows." The arrows do not have to pierce every harness to change the battle. They hit horses. They strike visors. They find weak places. They make men lower their sight and bunch closer together.

Then the cavalry comes in.

Waurin names Sir William de Saveuse in the charge against the archers. His horses strike the English stakes, but the stakes have little hold in the soft ground. Saveuse and his companions press on anyway. Some horses stumble or fall. The archers close in and kill them.

Waurin calls it "a great pity." He does not turn it into a joke. These are men doing what the plan required, inside ground that ruins the plan.

The worse damage may come backward. Wounded horses and riderless horses crash into the advancing French vanguard. Men already moving through mud are struck by their own cavalry. The ranks tighten.

Then the front reaches the English.

Waurin says the French were "so closely pressed one against another that none of them could lift their arms to strike their enemies, except some that were in front." That is the center of the disaster, the pressure of men packed too tightly to fight.

Seeing the vanguard shaken, Waurin says the English archers come out from behind their stockade. They throw away bows and quivers, and take swords, hatchets, mallets, axes, falcon-beaks, and other weapons.

A man who goes down in full harness is hard to lift. The men behind him trip over him or stop when they cannot get around. The crowd leaves little room for either man to move. The French vanguard physically breaks under arrows, mud, horses, and crowd pressure.

Then the rear of the field changes everything.

## The Order
After the first French divisions are beaten, the field does not become simple. It becomes valuable.

Prisoners are being taken everywhere, because ransom is normal practice among men-at-arms. A duke, a count, a banneret, even a well-born squire can be worth more alive than dead. Captors know this. Prisoners know it too.

But the sources split on the moment that follows.

Waurin and Jean Le Fevre connect Henry's order to a renewed French movement from the rear and center, as if another attack is forming. Monstrelet puts heavier weight on the baggage raid, led by Robinet de Bournonville, Ysembart d'Azincourt, and about 600 peasants. Britannica summarizes the practical fear this way: Henry believed the plundering at the baggage might be an attack from behind.

Waurin's sentence is cold. He later wrote, "When the King of England perceived them coming thus he caused it to be published that every one that had a prisoner should immediately kill him." Then, when men resisted, Waurin says Henry appointed a gentleman with 200 archers to carry the order out.

That is the moral fact. Men who had surrendered were killed on Henry's command because he feared the battle was not secure.

Ghillebert de Lannoy's own memory gives the chaos from inside it. He was wounded in the knee and head, lay among the dead, was taken while bodies were being stripped, placed with wounded prisoners, endangered during the renewed alarm, captured again, and later ransomed for 1,200 gold ecus.

A battlefield full of valuable prisoners was suddenly a battlefield where surrendered men stood under immediate threat from the king's order.

And somewhere in that same field, Waurin's father and half-brother are already dead or dying. He does not describe the moment.

## Azincourt Named
By late day, the French rear guard is gone, broken, or pulling away. The rain is coming back. Evening is closing on a field that has been fought over for only a few hours, but already has the weight of a national disaster.

Henry controls the ground now. But the battle still needs a name.

Waurin says the king asks what castle is nearest. He is told Azincourt. Then Waurin gives him the line that will carry into memory: "It is right, then," Henry says, "that this our victory should for ever bear the name of Azincourt."

In French, it is Azincourt. In English, it becomes Agincourt. The place is not vague. It is the ground between Azincourt and Tramecourt, in Artois, in today's Pas-de-Calais, where the road to Calais had been blocked and the woods had narrowed the field.

Waurin says that as evening drew on, and the rain returned, Henry went back to Maisoncelles. The English archers remained among the dead and the fallen. They turned bodies over and found men still alive.

One of them was Charles, Duke of Orleans.

Other major prisoners included the duke of Bourbon, the counts of Eu, Vendome, and Richemont, and Marshal Boucicaut. Boucicaut would die later as a prisoner in England. The historian Remy Ambuhl has traced more than 300 surviving prisoners by name.

Charles of Orleans would spend 25 years in English captivity. He was freed in 1440, and later remembered as a poet.

So the battle becomes history before the dead have even been counted. It has a name now. Agincourt.

But Waurin's most personal evidence comes after the fighting, when daylight returns and the names become bodies.

## The Death Roll
Saturday, 26 October, is the morning after.

Waurin says, "Next day, which was Saturday, the King of England and his whole army turned out from Maisoncelles," and crossed the battlefield. What they found was not ceremony. It was the dead, stripped by English soldiers and by people from the countryside.

In the revised manuscript note printed by Hardy, the sentence is more direct. Waurin says, "it was a pitiable thing to see the great body of nobles who were there slain," left uncovered, "as they left their mother's womb."

Then the private loss enters the public record.

In Waurin's list of the dead are "the Lord of Wavrin and his son." That is Robert de Wavrin, Jean's father, and Robert de Wavrin junior, his legitimate son.

That is all he gives us. Waurin leaves no scene of their death, only their names in the list.

The casualty numbers are still argued. Britannica gives about 6,000 French dead. The Musee de l'Armee gives more than 5,000. Monstrelet says 5,800 were buried in three pits. Waurin gives 10,000. The Azincourt 1415 museum memorial names more than 500 identified dead from Anne Curry's research.

Among the dead were Charles d'Albret, Anthony duke of Brabant, Philip count of Nevers, Edward duke of Bar, John duke of Alencon, Admiral Jacques de Chatillon, David de Rambures, and the lord of Wavrin with his son.

English losses are disputed too. Britannica gives about 400, the Musee de l'Armee about 500, while Waurin and Monstrelet give about 1,600. Named English dead included Edward duke of York, Henry's cousin, and Michael de la Pole, 3rd Earl of Suffolk. Waurin also names the Earl of Oxford, but that is an error. Oxford died in 1417.

Waurin's death roll preserves the names, but the exact number of dead is still unresolved.

## Saw With My Eyes
After the death roll, Waurin stops listing names and tells you why he believes the list matters.

In the Hardy translation, he writes that "for so many noblemen and gentle esquires were killed that it was pitiable, as I the author of this work saw with my eyes." That is the line that carries the whole account. Not because it means he saw every charge, every prisoner, every death. He did not.

He continues by explaining his method. He says he inquired from officers-at-arms and from others of both armies, and that he was informed by Sir Hues de Lannoy and by Gilbert de Lannoy, men of the same Burgundian world who knew the war, the families, and the names.

That matters.

Waurin wrote as an old knight and compiler about 40 years later, using memory, heraldic inquiry, family knowledge, and overlapping chronicle material.

Anne Curry, in Agincourt: A New History, warns that Monstrelet, Waurin, and Jean Le Fevre are extremely close, and the exact relationship between their accounts remains difficult. Morand argued that Waurin knew Le Fevre's original account and copied from it. The Medieval Soldier project keeps the same problem visible in its work on the French death lists, because scholars still ask who copied whom.

The boundary is clear. Waurin saw the assembly. He was in the French army. He remembered the silent horses. He saw the noble dead. Later, he investigated the rest.

The 15-year-old did not see everything. He survived to tell us what he saw, and later inquiry supplied the names.

But after the names came burial.

## What Was Buried
The English dead were handled apart from the French. Edward duke of York, Henry's cousin, was not left in the common earth of Artois. His remains were taken home. The army that had stood all day in mud and hunger did not stay to make a monument.

It moved.

Henry led his men on toward Guines and Calais, with prisoners walking beside the army because prisoners were money. Remy Ambuhl's work on Agincourt captives shows how real that economy was, tracing more than 300 named prisoners through ransom, custody, and record.

Henry crossed back to England. He landed at Dover on 16 November 1415. On 23 November, he entered London in triumph.

But the field behind him still had to be dealt with.

The French burial effort was organized under Philip count of Charolais, the young Burgundian heir who had been kept from the battle. Monstrelet says Philip appointed the abbot of Ruisseauville and the bailiff of Aire to bury the dead left on the field. Monstrelet gives 5,800 bodies placed in three trenches, consecrated, then hedged with thorns against wolves and dogs.

Modern archaeology has not found those burial pits. Medieval Soldier and modern battlefield work state that no mass graves have yet been identified. That absence does not prove Monstrelet invented them. It means the texts describe burials that archaeology has not yet located.

For Waurin, the loss was not abstract. The Medieval Soldier database records Robert de Wavrin and Robert de Wavrin junior among the dead, with burial at the Abbey of Han near Lillers.

Agincourt did not end the war. In 1420, the Treaty of Troyes made Henry heir of France. He married Catherine of Valois. In 1422, he died.

The burials finished the immediate work on the field, but Agincourt's memory stayed unsettled. And the boy in the French camp would later stand in English service.

## The Old Knight
By 1417, Jean de Waurin appears as a squire in the Burgundian company of Philippe de Saveuse. In 1421, he was connected with the crusade against the Hussites.

Then his world turned.

On 10 September 1419, Duke John the Fearless of Burgundy was murdered at Montereau. Burgundy moved into alliance with England, and Waurin, who had stood in the French army as a boy at Agincourt, later fought on the English side.

He was at Cravant in 1423. He was at Verneuil in 1424. In 1429, he served under Sir John Fastolf at Beaugency and Patay, against the French army in which Joan of Arc rode. Waurin names "the maid Joan" among the French leaders, but he never says he saw her or met her.

In the 1891 Rolls Series volume, writing of Verneuil, Waurin looks back and says, "I saw the assembly at Azincourt... but certainly that at Verneuil was of all the most formidable and the best fought." The old comparison tells you what kind of life followed the rainy field. One battle did not release him from war. It carried him deeper into it.

Around 1437, he left the army. He married a well-off widow of a Lille burgess. He was legitimated by the duke, was a knight by 1442, and was legitimated again by Charles VII in 1447. By the 1460s, he was a ducal councillor and chamberlain.

He became a diplomat, collector, and maker of manuscripts, connected with the world of the Master of Wavrin. In 1469, he followed Warwick from Saint-Omer to Calais to gather material. He wrote, as his prologue says, "feeling within me that old age is approaching."

The last image is rain on trampled ground, horses quiet in the dark, and a 15-year-old in the French camp with the two Roberts still alive for one last night.

Then the record closes around him. The burial list names Robert de Wavrin and Robert de Wavrin junior among the dead. Waurin's chronicle puts the boy there beside them. Decades later, he preserves the sentence that holds it all together. His father died at Agincourt, "where on that day I was."
