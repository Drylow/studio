# The Zulu Warrior Britain Blamed for a War — What He Saw at Isandlwana

It is still dark before dawn on 22 January 1879, and you are in a hidden valley below the Nqutu heights with Mehlokazulu kaSihayo, the eldest son of the chief Sihayo kaXongo and an officer of the iNgobamakhosi regiment. Around him lie roughly 20,000 to 25,000 Zulu warriors, on a day he said they did not mean to fight. About 7 miles away, below Isandlwana, some 1,700 to 1,800 British and colonial troops are in camp. Weeks earlier, Britain had demanded that he be handed over, and made him part of its case for this war. The popular story of what happens next starts with British mistakes and ammunition boxes. So why does Mehlokazulu's own account begin with a hidden army, a new moon, and a battle that was not supposed to happen today? By afternoon, more than 1,300 men on the British side will be dead.

## The blamed son
In July 1878, months before Isandlwana, the line is crossed near Rorke's Drift.

The first crossing comes out of a family crisis inside Sihayo kaXongo's homestead. From there, it moves straight to the Natal border.

One of Sihayo kaXongo's wives flees out of Zululand and into Natal. She takes refuge on the British side of the border. Frances Colenso, writing from contemporary Natal reports and parliamentary papers, recorded that a party led by Mehlokazulu and one of his brothers seized her there, carried her back into Zululand, and she was put to death.

About a week later, Colenso wrote, the same young men, joined by two more brothers and an uncle, seized a second wife who had fled with a young man. She too was supposed to have been put to death.

Two women were seized and killed, or presumed killed. There is no need to soften that.

Colenso also stressed that no Natal people were injured, and no Natal property was taken. But the boundary had been crossed. That gave British officials a case they could turn into a demand.

Sir Henry Bulwer requested that Cetshwayo hand over Sihayo's brother Zuluhlenga, and Sihayo's sons Mehlokazulu, Bekuzulu, Nkumbikazulu, and Tshekwana, for trial.

Even on the Zulu side, responsibility was disputed. Mehlokazulu later said, in notes printed by Vijn and Colenso, "The King sent me." But Zulu court officials told Magema, the Colensos' informant, that Cetshwayo knew nothing of it.

So the man Britain helped make into a reason for war would later become one of the clearest named Zulu witnesses to Britain's most famous defeat in that war.

By then, Mehlokazulu was no longer the whole issue. A family crime on the border was being folded into imperial policy.

## The impossible demand
On 11 December 1878, the ultimatum is read to Zulu representatives.

The ultimatum uses official language, but its practical effect is to put military pressure on Cetshwayo.

Sir Bartle Frere, the British high commissioner, gives the Zulu kingdom 30 days to comply with conditions that go far beyond the Sihayo case. They include handing over Sihayo's relatives. They also include breaking up the Zulu military system itself, with its age regiments, royal permissions to marry, and the king's control over armed service.

Britannica summarizes Frere's ultimatum as one designed to be impossible to satisfy. Those official conditions made invasion the likely next step.

Cetshwayo kaMpande, ruling from oNdini, commonly rendered Ulundi, had offered a fine over the Sihayo affair. Frances Colenso's 1880 account lays out how the request became a demand, and how that demand was joined to larger conditions no Zulu king could safely accept. The British Blue Book prints the terms in official form, dry sentences carrying the weight of invasion.

This is the first thing the camp legend leaves too small.

Later accounts emphasized battlefield drama, command failure, and ammunition boxes. The British Blue Book and Frances Colenso's account show that the political path had already been laid through deadlines, border law, and demands on the kingdom.

There are 30 days.

If they pass without compliance, British columns will cross the Mzinyathi, the Buffalo River, into Zululand. They will carry Martini-Henry rifles, 7-pounder guns, transport animals, wagons, and the confidence of an empire that expects a short campaign.

The same river crossing near Sihayo's home will soon sit behind the British camp at Isandlwana.

The deadline had 30 days to run. By the eleventh day of January, the crossing had begun.

## Across the Buffalo
On 11 January 1879, Lord Chelmsford's Centre Column crosses at Rorke's Drift.

Locally, the place is kwa Jim, after the trader Jim Rorke. On British maps, it is the drift over the Mzinyathi, or Buffalo River. On the far bank is Zululand.

The column is not small. It includes men of the 24th Regiment, mounted volunteers, police, Natal Native Contingent men, gunners, engineers, drivers, oxen, wagons, and stores.

The next day, 12 January, the column attacks Sihayo's people in the Batshe valley and burns his homestead. The War Office Narrative gives 30 Zulus killed.

Among the dead, British parliamentary material cited by Colenso identified Nkumbikazulu, Mehlokazulu's brother.

Mehlokazulu is not there. According to the Natal Witness material quoted in Vijn and Colenso, he and some of his brothers had been sent away to Mbilini's district, and rejoined their regiment only two or three days before Isandlwana.

By 12 January, the attack at Batshe valley has become one of the invasion's opening actions.

The British bring Martini-Henry breech-loading rifles, two 7-pounder guns that will later be in camp, rockets, and trained infantry used to firing by companies. The Zulu army brings shields, spears, some firearms, regiments organized as amabutho, and a tactical system often described as chest and horns.

Below Isandlwana, the British camp is not entrenched. It is not laagered with wagons. It stands open in enemy country.

For a while, the camp under the hill looked like the center of the invasion. That didn't last.

## The unguarded camp
In the days before 22 January, the camp below Isandlwana spreads under the hill like a town that has arrived too quickly.

There are wagons, bell tents, ammunition reserves, oxen, horses, infantry companies of the 24th Regiment, colonial mounted men, police, gunners, and Natal Native Contingent units. The War Office Narrative lists the pieces carefully, because after the disaster every piece will matter. The National Army Museum puts the force left after Chelmsford's split at about 1,800 men.

That is a large camp, spread across open ground without earthworks.

Brevet Lieutenant Colonel Henry Pulleine is left in command when Lord Chelmsford rides out. At about 4 in the morning on 22 January, Chelmsford takes Colonel Glyn, six companies of the 2/24th, four guns, and mounted infantry to support Dartnell and Lonsdale in the hills about 10 miles to the southeast. The official Narrative says each infantryman carried 70 rounds.

The practical result is simple. The invading column has been divided. The camp below the hill is still large, but the number of men left to defend its edge is too small for the space it occupies.

Colonel Anthony Durnford of the Royal Engineers is still on the road from Rorke's Drift with roughly 500 men, including mounted Africans, a rocket battery, and two Natal Native Contingent companies.

The later argument will circle around Durnford and Pulleine, who outranked whom, and who should have done what. But the ground already shows the danger. A large camp is sitting open under a hill, with the enemy army not where Chelmsford thinks it is.

Isandlwana rises behind the tents, with slopes, a narrow neck, and blind ground that will decide who can see, who can run, and who gets cut off.

From the tents, the hill looked quiet, while about 7 miles away the main Zulu army lay in the Ngwebeni valley.

## The hidden valley
The Zulu army has not vanished. It has marched.

According to the War Office Narrative, drawing on Zulu statements after the battle, the army left Nodwengu near Ulundi on 17 January, reached Isiphezi hill on the 20th, and halted on the 21st under the Nqutu heights, about 7 miles northeast of the British camp.

The battlefield historian Ian Knight identifies that valley as the Ngwebeni valley. That is where the great weight of the army lies hidden in broken ground.

The usual strength given by the National Army Museum and Britannica is roughly 20,000 to 25,000 warriors. Uguku, of the uKhandempemvu, and the uNokhenke prisoner reported in The Times through Frances Colenso, also point toward that larger scale. The official 1881 Narrative gave a lower figure, about 14,000 present, with 10,000 attacking the camp.

Even the lower number is enormous against 1,700 or 1,800 men below the hill.

Bertram Mitford's uMbonambi veteran named regiments there: uNodwengu, uNokhenke, uMbonambi, uMpunga, uKhandempemvu or uMcijo, and the iNgobamakhosi with the uVe. The reserve included the Undi corps, with uThulwana, and uDloko, the men who later go toward Rorke's Drift.

The commanders are Ntshingwayo kaMahole Khoza and Mavumengwana kaNdlela Ntuli. Ntshingwayo was an older commander.

Mehlokazulu later gave Norris-Newman the picture from inside that hiding place. He said: "We slept the night before the battle in a valley rising from the Ngnutu range, and running eastward towards the King's kraal. It abounds with scrubby bush and small stones."

Then he explained the timing.

He said: "No orders were given as to the attack; it was not our day. Our day was the following day: it being the new moon we did not intend to fight. Our intention was to attack the camp next day at dawn, but the English forces came to attack us first."

A solar eclipse can only happen at new moon, and later that afternoon other Zulu veterans would remember the sun darkening. Mehlokazulu's printed account does not mention it.

The decision to wait one more night mattered because Raw's patrol would find the army before that plan could happen.

## Raw finds them
Morning comes to the British camp with reports from the northeast.

At about 8 a.m., Zulus are seen or reported beyond the camp. The men stand to arms. Pulleine receives messages, sends reports, and waits under a hill that gives some shelter but blocks vision in the wrong places.

At about 10, or 10:30, Durnford arrives from Rorke's Drift with mounted men, a rocket battery, and Native Contingent companies. As senior officer, he takes command, though the exact handling of authority between him and Pulleine becomes one of the controversies afterward.

At about 11, the official Narrative says Durnford rides east across the plain with two troops and the rocket battery. Lieutenant Charles Raw, Captain Barton, and Lieutenant Roberts take mounted men onto the northern heights.

From the Zulu side, Mehlokazulu said in the Royal Engineers Journal extract quoted by Frances Colenso that he had been out that morning with three other mounted officers, watching the British. They reported to Ntshingwayo. Ntshingwayo said they would see what the British were going to do.

Then the waiting ends.

"Presently," says Mehlokazulu, "I heard Tshingwayo give orders for the Tulwana and Ngyaza regiments to assemble." He said Ntshingwayo then ordered the others "to assemble and advance in the direction of the English camp."

Raw's troop, pursuing or following cattle, comes upon the concealed Zulu army. The uKhandempemvu rises first. Then the main body rises.

Mehlokazulu told Norris-Newman: "Before these mounted men knew where they were the main body of the Zulus got up and swarmed in every direction."

Films often show one long charge across open ground. The accounts describe something rougher. The army is found in broken ground and scrub, rises into motion, and begins forming itself while mounted scouts fall back under pressure.

Around noon, the camp hears firing.

What comes next is the part that still doesn't make sense if the battle is only told from the tents.

## The army rises
He later told Norris-Newman: "The mounted men retired and advanced four times; we just went on, and they retired before us, our Zulu army appearing to become more numerous every moment; we never stopped in our advance."

His description gives the sequence and the scale of the advance.

If you are with the iNgobamakhosi on the left, you see the mounted men pull back, turn, fire, and come on again. Four times. Each time, more Zulu warriors rise behind the first line, from folds in the earth, from scrub, from low ridges and stones that hide men until they move.

This is what "swarmed in every direction" meant.

The chest moves toward the British front. The right horn sweeps around behind Isandlwana. The left horn, with the iNgobamakhosi and the uVe, presses toward the camp's right and the road back to Rorke's Drift.

The official Narrative later described the Zulu movement as a broad horseshoe. That is the shape to hold in your mind. The formation is loose, deep, and moving across broken ground, with space enough for men to keep advancing under fire.

Mehlokazulu uses the words "wings" and "chest of the army," terms from the tactical language of the Zulu army, as he describes the advance spreading across the ground.

A uNokhenke prisoner, reported in The Times and quoted by Frances Colenso, said Cetshwayo had told them to attack by daylight, because there were enough Zulus to "eat it up," and to march slowly, so they would not tire. An uMbonambi veteran later told Bertram Mitford that the king's order was to drive the white soldiers back across the Umzinyati, the Buffalo, into Natal.

The point is endurance, spacing, and envelopment.

The British line is about to fight what it can see, while the horns move toward the ground the camp cannot control.

The camp did not fall all at once. It was held, stopped, and folded in.

## The red hill
Mehlokazulu's regiment reaches the ground near a small red rise, close enough for the camp to be visible below Isandlwana.

He later told Norris-Newman: "There is a little red hill which overlooks Isandwhlana, within sight of the camp, and there the Ngobamakosi, to which I belong, came in contact with two companies of mounted men."

Those mounted men were Durnford's horsemen and colonial volunteers in the broad sense, including Natal Carbineers, Edendale men, and others working that side of the field. The exact place of every troop is still argued from British reports, survivor memories, and the ground itself. But Mehlokazulu's point is plain. The left horn has met organized fire.

From the British side, the two 7-pounder guns begin firing at long range. The War Office Narrative gives about 3,400 yards for some of the early artillery fire. Infantry companies stretch outward, until the line later runs more than 2,000 yards across broken ground.

Mehlokazulu remembered the guns differently from the men directly under the shells.

He said: "The cannon did not do much damage. It only killed four men in our regiment, the shot went over us."

That can happen. Artillery on open ground can frighten men and break up movement, but if the range is wrong, or the targets are loose and shifting, shells pass over or strike only small groups.

Other Zulu witnesses stood in worse places. An uMbonambi veteran told Mitford: "My regiment was here and lost a lot of men; they kept tumbling over one upon another." Uguku, of the uKhandempemvu, told Colenso: "We were now falling very fast."

The Zulu accounts describe men being checked, losing men, lying down, rising, and going forward again.

The left horn now meets organized fire from men hidden low enough that Mehlokazulu can mainly see their helmets.

Durnford's cover held the front for a time, but it did not stop the wider horn movement.

## The donga
Now put yourself down at the dry gully on the side of that little hill.

The ground drops just enough to hide horses and men. A mounted defender can pull his horse into cover, kneel or stand behind the lip, and fire at warriors crossing open, uneven ground.

Mehlokazulu gave Norris-Newman the clearest description of it. He said: "On the side of this little hill there is a donga, into which the mounted men got, and stopped our onward move there. We could not advance against their fire any longer. They had drawn their horses into this donga, and all we could see were the helmets. They fired so heavily we had to retire."

The detail matters because it shows the practical mechanics of the fight.

The defenders expose little more than heads and helmets. The attackers have to rise, run, drop, and rise again while bullets come from a line they can barely see.

For a time, the Zulu left was stopped by a ditch full of horsemen.

But Mehlokazulu is watching the larger movement around that one strong point. He said: "The wings of the Zulu army were running on both sides above Isandlwana, and below towards Rorke's Drift; the men in this donga were firing on the chest of the army."

There is no contradiction. Fire from the donga is striking the central mass while the horns continue stretching around both sides of the battlefield.

The rocket battery is in trouble too. Official accounts and later summaries describe mules and equipment failing as the battery moves over broken ground. Mehlokazulu's fuller account says the rockets could not fire after trouble with the mules. The battery is overrun.

Durnford's problem is now plain. The donga checks the men directly in front of it, while the horns keep moving beyond its field of fire.

His horsemen are running short of ammunition. They can be outflanked. The camp line is too extended, and the right horn is moving behind Isandlwana.

The donga delays the Zulu advance in front of Durnford, but the ground on both sides remains open.

But the version you've heard is not the full picture. Ammunition matters late in the battle, after the extended line is already being enveloped.

## The line folds
Soon after 1 p.m., the donga begins to empty.

Durnford's mounted men fall back toward the camp because the ground they hold no longer controls the field. The Zulu left is pressing them. The horns are moving past them. The firing line behind them is stretching beyond what it can hold.

According to the War Office Narrative, companies of the 24th are falling back from the heights and advanced positions into a defense spread across more than 2,000 yards. That is too much ground for too few men, with tents, wagons, horses, ammunition boxes, and the hill itself behind them.

The Narrative later blamed a gap left when Natal Native Contingent men fled. That became part of the old official story. Later historians, including Ron Lock and Peter Quantrill, disputed that blame, and the ground points to something larger.

The camp is open. The perimeter is too wide. The command has been divided since before dawn.

Later official blame fell heavily on African auxiliaries and Durnford. Later ground-based analysis points to command division, camp placement, and numbers.

Mehlokazulu later described the moment the line folded. He said: "When the soldiers retired on the camp, they did so running, and the Zulus were then intermixed with them, and entered the camp at the same time. The two wings then met in the rear of the camp, and those who were in the camp were thus blocked in"

That is the battle changing shape.

Once retreating soldiers and advancing warriors are mixed together, volley fire loses its order. Officers cannot dress a line already breaking through tents. The camp itself becomes the battlefield.

Smith-Dorrien, one of the few imperial officers who escaped, later remembered the Zulu advance as "a low musical murmuring noise, which gave the impression of a gigantic swarm of bees getting nearer and nearer."

The ammunition crisis is real, and it comes after the long line, the open camp, and the closing horns have already made the defense unstable.

Up to this point, the camp still had firing lines.

That was about to flip into scattered stands.

## Among the tents
Now you are below Isandlwana itself.

The firing line has folded back into a camp of white tents, wagons, ammunition boxes, 2 guns, horses, oxen, dust, and smoke. Men are moving in every direction at once. Some try to form. Some try to load. Some run for the neck and the old road out.

Mehlokazulu's account matters because it refuses to make this clean. He said: "what with the smoke, dust, and intermingling of mounted men, footmen, Zulus, and natives, it was difficult to tell who was mounted and who was not."

That line cuts against the neat battlefield image in later paintings.

Once the attack reaches the camp, even a man inside it cannot divide the field into neat blocks of red coats, horsemen, auxiliaries, and Zulu regiments. The battle has become close, confused, and local.

Other Zulu memories show the same broken shape. An uNokhenke veteran told Bertram Mitford of men under the cliff: "They fought well, a lot of them got up on the steep slope under the cliff behind the camp, and the Zulus could not get at them at all"

The accounts point to scattered resistance beneath the hill, on the slopes, among wagons, and near the neck, rather than to one neat last square.

Mehlokazulu remembered the mounted men, too. He said: "The Carbineers on entering the camp made a strong stand there, and their firing was very heavy. It was a long time before they were overcome, before we finished them. When we did get to them they died in one place all together."

Then he added: "They threw down their guns, when their ammunition was done, and then commenced with their pistols, which they used as long as their ammunition lasted; and then they formed a line, shoulder to shoulder, and back to back, and fought with their knives."

The Royal Engineers Journal extract, quoted by Frances Colenso, gives another close view of a mixed party around the officer the editor identified as Durnford. Mehlokazulu said they "made a desperate resistance, some firing with pistols and others using swords."

He later saw "a dead officer, with his arm in a sling and a big moustache," surrounded by dead men. That identification was an editor's gloss. Smith-Dorrien and later accounts place Durnford's body on the neck.

And now the old wagon road matters more than the tents.

It is the last way out.

## The old road
The neck is the saddle between Isandlwana hill and the ground leading away toward the Mzinyathi, the Buffalo River.

Across it runs the old Dutch road. In the last phase of the battle, that road becomes a border. On one side are men still inside the trap. On the other are men trying to reach the river before the Zulu horns close behind them.

Mehlokazulu remembered this ground clearly. He said: "The resistance was stout where the old Dutch road used to go across; it took a long time to drive back the English forces there; they killed us and we killed them, and the fight was kept up for a long time."

Then he gave the practical reason the end came. He said: "The British troops became helpless, because they had no ammunition, and the Zulus killed them."

Uguku of the uKhandempemvu, quoted by Bishop Colenso, gave the same picture of the last British square on the neck. He said: "Their ammunition was now done, except that they had some revolvers which they fired at us at close quarters."

The sources do show men running out of ammunition near the end.

Smith-Dorrien, writing in 1925, gave the line that shaped the later legend. He remembered: "In those days the boxes were screwed down and it was a very difficult job to get them open, and it was owing to this battle that the construction of the ammunition-boxes was changed."

He also recalled going to Quartermaster Bloomfield of the 2/24th for cartridges, and Bloomfield telling him: "For heaven's sake, don't take that, man, for it belongs to our Battalion."

Smith-Dorrien's memory shaped the legend. Archaeology and later analysis show ammunition boxes alone cannot explain the defeat.

Mehlokazulu, Uguku, and the official Narrative all point to men running out near the end. But historians such as F. W. D. Jackson, Mike Snook, and Ian Knight have argued that screwed ammunition boxes, by themselves, did not lose Isandlwana.

The ground matters here.

In 2000, the Glasgow University team under Dr Tony Pollard found tin box linings and fittings along British firing positions. That means boxes had been opened. Ammunition had reached parts of the line.

Mehlokazulu's post-battle evidence makes the final stage plainer. He said: "We searched the pouches of the men; some had a few cartridges, most of them had none at all; there were very few found."

So the boxes did not have to lose the battle for ammunition failure to be real. A long, collapsing line can run empty at the last stands, even after cartridges have reached part of it.

And one figure stayed with Mehlokazulu.

He said: "There was a tall man who came out of a waggon and made a stout defence."

The tall man remains unnamed. Mehlokazulu only thought he was an officer.

At the end, the men were empty.

That does not mean the screw on a box explains Isandlwana.

But beyond the neck, the fugitives are already running for the river under a darkening sun.

## The black sun
NASA's Five Millennium Canon of Solar Eclipses lists a partial solar eclipse on 22 January 1879, and that afternoon its shadow passes over Isandlwana.

It is a partial darkening. It is not annular here. It does not turn noon into night. When Bertram Mitford later used annular language, that was loose for this place.

But it matters.

The same new moon that Mehlokazulu said made 22 January the wrong day to fight is also the astronomical condition that makes a solar eclipse possible. His printed 1879 account does not mention it. Other Zulu veterans did.

Mitford's anonymous uMbonambi veteran later said: "The tumult and the firing was wonderful; every warrior shouted 'Usutu!' as he killed anyone, and the sun got very dark, like night, with the smoke. The English fought long and hard."

An anonymous uNokhenke veteran told Mitford: "The sun turned black in the middle of the battle; we could still see it over us, or should have thought we had been fighting till evening."

That memory has to be handled carefully, because smoke, dust, and fear all affected how veterans described the darkening sun.

But the eclipse was real, and it began as the camp was breaking. It peaked during the last stands and the pursuit.

By about 2 p.m., the official Narrative says only fugitives remained. Lieutenant Melvill's watch later stopped at 2:10. The road over the neck has been cut, so men trying to escape are forced over broken ground for miles toward the river, at the place later called Fugitives' Drift.

Melvill and Coghill carry the Queen's Colour of the 1/24th. Both are killed at the river. The colour is recovered on 4 February, according to the Narrative.

Smith-Dorrien later wrote that only five imperial officers escaped. Essex, Cochrane, Gardner, Curling, and Smith-Dorrien himself, all in blue coats.

The sun was dimming over the battlefield. Down by the river, another fight was beginning to take shape.

## The reserve crosses
Mehlokazulu later said: "These reserves complained that they had had no opportunity of taking part in the battle of Isandwhlana, and therefore they went on to Rorke's Drift, and fought there."

Those reserves were roughly 4,000 men under Dabulamanzi kaMpande, Cetshwayo's brother. They included men of the Undi corps and uDloko, units that had not borne the main weight at Isandlwana.

After Isandlwana, the reserve movement continued west toward the Mzinyathi, and became the attack on Rorke's Drift.

Mehlokazulu later reflected that Zulu forces had acted contrary to Cetshwayo's instructions when attacking strong positions, especially at Kambula. That warning matters. But the exact command chain at Rorke's Drift should not be made simpler than the sources allow.

He also said: "We who had fought at Isandwhlana were as tired as the Englishmen, and many more of the English forces would have escaped if the reserve regiments had not come up."

At Rorke's Drift, about 150 defenders are attacked for more than 12 hours into 23 January by about 4,000 Zulu warriors. British losses are 17 killed. Zulu dead are given as a range. The War Office Narrative counted about 400 bodies. Britannica gives more than 500. Mehlokazulu said he heard about 300.

Later, Rorke's Drift becomes 11 Victoria Crosses and the heroic counter-image to Isandlwana.

That is the popular frame.

From Mehlokazulu's side, it is the action of reserves who had missed the first battle, crossing toward the same river that fugitives are trying to reach. Later British memory separated Isandlwana and Rorke's Drift, but on 22 January the two actions belonged to the same afternoon of fighting.

The casualty figures came next, and they did not agree in every column.

## Counting the dead
The official figures do not agree in every column, but they all point to the same scale.

The War Office Narrative lists 52 officers and 806 white non-commissioned officers and men killed, plus 200 to 300 African troops whose exact number was not ascertained. The National Army Museum's official return gives 858 Europeans and 471 Africans killed. Britannica gives about 800 regulars and 500 auxiliaries.

The safe spoken number is this. More than 1,300 men on the British side were dead.

Zulu losses are harder. The Narrative says not less than 1,000 were killed. At his Pietermaritzburg examination, printed through Vijn and Colenso, Mehlokazulu said: "there might have been 1,000 killed there."

Mehlokazulu also said: "The dead Zulus were buried in the grain siloes in two kraals; some in dongas, and elsewhere. Zulus died all round Isandwhlana."

The treatment of the dead has often been described badly, or used as horror instead of explanation. Historian Ian Knight explains the cutting of the dead as a rite of belief, meant to free the spirit and to protect the killer from spiritual pollution.

Mehlokazulu put the belief plainly: "All the dead bodies were cut open, because if that had not been done the Zulus would have become swollen like the dead bodies."

There is no triumph here.

Smith-Dorrien later reported that Zulus told him the country ran rivers of tears, with almost every family mourning a close relative.

And Mehlokazulu's war was not over. On 29 March, at Kambula, he said the iNgobamakhosi attacked first and suffered heavily. He remembered: "The regiments were anxious to attack, but we went there cross, our hearts were full, and we intended to do the same as at Isandwhlana."

At Ulundi, the last battle, he said: "we did not fight with the same spirit, because we were then frightened. We had had a severe lesson."

He lives through Isandlwana, Kambula, and Ulundi.

After Ulundi, those experiences were taken down as testimony while he was a prisoner.

## The prisoner speaks
After Ulundi fell on 4 July 1879, the war did not end for Mehlokazulu.

Cetshwayo was captured in August, and Mehlokazulu gave himself up under an assumed name. He was recognized, arrested, and brought to Pietermaritzburg on 4 September, where he was held in the gaol, according to the note preserved by Vijn and Frances Colenso.

In September 1879, he gave the account as a prisoner in Natal, through interpreters, to people from the invading side.

That matters.

His testimony is invaluable, but it has to be handled carefully. Editors added explanations. Interpreters carried his meaning across languages. Norris-Newman said the long account was given in Durban, but its references to distances and places point toward Pietermaritzburg, where Mehlokazulu was being held. The safest description is that it was given in Natal in September 1879, while he was a prisoner.

The account, dated 27 September, appeared in a supplement to the Natal Mercury. Charles L. Norris-Newman reprinted it in 1880. The Natal Witness reported examination material on 2 October 1879. Extracts also appeared in the Royal Engineers Journal in February 1880, and Frances Colenso quoted them in her work with Lieutenant Colonel Edward Durnford, Anthony Durnford's brother.

Colenso's interest was not distant. Her preface acknowledged that the concern of Durnford's friends would be of no ordinary kind.

Yet the record still carried Mehlokazulu's view.

A magistrate declined to commit him for trial because there was no offence punishable under Natal law, according to Colenso's citation of the Natal Colonist on 27 October. Mehlokazulu went home.

His words were printed in 1879 and 1880, but later British-centered accounts pushed them to the edge. British official memory placed responsibility for the defeat on Durnford and on African auxiliaries, while later histories stayed rooted mainly in British sources. Ian Knight has observed that sustained African-centered study began only in the 1970s, after earlier writers such as Reginald Coupland had met Zulu veterans but still built their accounts around the British record.

Mehlokazulu's account leaves the British mistakes and the ammunition failure in place. But it adds the parts his captors could not see clearly. The hidden army discovered early. The left horn checked by fire. The chest held under pressure. The wings closing behind an overextended camp. And the final stands running empty after the trap had already shut.

But he did not remain a voice on paper. Three years later, a traveler found him near the country where the war had begun.

## Back by the river
In 1882, Bertram Mitford visited Mehlokazulu near the Upoko River, close to Sihayo's old country and the border that had helped set the war in motion.

Mitford described him as a sub-chief of the iNgobamakhosi regiment and a good shot. He recorded that Mehlokazulu had ten wives. His father, once a powerful chief, was now, in Mitford's words, nobody. One of his brothers had died in the war.

Border traders called him a scamp. Mitford himself found him a courteous host.

That does not make Mehlokazulu innocent. He was tied to the deaths of two women in 1878. He fought at Isandlwana, Kambula, and Ulundi. He was a violent participant in a violent age, and later became a colonial prisoner whose testimony survived because his enemies wanted answers.

Mitford wrote that Mehlokazulu said "he was a young man and wanted to prove himself a warrior. He had been in all the principal engagements: Isandhlwana, Kambula, and Ulundi, and now he wanted to 'sit still.'"

He also remembered Mehlokazulu saying that "he supposed he must have killed some one, but there was a great deal of confusion."

That sentence has to be read carefully. It shows how battle looked from inside it, where smoke, dust, movement, and fear made individual acts hard to separate.

The wider kingdom had already been broken. Cetshwayo was exiled to Cape Town, visited Britain in 1882, returned to Ulundi in January 1883, and died at Eshowe in February 1884. Zululand was then divided into 13 chiefdoms. Ntshingwayo was killed on 21 July 1883, when Zibhebhu attacked oNdini.

Mehlokazulu's life did not end in the 1879 archive.

In 1906, his name appeared again in colonial records during the Bambatha rising. The same border world that had made him famous in 1878 had not finished with him.

## Mome Gorge
In 1906, Mehlokazulu was a chief in the Nqutu district, the district of Isandlwana.

The Bambatha rising had grown from resistance to the poll tax and colonial rule. James Stuart's 1913 history says Mehlokazulu avoided a summons and meeting with Stuart on 16 May, then joined the rising.

Stuart called him "the notorious ringleader, Mehlokazulu, one of the men who started the Zulu War." That was a colonial judgment, not a neutral description.

At dawn on 10 June 1906, Mehlokazulu was shot at Mome Gorge in Nkandla.

Before dawn in 1879, he had lain in a valley with an army that did not intend to fight that day. In 1906, he died in another colonial war.

His words survived through interpreters, prisoners' examinations, newspapers, the Royal Engineers Journal, Norris-Newman's book, and the Colensos' work. They were printed, then pushed to the edge of a British-centered story.

"The mounted men retired and advanced four times; we just went on, and they retired before us, our Zulu army appearing to become more numerous every moment; we never stopped in our advance."

Before dawn, Britain had not seen the army in the valley, and after the war, it still did not put Mehlokazulu at the center of the story.
