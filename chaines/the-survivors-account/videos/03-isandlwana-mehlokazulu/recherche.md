NOTES: "What a Zulu Warrior Saw at Isandlwana Was Left Out of British History"
Battle of Isandlwana, Wednesday 22 January 1879, below Isandlwana hill, Zululand (today Nqutu area, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa), about 11-12 km in a straight line (computed from map coordinates) east of the Rorke's Drift crossing on the Mzinyathi (Buffalo) River.

THE WITNESS: Mehlokazulu kaSihayo ("Mehlo-ka-zulu" in Mitford, "Meshla Kwa Zulu" in Norris-Newman; usually translated "eyes of the Zulu"; The Californian, 1893: "the eye of the Zulus"). Eldest son of the chief Sihayo kaXongo, whose home lay near Rorke's Drift (Mitford). An officer ("sub-chief", Mitford) of the iNgobamakhosi regiment, the Zulu left at Isandlwana. In his early-to-mid twenties in 1879 (Mitford guessed "about five or six-and-twenty" in 1882; no birth record found). His seizure of two of his father's runaway wives on Natal soil in July 1878 was one of the British reasons for war. Taken prisoner after the war, he gave his testimony in Natal. Why him: the best-documented NAMED Zulu eyewitness of Isandlwana with a long first-person account in public-domain print, and his life spans the whole story (cause, battle, captivity, death in the 1906 rebellion).

VERIFIED QUOTES: MEHLOKAZULU (verbatim; 1-16 = Norris-Newman 1880; spelling as printed: "Isandwhlana", "Ngobamakosi", "Ngnutu" = Nqutu)
1. "We slept the night before the battle in a valley rising from the Ngnutu range, and running eastward towards the King's kraal. It abounds with scrubby bush and small stones." — context: night of 21-22 Jan. 1879, the hidden army (the valley now identified as the Ngwebeni), about 7 miles NE of the camp. p. 79.
2. "No orders were given as to the attack; it was not our day. Our day was the following day: it being the new moon we did not intend to fight. Our intention was to attack the camp next day at dawn, but the English forces came to attack us first." — morning of 22 Jan. p. 79.
3. "The attention of the English mounted troops was drawn to the few men who had exposed themselves under the range, and before these mounted men knew where they were the main body of the Zulus got up and swarmed in every direction." — late morning; British-led scouts stumble on the uKhandempemvu (uMcijo) and the army rises. p. 79.
4. "The mounted men retired and advanced four times; we just went on, and they retired before us, our Zulu army appearing to become more numerous every moment; we never stopped in our advance." — the advance toward the camp, about noon. p. 79.
5. "There is a little red hill which overlooks Isandwhlana, within sight of the camp, and there the Ngobamakosi, to which I belong, came in contact with two companies of mounted men." — the Zulu left meets Durnford's horsemen and colonial volunteers. pp. 79-80. Short form: "there the Ngobamakosi, to which I belong, came in contact with two companies of mounted men."
6. "But on the side of this little hill there is a donga, into which the mounted men got, and stopped our onward move there: we could not advance against their fire any longer. They had drawn their horses into this donga, and all we could see were the helmets. They fired so heavily we had to retire; we kept lying down and rising again." — early afternoon; Durnford's mounted men (Natal Carbineers, Edendale men, others) hold a dry gully ("donga") in front of the camp's right. p. 80.
7. "At this time the wings of the Zulu army were running on both sides above Isandwhlana, and below towards Rorke's Drift; the men in this donga were firing on the chest of the army." — the horns go round both flanks while the chest is held: his own words for the formation. p. 80.
8. "When the soldiers retired on the camp, they did so running, and the Zulus were then intermixed with them, and entered the camp at the same time. The two wings then met in the rear of the camp, and those who were in the camp were thus blocked in" — the British line collapses, from about 1 p.m. p. 81.
9. "The cannon did not do much damage. It only killed four men in our regiment, the shot went over us." — the two 7-pounders firing on the iNgobamakhosi. p. 82.
10. "what with the smoke, dust, and intermingling of mounted men, footmen, Zulus, and natives, it was difficult to tell who was mounted and who was not." — the fight among the tents. p. 82.
11. "The Carbineers on entering the camp made a strong stand there, and their firing was very heavy. It was a long time before they were overcome—before we finished them. When we did get to them they died in one place all together. They threw down their guns, when their ammunition was done, and then commenced with their pistols, which they used as long as their ammunition lasted; and then they formed a line, shoulder to shoulder, and back to back, and fought with their knives." — last stand of the colonial volunteers near the neck. pp. 80-81. Can be split after "all together."
12. "The resistance was stout where the old Dutch road used to go across; it took a long time to drive back the English forces there; they killed us and we killed them, and the fight was kept up for a long time. The British troops became helpless, because they had no ammunition, and the Zulus killed them." — the stand on the neck where the wagon road crossed. p. 81 ("English" half-smudged in the scan).
13. "We searched the pouches of the men; some had a few cartridges, most of them had none at all; there were very few found." — after the fight; Zulu evidence in the ammunition debate. p. 83.
14. "There was a tall man who came out of a waggon and made a stout defence, holding out for some time, when we thought all the white people had been driven out of camp." ... "at last he commanded our attention by the plucky way in which he fought, and because he had killed so many." — one of the last British soldiers, killed "immediately under the Isandwhlana hill"; he thought him an officer (gaiters, shaved chin); name unknown. p. 83 (mark the cut).
15. "These reserves complained that they had had no opportunity of taking part in the battle of Isandwhlana, and therefore they went on to Rorke's Drift, and fought there." — afternoon; the reserve ("men with rings", older married men) crosses toward Rorke's Drift. p. 84.
16. "We who had fought at Isandwhlana were as tired as the Englishmen, and many more of the English forces would have escaped if the reserve regiments had not come up." — the pursuit toward the river. p. 84.
17. "Presently," says Mehlokazulu, "I heard Tshingwayo give orders for the Tulwana and Ngyaza regiments to assemble. When they had done so, he gave orders for the others to assemble and advance in the direction of the English camp. We were fired on first by the mounted men, who checked our advance for some little time." — RE Journal statement via Colenso 1880, p. 413 note. Before this he rode out that morning with three other mounted officers to watch the British and reported to Ntshingwayo, who said "All right, we will see what they are going to do." ("Ngyaza": unidentified.)
18. "made a desperate resistance, some firing with pistols and others using swords, and I repeatedly heard the word 'Fire!' given by someone. But we proved too many for them, and killed them all where they stood. When all was over I had a look at these men, and saw a dead officer, with his arm in a sling and a big moustache (Colonel Durnford, R.E.), surrounded by dead carbineers, soldiers, and other men whom I did not know." — RE Journal via Colenso, p. 414 note; a mixed party of about a hundred mounted men and infantry; the bracket is the editor's. Same statement: the soldiers first "in loose order", then "massing together", when "they fired at a fearful rate."

VERIFIED FACTS: THE ROAD TO WAR
- July 1878 (reported 28 July, Colenso 1880): a wife of Sihayo fled into Natal; a party led by Mehlokazulu and a brother seized her where she had taken refuge, took her back to Zululand and she was put to death; about a week later the same young men, with two more brothers and an uncle, seized a second wife who had fled with a young man; she "is supposed to have been put to death" (Colenso; same story in Mitford, naming Mehlokazulu and Nkumbikazulu as eldest and second sons). No Natal people or property were harmed (Colenso).
- Sir Henry Bulwer (Natal) asked Cetshwayo to hand over Sihayo's brother Zuluhlenga and sons Mehlokazulu, Bekuzulu, Nkumbikazulu and Tshekwana for trial (BPP C.2220, cited in Vijn/Colenso note 6). Cetshwayo offered a fine; the request became a demand in the ultimatum (Colenso 1880). Mehlokazulu later said "The King sent me"; Zulu court officials told the Colensos' informant Magema the king knew nothing of it (Vijn/Colenso notes): give both.
- Ultimatum read to the Zulu representatives on 11 December 1878 (National Army Museum); Frere designed it "to be impossible to satisfy": the Zulu were, among other things, to dismantle their "military system" within 30 days (Britannica).
- 11 Jan. 1879: the Centre (No. 3) Column crossed the Mzinyathi at Rorke's Drift. 12 Jan.: it attacked Sihayo's people in the Batshe valley and burnt his homestead; official count 30 Zulus killed (Narrative); Nkumbikazulu was identified among the dead (BPP C.2242, cited by Colenso). Mehlokazulu and his brothers had been sent away to Mbilini's district and rejoined their regiment two or three days before Isandlwana (Natal Witness via Vijn/Colenso).

VERIFIED FACTS: FORCES AND COMMANDERS
- Zulu army: "over 20,000" (NAM), "more than 20,000" (Britannica), "about 25,000" (NAM collection). Zulu witnesses: Uguku "20,000 to 25,000"; the uNokhenke prisoner about 20,000. The official Narrative (1881) gives about 14,000, "of whom 10,000 attacked the camp". Use 20,000-25,000 and mention the lower official figure.
- Regiments engaged (Mitford's uMbonambi man): uNodwengu, uNokhenke, uMbonambi, uMpunga, uKhandempemvu (uMcijo), iNgobamakhosi (with the uVe, "the boys' regiment"). Reserve: the Undi corps (uThulwana etc.) and uDloko, which went on to Rorke's Drift.
- Commanders: Ntshingwayo kaMahole Khoza and Mavumengwana kaNdlela Ntuli (Britannica); Ntshingwayo about 70 (Wikipedia citing Ian Knight, "Zulu Rising", 2010). Cetshwayo stayed at oNdini (Ulundi).
- March: left Nodwengu near Ulundi on 17 Jan., Isiphezi hill on the 20th, on the 21st halted under the Nqutu heights about 7 miles NE of the camp (Narrative, from Zulu statements); usually identified as the Ngwebeni valley (Wikipedia: "generally been thought").
- Camp after Chelmsford left at about 4 a.m. (Narrative): 30 mounted infantry, about 80 mounted Volunteers and Police, two 7-pounders with 70 gunners, five companies 1/24th and one of 2/24th (2nd Warwickshire), four companies Natal Native Contingent (NNC), 10 pioneers, under Brevet Lt-Col. Henry Pulleine, ordered to defend the camp. Col. Anthony Durnford RE arrived about 10 a.m. (some say 10:30) with five troops of mounted Africans (Basotho, Edendale and Zikhali men: Colenso), a rocket battery and two NNC companies, about 500 men (Wikipedia), and as senior officer took command. Totals: about 1,700 (NAM), "fewer than 2,000" (Britannica).
- Chelmsford took Col. Glyn, six companies 2/24th, four guns and mounted infantry to support Dartnell and Lonsdale in the hills about 10 miles south-east; each man carried 70 rounds (Narrative).
- Weapons: Martini-Henry breech-loading rifles, 7-pounder guns, rockets; Zulu shields and spears (assegais) and some firearms (Smith-Dorrien: "a few of them had firearms"; Mehlokazulu: "our firing was bad").
- Formation: chest and horns (Mehlokazulu's "wings" and "chest of the army"; Uguku: the uNokhenke "ran out in the shape of a horn"; Mitford's man: the iNgobamakhosi "formed the left horn"); the Narrative: "a loose but deep formation of horseshoe shape".

VERIFIED FACTS: THE DAY (approximate local times)
- About 8 a.m. Zulus reported to the NE; troops stand to arms. About 11 a.m. Durnford rides out east across the plain with two troops and the rocket battery and sends two troops (Lt Charles Raw's; Capt. Barton's and Lt Roberts's) onto the heights to the north (Narrative).
- Late morning (about 11 a.m.-noon; sources differ): Raw's troop, chasing Zulu cattle, finds the army; the uKhandempemvu rises first, then the rest (Narrative; quote 3; uNokhenke prisoner). Firing heard in camp at noon.
- 12-1 p.m.: the right horn sweeps behind the hill; the 24th falls back from the heights into a thin line "over 2,000 yards in length"; the guns open at about 3,400 yards; the rocket battery is overrun on the plain (Narrative; Mehlokazulu: mules thrown over a boulder, the rocket could not fire).
- Durnford's horsemen hold the donga on the right until outflanked and short of ammunition, then fall back (Narrative; quote 6). Soon after 1 p.m. the line breaks; the Narrative blames a gap left by fleeing NNC men (disputed by Ron Lock and Peter Quantrill, "Zulu Victory", 2002).
- 1-2 p.m.: fighting in the tents; last stands of the 24th under the hill and of volunteers, 24th men and Durnford on the neck (Narrative; quotes 11, 12, 18; S3, S5). Durnford's body was found on the neck (Smith-Dorrien). "By 2 p.m." only fugitives remained (Narrative); Lt Melvill's watch stopped at 2:10 (Narrative).
- The road over the neck was cut; fugitives ran over broken ground about 4 miles to the river at what became Fugitives' Drift; Melvill and Coghill, carrying the Queen's Colour of the 1/24th, were killed at the river; the colour was recovered on 4 Feb. (Narrative; Smith-Dorrien).
- Captured: the two guns, about 800 Martini-Henry rifles and about 400,000 cartridges (Narrative; Britannica "nearly 1,000 rifles").

VERIFIED FACTS: THE ECLIPSE
- Annular solar eclipse of 22 Jan. 1879, greatest at 11:53 UT, annular path over South America and south-central Africa (Wikipedia). At Isandlwana it was only PARTIAL (Astronomy.com, 22 Jan. 2026: "around 2:30 in the afternoon").
- Our computation (JPL DE440s ephemeris, Skyfield; 28.36 S, 30.65 E): partial from about 1:10 to 3:50 p.m. local time (11:10-13:51 UT), maximum about 2:35-2:40 p.m., about 56% of the Sun's disc covered; Sun high in the west. So it began as the camp broke and peaked during the last stands and the pursuit.
- A solar eclipse happens only at new moon: the "new moon" that kept the army from attacking that day (quote 2; Mitford's man: the moon was "wrong"; Narrative: "the state of the moon was considered unpropitious") is the same event.
- Two separate veterans told Mitford the sun darkened (S1, S2); S1 also blames the smoke. 56% only dims daylight: present it as they described it, never as "night".

VERIFIED FACTS: THE AMMUNITION QUESTION (both sides, named)
- Traditional story: boxes screwed down, a quartermaster guarding his battalion's reserve, the line running dry (Smith-Dorrien 1925: the troops "would have repulsed the Zulus in the open had not ammunition run short"); popularised by Donald R. Morris, "The Washing of the Spears" (1965) (Ian Knight; Wikipedia).
- Zulu evidence that men ran out at the end: Mehlokazulu (quotes 11-13), Uguku (S5); the Narrative too ("their ammunition being expended").
- Challenges: F. W. D. Jackson (1965) "effectively challenged ... the ammunition question" (Ian Knight, Zulu Musings, 4 Sept. 2026); Mike Snook ("How Can Man Die Better", 2005) argues the single-screw sliding lid was no real obstacle (forum summaries, book not read); Lock and Quantrill (2002) built a replica box and tried to force it (reviews; result not verified). Ian Knight: the Mark V/VI box held 600 rounds, weighed nearly 80 lb, lid held by one brass screw, and "The alleged difficulties in opening the box in 1879 are not generally supported by eyewitness accounts" (Zulu Musings, 11 Sept. 2026); the British held too large a perimeter (Wikipedia citing Knight).
- Archaeology 2000 (Glasgow University team under Dr Tony Pollard): tin box linings and fittings along the British firing positions, "sure sign that boxes had been opened there" (Military History Matters, 2011); evidence of "a significant level of re-supply" and a firing line further forward than supposed (Knight).
- Safe wording: men did run out at the end, but most historians no longer think screwed boxes lost the battle; they point to an over-extended line, an unfortified camp and the speed of the envelopment.

VERIFIED FACTS: DEAD AND WOUNDED (ranges)
- British side: 52 officers and 806 white NCOs and men killed, plus "from 200 to 300" African troops, not ascertained (Narrative 1881); official return "858 Europeans and 471 Africans killed" (NAM collection); Britannica: some 800 regulars and 500 auxiliaries. Say "more than 1,300".
- Zulu: "not less than 1,000" killed (Narrative); Mehlokazulu: "might have been 1,000"; higher modern estimates (to 2,000-3,000) not verified. Smith-Dorrien heard Zulus say "the country ran rivers of tears, almost every family bemoaning the loss of some near relative".
- Smith-Dorrien names the only five imperial officers who escaped (Essex, Cochrane, Gardner, Curling, himself), all in blue coats.

VERIFIED FACTS: RORKE'S DRIFT (brief)
- About 4,000 men of the reserve under Dabulamanzi kaMpande (Cetshwayo's brother) attacked the post at Rorke's Drift ("kwa Jim", after the trader Jim Rorke: Mitford), held by about 150 men, for more than 12 hours into 23 Jan.; 17 British killed; about 400 Zulu dead (NAM; Narrative "about 400 bodies"; Britannica "more than 500"; Mehlokazulu "about 300"); 11 Victoria Crosses (NAM). Mehlokazulu: the reserves went because they had missed Isandlwana (quote 15).

VERIFIED FACTS: RITUAL AFTER BATTLE (say once, respectfully)
- Ian Knight ("Wet with Yesterday's Blood: The Disembowelling Controversy", Anglo-Zulu War Historical Society Journal 6, citing Mehlokazulu): stabbing a fallen enemy (ukuhlomula) shared the honour of the kill; opening the abdomen (qaqa) was required by belief, to let the spirit escape, or the killer would be haunted; warriors wore a garment of the dead until cleansed of umnyama, the spiritual pollution of killing.

VERIFIED FACTS: WHAT BECAME OF HIM
- Kambula, 29 March 1879: the iNgobamakhosi attacked first and suffered most (his account). Ulundi, 4 July 1879 (Britannica). Cetshwayo captured August 1879, exiled to Cape Town, visited Britain 1882, back at Ulundi January 1883, died at Eshowe February 1884; Zululand split into thirteen chiefdoms (Britannica). Ntshingwayo killed 21 July 1883 when Zibhebhu attacked oNdini (Wikipedia).
- He gave himself up after Ulundi under an assumed name, was recognised, arrested, reached Pietermaritzburg on 4 Sept. 1879 and was held in the gaol (Vijn/Colenso note 6). The magistrate declined to commit him for trial (no offence punishable under Natal law) and he went home (Colenso 1880, citing the Natal Colonist, 27 Oct. 1879; Mitford).
- 1882: Mitford found him at his homestead near Sihayo's on the Upoko river, "a sub-chief of the Ngobamakosi regiment and a good shot", with ten wives; his father "had been a powerful chief but now was nobody"; a brother had died in the war (Mitford pp. 132-151). Border traders called him a scamp (Mitford); Mitford found him a courteous host.
- 1906: a chief with his own ward in the Nqutu district (Isandlwana's district). He avoided a summons to the magistrate and a meeting with intelligence officer Capt. James Stuart (author of the 1913 history) on 16 May 1906, then joined the rising led by Bambatha kaMancinza, fed by resentment of the poll tax. He was shot in the action at Mome Gorge, Nkandla, at dawn on 10 June 1906 (Stuart 1913, pp. 295-317: "the notorious ringleader, Mehlokazulu, one of the men who started the Zulu War, was shot": a colonial verdict, attribute it).
- How his words reached us: taken down through interpreters while a prisoner; printed in the Natal Witness (2 Oct. 1879) and Natal Mercury, the RE Journal (Feb. 1880), Norris-Newman (1880) and the Colensos' books (1880). Frances Colenso wrote with Lt-Col. Edward Durnford, Anthony Durnford's brother, whose interest "would be of no ordinary kind" (preface, 22 Jan. 1880).
- "Left out": Chelmsford's Court of Inquiry laid "responsibility for the command decisions which led to defeat ... at the feet of Anthony Durnford" and deflected blame onto the black auxiliaries; Reginald Coupland met Zulu veterans but his account "remains firmly rooted in British sources"; African-centred study began only in the 1970s (Ian Knight, Zulu Musings, 4 Sept. 2026). Zulu accounts were printed in 1879-80, then sidelined for about ninety years.

STORY SHAPE (about 38 min; his words are the spine)
1. Cold open (0-3): dawn in the hidden valley, 22 Jan. 1879; a young iNgobamakhosi officer; "it was not our day" (quote 2). The man we follow is also one of the reasons the war began.
2. The raid (3-7): July 1878, Sihayo's runaway wives, the women's fate said plainly; Bulwer's request, Cetshwayo's fine, "The King sent me" vs the court officials; Frere's ultimatum of 11 Dec. 1878.
3. Invasion (7-10): 11 Jan. crossing at Rorke's Drift; 12 Jan. Sihayo's homestead burnt, Nkumbikazulu killed; the British column, the 24th, Martini-Henrys, the NNC; a camp under Isandlwana, not laagered.
4. The impi (10-14): 17 Jan. departure, the king's orders (S4, S8), 20,000-25,000 men, Ntshingwayo and Mavumengwana; the night in the valley (quote 1), the new moon. Chelmsford marches out at 4 a.m. with half the column.
5. Discovery (14-18): Durnford arrives; Raw's troop and the cattle; "swarmed in every direction" (quote 3); Ntshingwayo's orders (quote 17); the advance (quote 4).
6. The horns (18-22): the formation explained; the red hill (quote 5); the guns (quote 9); the British volleys (S4, S5); "the chest of the army" (quote 7).
7. The donga (22-25): "all we could see were the helmets" (quote 6); rockets overrun; Durnford falls back; the line breaks (quote 8).
8. Tents and neck (25-30): confusion (quote 10); the 24th under the cliff (S3); the volunteers and Durnford (quotes 11, 18); the old road (quote 12); the tall man (quote 14); the ammunition debate (quote 13, S7, archaeology).
9. The black sun (30-33): the eclipse in Zulu memory (S1, S2) and in astronomy (partial, about 56%, about 2:35 p.m.), the new-moon link; Melvill's watch at 2:10; Fugitives' Drift; the reserves and Rorke's Drift in one minute (quotes 15, 16).
10. The dead (33-35): more than 1,300 on the British side, at least 1,000 Zulu (his estimate); the ritual explained once; "rivers of tears"; Kambula and Ulundi in his words.
11. What became of him (35-38): false name, gaol, testimony, no trial; Mitford's 1882 visit ("a great deal of confusion"); Mome Gorge, 10 June 1906; printed in 1879-80, then sidelined. Close on quote 4 or "Zulus died all round Isandwhlana."

RULES
- Quote Mehlokazulu only from the lists above, exactly as printed (period spellings stay inside quote marks; use the short forms given). Never merge statements. Credit each: his 1879 account (Norris-Newman), the RE Journal statement (via Colenso), or Mitford's reported conversation ("Mitford wrote that he told him").
- Say once that he spoke as a prisoner, through interpreters, to his enemies, and that editors added glosses. Do not invent his thoughts, dialogue or kills: he would not say ("a great deal of confusion").
- The eclipse lines (S1, S2) and the other veterans' words belong to them; Mehlokazulu's printed account does not mention the eclipse.
- Numbers as ranges with sources: Zulu army 20,000-25,000 (official 1881: about 14,000); camp about 1,700-1,800; British-side dead more than 1,300; Zulu dead at least 1,000; Rorke's Drift about 150 vs about 4,000, 17 British and about 350-500 Zulu dead, 11 VCs.
- Eclipse: partial (about 56%) at Isandlwana, not annular there (Mitford's "annular" is loose); the Zulu did not time the attack to it; never "day turned to night".
- Ammunition: both sides with names; never "the boxes lost the battle"; never deny that men ran out at the end.
- Do not blame the African auxiliaries or Durnford as fact (that was the Court of Inquiry's line, now disputed).
- The 1878 raid: two women seized and killed, said plainly, no excuse, no relish; the British used it as a pretext. Neither villain nor hero: let the sources speak.
- Respect: never "savage", "barbarian", "hordes"; never read the slur "Kafir" (it is in Norris-Newman's next lines and in Colenso: avoid those sentences); Mitford's and Stuart's condescension only as attributed colonial opinion. Say warriors, regiments (amabutho), by name.
- Violence plain, never close-up: one sentence on the ritual cutting; leave out the beheading he mentions and his line about bayonets "through the throat or stomach".
- Names in narration: Mehlokazulu kaSihayo, Sihayo kaXongo, Cetshwayo kaMpande, Ntshingwayo kaMahole, Mavumengwana kaNdlela, Dabulamanzi kaMpande, iNgobamakhosi, uKhandempemvu (uMcijo), uMbonambi, uNokhenke, uNodwengu, oNdini (Ulundi), Mzinyathi (Buffalo), Nqutu; Chelmsford, Durnford, Pulleine, Smith-Dorrien, Melvill, Coghill, Raw. Leave unnamed: the uKhandempemvu officer who rallied his men, the "tall man", the sword-waving officer (Mitford only "supposed" Younghusband).
- Do not use: "An assegai has been thrust into the belly of the nation..." (no source found); "the day of the dead moon" as the Zulu name (unverified); "Men of Harlech"/a Welsh 24th (myth, Military History Matters 2011).
- "Left out of British history" = printed in 1879-80, then sidelined (Inquiry, British-centred histories, until the 1970s). Never say censored or hidden.

NOT VERIFIED: his birth year; the full RE Journal text (only Colenso's extract; officer/dates via Whybra); Durban vs Pietermaritzburg for the 27 Sept. account; "Ngyaza"; Lock and Quantrill's box result; Zulu dead above 1,000; rebels killed at Mome Gorge; his part in the 1880s Zulu civil war.
