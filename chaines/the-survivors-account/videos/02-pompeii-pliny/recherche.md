# Research notes — The 17-Year-Old Who Watched Pompeii Die — His Letters Still Exist

Single eyewitness: Pliny the Younger, two letters to the historian Tacitus about the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79: Letter 6.16 (his uncle's death) and Letter 6.20 (his own night and flight at Misenum). Both letters read in full in:
- MAIN TRANSLATION (public domain): William Melmoth's 18th-century translation revised by F. C. T. Bosanquet, "Letters of Pliny", Project Gutenberg eBook #2811 (there 6.16 = "LXV -- To TACITUS", 6.20 = "LXVI -- To CORNELIUS TACITUS"). Quotes checked by script against the downloaded text; only line-break hyphens rejoined ("pumice-stones") and inner double quotes shown as single quotes.
- SECOND TRANSLATION (public domain, ONE line only): J. B. Firth, "The Letters of the Younger Pliny" (Walter Scott Publishing, London, 1900), archive.org scan (Digital Library of India 2015.38111); OCR noise fixed ("don^t" -> "don't"). The attalus.org text is Firth "with a few words and phrases modified": do not quote it as Firth.
- LATIN: The Latin Library (Book 6) and the Dickinson College Commentaries (DCC) on 6.16.
Why Firth: Pliny writes "agebam enim duodevicensimum annum" = "I was in my eighteenth year", i.e. 17. Melmoth says "but eighteen years of age", which contradicts the title; Firth says "for I was only in my eighteenth year". Use Firth for quote 12 only.

VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim; translation named; never rephrased)
1. "On the 24th of August, about one in the afternoon, my mother desired him to observe a cloud which appeared of a very unusual size and shape." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, Letter 6.16. Context: Misenum, first day of the eruption; his mother points the cloud out to her brother, Pliny the Elder (Latin: "hora fere septima", about the seventh hour).
2. "the appearance of which I cannot give you a more exact description of than by likening it to that of a pine tree, for it shot up to a great height in the form of a very tall trunk, which spread itself out at the top into a sort of branches" — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: the column seen from Misenum, across the bay; nobody yet knew which mountain it came from.
3. "He ordered a light vessel to be got ready, and gave me leave, if I liked, to accompany him. I said I had rather go on with my work; and it so happened, he had himself given me something to write out." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: the uncle wants a closer scientific look; the 17-year-old chooses his homework, which keeps him at Misenum.
4. "who was in the utmost alarm at the imminent danger which threatened her; for her villa lying at the foot of Mount Vesuvius, there was no way of escape but by sea; she earnestly entreated him therefore to come to her assistance." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: as the uncle leaves the house, a note arrives from a woman named Rectina (start the quote here: Melmoth's preceding "the wife of Bassus" follows an old reading; see RULES).
5. "Hastening then to the place from whence others fled with the utmost terror, he steered his course direct to the point of danger, and with so much calmness and presence of mind as to be able to make and dictate his observations upon the motion and all the phenomena of that dreadful scene." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: the uncle turns the science trip into a rescue and launches the galleys (Latin: quadriremes) toward the coast under the mountain.
6. "He was now so close to the mountain that the cinders, which grew thicker and hotter the nearer he approached, fell into the ships, together with pumice-stones, and black pieces of burning rock" — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: afternoon of day one, the fleet sailing into the fall-out; the shore ahead was blocked by the retreating sea and debris.
7. "'Fortune,' said he, 'favours the brave; steer to where Pomponianus is.'" — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16 (Latin: "Fortes fortuna iuvat: Pomponianum pete"). Context: the helmsman advises turning back; the uncle orders him on to his friend Pomponianus at Stabiae.
8. "They went out then, having pillows tied upon their heads with napkins; and this was their whole defence against the storm of stones that fell round them. It was now day everywhere else, but there a deeper darkness prevailed than in the thickest night" — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: Stabiae, morning of day two; the houses rocked with earthquakes, so the party chose the open fields under the falling pumice.
9. "He raised himself up with the assistance of two of his servants, and instantly fell down dead; suffocated, as I conjecture, by some gross and noxious vapour, having always had a weak throat, which was often inflamed." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: on the shore at Stabiae, after lying on a sail-cloth and twice asking for cold water; flames and a smell of sulphur had scattered the others. "As I conjecture": the cause is the nephew's guess.
10. "his body was found entire, and without any marks of violence upon it, in the dress in which he fell, and looking more like a man asleep than dead." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: found when daylight returned, "not till the third day after this melancholy accident" (Melmoth's words just before).
11. "I have faithfully related to you what I was either an eye-witness of myself or received immediately after the accident happened, and before there was time to vary the truth." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.16. Context: the end of the first letter; he admits the Stabiae part came from others, told to him straight after.
12. "I don't know whether my behaviour should be called courageous or rash — for I was only in my eighteenth year — but I called for a volume of Titus Livius, and read it, as though I were perfectly at my ease, and went on making my usual extracts." — J. B. FIRTH (1900), Letter 6.20. Context: night of day one at Misenum; a violent quake drove him and his mother into the courtyard. Credit Firth on screen.
13. "'If your brother,' he said, 'if your uncle be safe, he certainly wishes you may be so too; but if he perished, it was his desire, no doubt, that you might both survive him: why therefore do you delay your escape a moment?'" — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: morning of day two, outside Misenum; a friend of the uncle "who had lately come to him from Spain" (unnamed) urges mother and son to flee; they refuse while the uncle's fate is unknown, and the friend runs.
14. "My mother now besought, urged, even commanded me to make my escape at any rate, which, as I was young, I might easily do; as for herself, she said, her age and corpulency rendered all attempts of that sort impossible; however, she would willingly meet death if she could have the satisfaction of seeing that she was not the occasion of mine. But I absolutely refused to leave her, and, taking her by the hand, compelled her to go with me." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: the cloud had come down over the sea and hidden Capri and the promontory of Misenum.
15. "We had scarcely sat down when night came upon us, not such as we have when the sky is cloudy, or when there is no moon, but that of a room when it is shut up, and all the lights put out." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: they had just left the road so as not to be trampled by the crowd behind them.
16. "You might hear the shrieks of women, the screams of children, and the shouts of men; some calling for their children, others for their parents, others for their husbands, and seeking to recognise each other by the voices that replied; one lamenting his own fate, another that of his family; some wishing to die, from the very fear of dying; some lifting their hands to the gods; but the greater part convinced that there were now no gods at all, and that the final endless night of which we have heard had come upon the world." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: the darkness off the road. Can be split at "some wishing to die".
17. "I might boast that, during all this scene of horror, not a sigh, or expression of fear, escaped me, had not my support been grounded in that miserable, though mighty, consolation, that all mankind were involved in the same calamity, and that I was perishing with the world itself." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: heavy ash falling again; they had to keep standing up to shake it off.
18. "the real day returned, and even the sun shone out, though with a lurid light, like when an eclipse is coming on. Every object that presented itself to our eyes (which were extremely weakened) seemed changed, being covered deep with ashes as if with snow." — Melmoth/Bosanquet, 6.20. Context: the end of the darkness, day two; they then went back to Misenum.

VERIFIED FACTS — FROM THE LETTERS THEMSELVES (Melmoth/Bosanquet unless stated; Latin checked on The Latin Library)
- Why he wrote: Tacitus asked for the uncle's death "in order to transmit a more exact relation of it to posterity" (6.16), then for "what terrors and dangers attended me while I continued at Misenum" (6.20). Both letters end by saying a letter is not history; he calls the second "not in the least worthy" of Tacitus' history.
- The family: at Misenum, where the uncle was "with the fleet under his command" (Latin "classemque imperio praesens regebat"). His mother is the uncle's sister (the Spanish friend says "If your brother... if your uncle"); neither letter names her.
- Day one, afternoon: the uncle had been in the sun, taken a cold bath, eaten a light lunch and gone back to his books (6.16; same routine in Letter 3.5). "About one in the afternoon" = Latin "hora fere septima" (about the seventh hour). The cloud looked "sometimes bright and sometimes dark and spotted". He first ordered a "light vessel" (Latin "liburnica", a fast galley), then, after Rectina's note, launched the galleys (Latin "quadriremes", big warships) to help her and the many people on that coast. The letter never says whether Rectina was reached or survived.
- The crossing: the sea suddenly shallow and the shore blocked with debris; Stabiae is today Castellammare di Stabia (Melmoth's footnote). Pomponianus had loaded his baggage on ships, but the onshore wind kept him in, the same wind that carried the uncle in.
- Stabiae, night: bath, supper, sleep (his heavy breathing "on account of his corpulence" heard outside); the courtyard filling with "stones and ashes"; houses that "rocked from side to side"; torches in the daytime darkness; the sea too rough to sail; he died beside two slaves ("servants" in Melmoth).
- The body: found when daylight returned on "the third day" (Melmoth). Roman counting is inclusive: Firth has "three days after his death", the attalus revision "two days after the last day he had seen". Say "when daylight finally returned".
- Misenum, night of day one: earthquakes "for many days before", "quite an ordinary occurrence in Campania"; a violent quake that night; his mother rushed in as he was getting up to wake her; the courtyard between the buildings and the sea; Livy; the Spanish friend's scolding.
- Day two at Misenum: at first light (Latin "hora diei prima") the buildings tottered and they left town, pushed by a panicking crowd; carriages slid about on level ground even when wedged with stones; the sea drew back and stranded sea creatures; a black cloud split by zigzag flashes "like sheet-lightning, but much larger"; then the events of quotes 13-17; false rumours that part of Misenum had fallen and part was burning; a brief light they took for coming fire; heavy ash again; then a weak sun and a night back at Misenum with continuing quakes. The letter ends with them waiting for news of the uncle: he never tells how it came.
- What the letters do NOT say: neither letter names Pompeii or Herculaneum (checked in the Latin). He did not see those towns during the eruption; Misenum is about 29 km (straight line, computed from map coordinates) west of the crater.

VERIFIED FACTS — PEOPLE, DATES, SCIENCE, ARCHAEOLOGY (source named in each line; uncertain numbers as ranges)
- Pliny's birth and age: born AD 61 or 62 at Comum (Como) (Britannica, "Pliny the Younger"). In 6.20 he says he was "in his eighteenth year" (Latin "agebam enim duodevicensimum annum"), i.e. 17 (Latin Library text; Firth translation).
- Adoption: he was adopted by his uncle Pliny the Elder (Britannica). Full name: Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus (Gutenberg edition). His uncle left him "one hundred and sixty volumes of extracts" in his will (Pliny, Letter 3.5, Melmoth/Bosanquet).
- Misenum: the chief station of the Roman navy on the west coast of Italy, at the promontory forming the western point of the Bay of Naples (Dickinson College Commentaries, note on 6.16). Pliny the Elder commanded the fleet in the Bay of Naples (Britannica, "Pliny the Elder").
- Pliny the Elder: born AD 23 at Novum Comum; author of the 37-book Natural History, completed in 77 and dedicated to Titus, emperor shortly before the eruption (Britannica). His nephew says he died "in his fifty-sixth year" (Letter 3.5, Latin "anno sexto et quinquagensimo"), so 55 or 56; Britannica says "aged 56". WARNING: the Melmoth text of 3.5 wrongly says "sixty-sixth year": do not use it.
- How the sources describe his death: his nephew's conjecture of suffocation by fumes (6.16, above); Britannica says he "was overcome by the fumes resulting from the volcanic activity". Suetonius' short Life of Pliny the Elder (Rolfe translation, Loeb 1914, on LacusCurtius) gives a second tradition: "He was suffocated by the shower of dust and ashes, although some think he was killed by a slave, whom he begged to hasten his end when he was overcome by the intense heat." Present that only as a rumour Suetonius recorded.
- The date debate: the oldest manuscripts of 6.16 give "nonum Kal. Septembres" = 24 August AD 79 (Latin Library text; Pompeii Archaeological Park, E-Journal degli Scavi di Pompei no. 27, 12 Dec 2024, citing Pedar Foss 2022). An autumn date had long been argued from finds such as autumn fruit. In October 2018 a charcoal graffito reading "XVI K NOV" (17 October) was found in the House with the Garden (Casa del Giardino, Regio V); site director Massimo Osanna and culture minister Alberto Bonisoli presented it as evidence for a date after 17 October, widely reported as 24 October (Phys.org, Oct 2018). In Dec 2024 the Park's E-Journal (Gabriel Zuchtriegel, Chiara Comegna et al.) reported an experiment showing such charcoal writing can last up to about ten months (so it may date from October 78) and concluded there is not enough evidence to reject 24 August (La Brújula Verde report of the study; Pompeii Archaeological Park page). PRESENT AS AN OPEN DEBATE.
- Distances: Herculaneum, Pompeii and Stabiae lay about 7, 10 and 16 km from the vent (Petrone et al., PLoS ONE 2018). The surges reached "up to 30 kilometers northwest and west of Vesuvius" (same paper).
- "Plinian eruption": volcanologists' name for very explosive eruptions with towering columns. The USGS glossary says they are "named for Pliny the Younger, who carefully described the disastrous eruption of Vesuvius in 79 A.D."; the U.S. National Park Service and a UC Santa Barbara volcanology page tie the name to Pliny the Elder. Safe wording: "named after the Plinys".
- Column height (modern estimates, Pliny gives no number): Sigurdsson et al. (1985) and Carey & Sigurdsson (1987, GSA Bulletin) estimate the column rose from about 14 to 26-27 km during the first ("white pumice") phase and reached about 32-33 km during the later ("gray pumice") phase. Erupted volume roughly 4 km³ (Sigurdsson et al., via the Michigan Tech volcano pages).
- Timeline as reconstructed by Sigurdsson et al. 1985 (Michigan Tech summary; it uses the 24-25 August dating): eruption began about midday on day one; white pumice fell for about 7 hours, then gray pumice for about 5 more, mostly south of the volcano; the first pyroclastic surge and flow hit Herculaneum about 1 a.m. on day two; surges at about 6:30 and 7:30 a.m. reached Pompeii (the 7:30 surge "overwhelmed" it); a final surge about 8 a.m. spread west across the gulf toward Misenum, and the same summary says the elder Pliny "probably died at Stabiae from the effects of this surge". Modern reconstruction, not Pliny's.
- Pyroclastic surges: fast, very hot clouds of ash and gas formed when the eruption column collapses; Petrone et al. 2018 give typical surge temperatures of 200-500 °C and speeds of 100-300 km/h. Mastrolorenzo et al. (PLoS ONE 2010) concluded that exposure to surges of at least 250 °C at 10 km from the vent was enough to kill instantly, even indoors, and that heat rather than ash suffocation was the main cause of death at Pompeii. Britannica still says most died from asphyxiation: present the cause as debated (heat and/or suffocation).
- Pompeii during the pumice fall: the first deaths at Pompeii came when roofs and floors collapsed under the pumice (Petrone et al. 2018). About 1.5 m of pumice had fallen by early day two and about 2.4 m in all (Michigan Tech summary of Sigurdsson). Britannica gives the city's total cover of pumice and ash as 6-7 m (19-23 ft). There was no lava flow into the city.
- Pompeii population: estimates range from about 10,000 to 20,000 (Britannica "between 10,000 and 20,000"; Petrone et al. 2018 "ca. 20,000"). Herculaneum about 4,000-5,000 (Petrone et al. 2018).
- Victims: more than 1,000 bodies have been found at Pompeii (Britannica; Pompeii Archaeological Park via AFP/Phys.org, 2023). The Park estimates 15-20 percent of the population died (AFP 2023). Britannica and Smithsonian cite about 2,000 deaths at Pompeii. The total death toll of the eruption is unknown: the USGS says "about 2,000 were killed"; the Smithsonian's Volcanoes of the World (1994 ed., cited on the Michigan Tech page) "at least 3,600". Use ranges, never one exact figure.
- Herculaneum boat-houses: excavations of the ancient shoreline started in the 1980s; about 140 victims were found on the beach and in 6 of the 12 waterfront chambers (fornici) and studied first by Sara Bisel; work in the early 1990s in the other 6 chambers found another large group; in all about 300 people had taken refuge in the 12 chambers and were killed by the first surge (Petrone et al., PLoS ONE 2018). Britannica: "more than 120" for the 1980s finds alone. Some reports give about 340 for the whole shore (UNVERIFIED). Say "around 300".
- The "boiling blood" hypothesis (Petrone et al. 2018, from iron residues on the Herculaneum bones) is disputed (a volcanologist quoted by Smithsonian). Hypothesis only, or leave out.
- Herculaneum burial: about 15-18 m (50-60 ft) of compact volcanic material, which preserved wood and food; regular excavations from 1738 under the king of Naples (Britannica, "Herculaneum").
- The earlier earthquake: an earthquake in 62 did great damage in both Pompeii and Herculaneum, and some damage was still unrepaired in 79 (Britannica, "Pompeii" and "Herculaneum").
- Pompeii excavation: excavations officially started in 1748 under Charles III of Bourbon, king of Naples (Archaeological Park of Pompeii, Google Arts & Culture). An inscription found in 1763 confirmed the site was Pompeii, usually quoted as "Rei Publicae Pompeianorum" (secondary sites only: say just "in 1763 an inscription confirmed the name").
- The casts: Giuseppe Fiorelli developed the technique in 1863: liquid plaster poured into the hollows left in the hardened ash where bodies had decayed, often with bones still inside (Smithsonian Magazine; Archaeological Park of Pompeii, Google Arts & Culture). More than 100 casts have been made (Smithsonian).
- DNA (2024): a genetic study of skeletal material inside the casts (Pilli et al., Current Biology, Nov 2024; Harvard/Reich lab) found that the adult in the House of the Golden Bracelet long called a "mother and child" was an adult male unrelated to the child; sexes and family links of several casts did not match the traditional stories (Current Biology; CNN).
- After the disaster: Emperor Titus "chose commissioners by lot from among the ex-consuls for the relief of Campania", and used the property of victims who left no heirs to rebuild the buried cities (Suetonius, Life of Titus 8.4, Rolfe translation; also the Pompeii Archaeological Park, "Pompeii after the Eruption"). The Park says surviving Pompeians recovered part of their possessions and the sites were abandoned.
- When Pliny wrote the letters: late 106 or early 107, about 27 years after the eruption (Dickinson College Commentaries, introduction to 6.16). He was then about 44-45. Tacitus was writing his Histories; the part covering the eruption is lost (only four books and part of the fifth survive) (DCC).
- How the letters were published: between 100 and 109 Pliny published nine books of selected private letters; a tenth book holds his official letters to Emperor Trajan and the replies (Britannica). The Vesuvius letters are in Book 6.
- How they reached us: medieval hand copies. The oldest surviving manuscript of the letters is a late 5th-century fragment of six leaves (Morgan Library, New York, M.462; Roger Pearse's manuscript list, tertullian.org; Edward Worth Library); whether it holds Book 6 was NOT verified. Fuller copies date from the 9th century, in Florence and the Vatican (Pearse's list). Aldus Manutius printed the first complete edition of all ten books in Venice in 1508, using a very old manuscript found in Paris (Edward Worth Library). A partial printed edition of 1471 is reported but NOT verified.
- What became of Pliny: he began practising law at 18 (Britannica); praetor in 93 and consul in 100 (Britannica), when he gave a speech of thanks to Trajan, the Panegyricus, his only surviving speech (Gutenberg edition's introduction). Around 110 Trajan sent him to govern Bithynia (northern Turkey) and investigate corruption; he apparently died there about two years later, c. 112-113 (Britannica; the Gutenberg introduction says "about 113").
- The Christians letter: as governor he wrote to Trajan (Letter 10.96) about people accused of being Christians: he asked them three times and punished those who persisted; he reported that they met "on a stated day before it was light, and addressed a form of prayer to Christ, as to a divinity" (Melmoth/Bosanquet, letter XCVII "To the Emperor Trajan"). Trajan replied: "Do not go out of your way to look for them" (letter XCVIII).

STORY SHAPE (chronological, about 38 min, seen through his eyes; his letters give the spine, science and archaeology only as context)
1. Cold open (0-3 min): dawn of day two, a 17-year-old pulling his mother off a road in total darkness, the shrieks around them (quotes 15, 16). Rewind: the letters he wrote 27 years later because a historian asked.
2. Misenum, AD 79 (3-7): the fleet base at the tip of the bay; the uncle, admiral and encyclopedist (sun, cold bath, books, 160 notebooks); the boy and his mother, the uncle's sister; earthquakes "for many days before", normal in Campania; across the bay, Herculaneum, Pompeii, Stabiae, still repairing damage from the earthquake of 62.
3. The pine-tree cloud (7-11): early afternoon, his mother points it out; the uncle climbs to look; the pine tree, bright and dark (quotes 1, 2); the offer of a boat and the boy's answer (quote 3). Context: what volcanologists now call a Plinian column, an estimated 26-33 km high.
4. Rectina's note (11-15): the change from science to rescue; the galleys launched; steering into danger while dictating notes (quotes 4, 5); hot cinders and pumice on the decks, the shallow sea, "Fortune favours the brave" (quotes 6, 7). Context (not his words): pumice piling up on Pompeii's roofs.
5. The night at Stabiae (15-19): Pomponianus' loaded ships held by the wind; the bath, the cheerful supper, the "burning villages", the snoring sleep (backup lines); the courtyard filling with ash; the houses rocking; pillows tied on heads; darkness at daytime (quote 8).
6. The shore (19-23): the sail-cloth, the cold water, flames and sulphur, the fall (quote 9); the body found "like a man asleep" (quote 10); Suetonius' darker rumour; what modern reconstructions say killed Herculaneum (first surge, night) and Pompeii (morning surges), and the final surge that rolled toward Stabiae and Misenum. Quote 11: how he knew all this.
7. Meanwhile at Misenum (23-27): study, bath, supper, uneasy sleep; the quake; the courtyard; Livy at 17 (quote 12, FIRTH); the friend's scolding; dawn, leaving town with the crowd; sliding carts, the sea pulled back, the black cloud cut by giant flashes; the friend's ultimatum and flight (quote 13).
8. The darkness (27-32): the cloud swallows Capri and the cape; his mother begs him to leave her; he refuses (quote 14); off the road; the darkness (quote 15); the voices, "no gods at all" (quote 16); false rumours; ash to shake off; "perishing with the world itself" (quote 17).
9. Daylight (32-35): the eclipse-like sun, ash like snow (quote 18); back to Misenum, another night of quakes; waiting for news of the uncle (last line of the letter). What the bay looked like after: towns buried, Titus' commissioners, survivors salvaging and leaving.
10. The letters still exist (35-38): lawyer at 18, consul in 100, governor of Bithynia, the Christians letter, died c. 113; Tacitus' account lost, Pliny's letters copied by hand for 1,400 years, printed in 1508; "Plinian"; archaeology: Herculaneum from 1738, Pompeii from 1748 (named in 1763), Fiorelli's casts in 1863, the boat-houses of Herculaneum in the 1980s-90s, DNA in 2024; the 2018 graffito and the open date debate. Close on his own claim to truth (quote 11) or the last line of 6.20.

RULES
- Quote Pliny verbatim only from VERIFIED QUOTES (or BACKUP LINES); always name the translation (Melmoth/Bosanquet, or Firth for quote 12 only). Never mix two translations inside one quote and never "modernise" the wording. Modern translations (Radice/Penguin etc.) are not public domain: "as in a closed room when the lamp is put out" is NOT in our texts.
- The age: say 17 ("in his eighteenth year"). Never put Melmoth's "but eighteen years of age" on screen. Born 61 or 62, so "17" rests on his own words.
- The title says "watched Pompeii die": in the narration say he watched the eruption that destroyed Pompeii, from Misenum about 29 km away. He never names Pompeii or Herculaneum, never saw them buried, and never describes bodies there. Do not put him on a hill "looking at Pompeii".
- His uncle's last night and death are second-hand (survivors told him "immediately after"); narrate them as "Pliny was told" / "he writes", never as something he saw. He was not on the ships.
- Rectina: call her only "Rectina". Melmoth's "the wife of Bassus" follows an older reading; modern Latin texts read "Rectinae Tasci" (Firth: "wife of Tascus"). Do not name her husband, do not place her villa in a named town, and never say she was rescued or died: the letter does not say.
- Unnamed people stay unnamed (the mother, the Spanish friend, the helmsman, the servants); besides the Plinys and Tacitus, only Rectina and Pomponianus are named. Do not invent who Pomponianus was. Spoken lines only as in the quotes ("Fortune favours the brave", the Spanish friend, "Let us turn out of the high-road", the mother's plea as reported); no invented dialogue.
- Date: say "24 August AD 79, the date in Pliny's letter", and present the October theory (2018 graffito) and the 2024 counter-study as an open debate. Never state 24 October as fact. Firth's "23rd of August" is a translator's slip: do not use it.
- Cause of the uncle's death: Pliny's guess, Britannica's "fumes", Suetonius' rumour only; no modern diagnosis as fact. His age: 55 or 56 ("in his fifty-sixth year"); never Melmoth's "sixty-sixth".
- Numbers only from the facts above, as ranges with their source: column 26-33 km (estimates), Pompeii 10,000-20,000 people, more than 1,000 bodies found, about 2,000 dead at Pompeii (estimate), total dead unknown (about 2,000 to at least 3,600 in different references), Herculaneum about 300 in the boat-houses. No invented statistics, no exact death toll.
- The eruption timeline by the hour is a modern reconstruction (Sigurdsson et al. 1985), not Pliny's: label it as such and keep it out of his mouth. It assumes the August date.
- Images: Pompeii was buried by pumice and pyroclastic surges, not by rivers of lava. Pliny's "flames" and "fire" are his words for what he saw; do not show lava pouring into the towns. Herculaneum's and Pompeii's dead are real people: show casts and skeletons with respect, no gore, no fake "last moments" scenes of identifiable victims (the 2024 DNA study showed some traditional stories about the casts were wrong).
- "Plinian": do not say Pliny coined the word; modern volcanologists named it after him (USGS) or his uncle (NPS).
- He wrote the letters at about 44-45, not as a teenager: "his letters still exist" means copies of what he wrote in 106-107, preserved through medieval manuscripts and printed in full in 1508.
