# What a 15-Year-Old Girl Saw at Gettysburg Was Too Brutal for Textbooks

It's the evening of Friday, July 3rd, 1863, the light is going, and at a front window of a farmhouse three miles south of Gettysburg, a 15-year-old girl is watching surgeons work on a bench below her. Her name is Tillie Pierce, she's a butcher's daughter from Baltimore Street, and you're about to see what she saw, the part of Gettysburg that was too brutal for the textbooks. If you stand there beside her, you can watch them lift a man onto that bench, press a cattle horn over his mouth, and reach for the saw. Just outside the yard, there's a pile of arms and legs higher than the fence. Your textbook gives this afternoon to Pickett's Charge, somewhere between 12,500 and 15,000 men crossing open fields toward the Union center. Tillie never saw it. Her Gettysburg is this farmhouse on the Taneytown Road, full of wounded men, at the end of three days that cost somewhere between 46,000 and 51,000 men killed, wounded, captured or missing. And tonight, she still doesn't know if her own family is alive.

## A town of ten roads

She wrote every bit of this down herself. In 1889, a book came out called At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle, signed Mrs. Tillie Pierce Alleman. She was writing about 25 years after that evening at the window, for her own children, she said, and she made a point of saying she didn't want to be counted among the heroines. On the first pages, she warned her readers what kind of book they were holding. She wrote, "The horrors of war are fully known only to those who have seen and heard them. It was my lot to see and hear only part, but it was sufficient."

Notice what she leaves out. Her age isn't anywhere in the book. It comes from her date of birth, March 11th, 1848, which makes her 15 the summer the armies arrive.

And in that summer, Gettysburg is nobody's idea of a battlefield. It's a market town known for two things, its Lutheran college and its Theological Seminary. The detail that matters is the roads. About ten of them meet here, and that's why two armies are going to arrive at once.

Tillie lives on Baltimore Street, in a double brick house with a row of linden trees out front, on the corner of Breckenridge Street. Her father, James Pierce, is a butcher, and his shop is right there at the house. At home it's her father, her mother, Margaret, and her sister, Maggie. Her two brothers, James and William, are away in the Union army. On weekdays she walks to the Young Ladies' Seminary on the corner of High and Washington Streets, where the teacher is a Mrs. Eyster.

On free days, she has the whole countryside. She picnics on the boulders of Culp's Hill. She drinks from Spangler's Spring. She goes berrying along the lanes, and in the evenings she watches the sun go down over South Mountain and the two Round Tops from Evergreen Cemetery. Culp's Hill, the cemetery, the Round Tops. By the first week of July, every one of those places is part of the Union line.

And the first time the war actually reaches her street, it comes down the hill toward her front door, firing.

## The Rebels are coming

It starts with rumors. In June 1863, Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia crosses into Pennsylvania, and Gettysburg keeps hearing that Confederate raiders are on the way. The older men of the town start drilling with whatever they can find, rusty guns, old swords, pitchforks and pick-axes.

The town's Black residents don't wait to see what happens. They hurry down Breckenridge and Baltimore Streets, right past the Pierces' corner, and out to the woods on Culp's Hill, convinced that being caught means being destroyed. They're right to be afraid. During this campaign, Confederate troops seize free Black people in southern Pennsylvania and send them south into slavery.

Friday, June 26th, in the afternoon. At the Young Ladies' Seminary, the girls are busy with their Friday afternoon literary exercises when someone spots a dark mass of men coming toward town from the direction of the Theological Seminary. Mrs. Eyster doesn't take any chances. She tells them, "Children, run home as quickly as you can."

Tillie runs. She gets inside the brick house on Baltimore Street, and she watches the rest through the shutters. She later wrote, "What a horrible sight! There they were, human beings! clad almost in rags, covered with dust, riding wildly, pell-mell down the hill toward our home! shouting, yelling most unearthly, cursing, brandishing their revolvers, and firing right and left."

Those horsemen belong to Lieutenant Colonel Elijah White's 35th Battalion of Virginia Cavalry, riding with Jubal Early's Confederate division. West of town, a regiment of emergency militia, the 26th Pennsylvania, along with a company of local students and townsmen, has already skirmished with them. It doesn't keep them out. Behind the cavalry comes the infantry, and the infantry ransacks the town.

Then Early's men hand Gettysburg a bill. Flour, meat, groceries, shoes, hats, and ten barrels of whiskey. Or, if the town would rather, $5,000 in cash. The merchants have already shipped most of their goods away, so there isn't much to hand over, and the raiders help themselves to whatever they can catch.

One of the things they catch belongs to the Pierces. The family owns a gentle, pretty mare that Tillie rides all the time, and her father has sent it out the Baltimore Pike with the hired boy, Sam. It doesn't get far enough. The raiders capture it. When Tillie cries and begs for her horse back, one of the Confederates tells her, "Sissy, what are you crying about? Go in the house and mind your business." Later she watches Rebel soldiers ride her mare up and down the street until it goes lame.

Her father tries the direct approach. He walks over to the Confederate commander, Colonel White, and asks for the horse. White has already heard about James Pierce. Someone has told him he's "A black Abolitionist" with two sons in the Union army. That someone, it turns out, is the hired boy's own sister. James Pierce comes home without the horse.

That evening, the raiders burn the railroad bridge east of town and the railroad cars with it, and then they march off toward York, about 25 miles northeast. Gettysburg has met the Confederate army for one afternoon, and so far it's cost the town a bridge and a girl's horse. Four days later, the next horsemen to ride up the street are wearing blue.

## Forgotten bouquets

Tuesday, June 30th, a little before noon. Union cavalry rides into Gettysburg and up Washington Street, heading out toward the Seminary. Tillie's book puts them at about 6,000 men. Modern counts are lower. She's standing at the corner of Washington and High Streets with her friends when her sister starts singing Our Union Forever. The trouble is, none of them know the verses. So they sing the chorus, and when it ends they sing it again, over and over, while the troopers ride past.

Those troopers belong to John Buford, and by the next morning they'll be holding the ridges west of town. Behind them, two whole armies are on the move. On June 28th, the Union Army of the Potomac got a new commander, George G. Meade, with Lee's army already loose in Pennsylvania. Now both sides are coming in along those ten roads, about 94,000 Union soldiers and somewhere between 71,000 and 75,000 Confederates.

In the Pierce parlor, the preparations are a little different. That evening, Tillie and Maggie fill a table with bouquets of flowers, ready to hand to the soldiers when the next regiments march through.

Wednesday, July 1st. Between nine and ten in the morning, the firing starts out toward Seminary Ridge, and then the cannon join in, and smoke starts to rise over the ridge. Tillie goes out to watch Union troops march through town toward the guns. Somewhere out on those ridges that morning, Major General John F. Reynolds is killed. Tillie will see what's left of him that same afternoon, or at least, she'll be told she has.

Near noon she hurries home, far too excited to eat. And the first thing she sees as she passes the parlor is the table, still covered in flowers. The soldiers have gone by, and nobody has given them a single bouquet.

Tillie isn't going to be in town much longer. Within about an hour, she's walking out of it.

## The road south

Right after the noon meal, a neighbor makes up her mind. Hettie Shriver lives a few doors along Baltimore Street with her two daughters, Sadie, who's 7, and Mollie, who's 5. Her husband is away with the army. She's decided to take the girls to her father's farm. Jacob Weikert's place sits on the Taneytown Road, on the eastern slope of Round Top, about three miles south of town. It seems safer out there than in town, so Tillie's parents let her go with them.

She packs nothing. In her words, "The only preparation I made for the departure, was to carry my best clothes down to the cellar, so that they might be safe when I returned; never thinking of taking any along, nor how long I would stay."

About one o'clock, they set out on foot, down Baltimore Street and through Evergreen Cemetery. On Cemetery Hill, Union soldiers are already planting cannon, and they tell the group to hurry, because the Rebels might start shelling them at any moment. If you're walking with this little group, you're hurrying two small girls past the guns, and off to the west, over Seminary Ridge, you can see the smoke and the shells bursting in the air.

They reach the Taneytown Road, and an ambulance wagon comes up behind them. Inside is the body of a dead soldier. Some of the men tell them it's General Reynolds. That's what she was told, and that's how she writes it.

A little way on, the mud gets so bad they have to stop. They wait at a small house, a story and a half high, on the west side of the road. The next day, that house becomes General Meade's headquarters. A Union soldier comes out and gets them onto a wagon that's already full. The wagon has no springs, and the mud comes almost up to the hubs.

That soldier turns up again later. A day or so after the battle, he finds Tillie and gives her a button from a Confederate coat, with a scrap of gray cloth still on it. When she sits down to write her book, she still has it.

Behind her, that afternoon, the Union line west of town collapses. The soldiers fall back through the streets of Gettysburg and up onto Cemetery Hill, where those cannon were going in. Confederates now hold the town, and they'll keep it until July 4th. Tillie's father, her mother and her sister are all still inside it.

The wagon jolts the rest of the way to the Weikert farm, and the family takes them in. It isn't long after they arrive that the first thing she'll never forget comes up the road at a gallop.

## Buckets and a barn

Union artillery comes racing up the Taneytown Road toward the battle. Then one of the caissons, the carts that haul the ammunition, blows up. A gunner is thrown clear and comes down in the wheat field next to the house. The men pick him up and carry him inside, right past Tillie. Her book describes him in one sentence. "As they pass by I see his eyes are blown out and his whole person seems to be one black mass."

Twenty-five years on, she still writes that moment in the present tense. And the first words out of this man, blinded and blackened, are about his Bible. He forgot to read it today, he says, and what will his poor wife and children say? They carry him upstairs and wrap him in cotton.

Meanwhile, infantry keeps coming up the Taneytown Road toward town, column after column, and the men are desperate for water. So Tillie finds a bucket. She runs to the spring on the north side, and she and the others carry water out to the moving column until the spring is empty. Then she switches to the pump on the south side and keeps going until night.

Late in the afternoon she meets her first wounded soldier, and it's nothing dramatic. He has his thumb tied up. She wrote down what he told her, and what she said back. "'Oh,' said he, 'this is nothing; you'll see worse than this before long.' 'Oh! I hope not,' I innocently replied."

He's right. Toward evening, the wounded start coming back from the fighting, limping, bandaged, some crawling, some carried on stretchers. Before night, the Weikert barn is full.

That evening, Tillie walks into the barn with Beckie Weikert, a daughter of the house. The wounded are crowded side by side. Some are groaning, some are crying, some are struggling and some are dying, and the attendants are trying to help them however they can. The two girls turn around and run back to the house, both of them in tears.

Down in the cellar kitchen, nurses are making beef tea for the wounded. They see the girls crying and cheer them up with funny stories. Then a chaplain gives Tillie her instructions. "Little girl, do all you can for the poor soldiers and the Lord will reward you."

So that's what she does. She's not a nurse, and she never pretends to be one. She carries water and bread, tears cloth for bandages, and sits with men who shouldn't be left alone. And all that night, back in Gettysburg, her family is somewhere inside a town full of Confederate soldiers, and there's no way for anyone to get a message to her.

The next morning, the men marching past the Weikert gate have something new to look at.

## The second day

Thursday, July 2nd. Lined up along the road, just outside the garden fence, is a row of coffins, rough wooden boxes waiting to be filled. The soldiers going by can't miss them. One man is told that there's no telling how soon he'll be put in one, and he answers that he'll consider himself very lucky if he gets one.

Tillie's back at her buckets, and every column she gives water to is marching toward town, toward her family. She keeps asking herself whether they're well, whether they're alive, whether she still has a home. About ten o'clock, artillery and ammunition trains roll in and fill the open ground east of the house.

That same morning, she watches a soldier crawling along on his hands and knees, completely worn out. An officer strikes him with his sword, three or four times. The man turns out to have sunstroke, and it takes hours of work to bring him round. His comrades say it plainly. "We will mark that officer for this." She never gives the officer's name.

A little later, three officers ride up to the gate on horseback. Tillie hands one of them a drink from her tin cup, and she apologizes for the cup. He tells her that's all right. Then the soldiers around the gate start cheering, he bows to her, and it's the cheering that tells her who he is. General George Meade, four days into his command of the entire Union army, has just been handed a drink by a butcher's daughter with a tin cup.

After that, a few field officers climb onto the Weikert roof through a trap door to watch the field, and they let Tillie come up and look through their field glasses. From up there, she gets the same view the officers get. She remembered it as grand and awful at the same time. But shortly before noon, much closer, she sees soldiers lying dead just behind the house. She's told they were picked off by Rebel sharpshooters up on Big Round Top.

All afternoon, Mrs. Weikert and her daughters bake bread. As each ovenful comes out, it's cut up and handed to the soldiers. Then, mid-afternoon, heavy cannon fire breaks out on the two Round Tops right behind the house, so suddenly that nobody knows what to do. The soldiers tell the family to get out, to a farmhouse about half a mile east across the fields. They run. And Mrs. Weikert remembers something she's left behind, so Mr. Weikert has to go back through the shelling to fetch it. It's her new quilted petticoat.

On the way across, Tillie sees a bright flash toward town, like sheet lightning, and she wonders out loud if it's Gettysburg burning. A soldier answers her, and she never forgot it. "'Yes, that is Gettysburg and all the people in it.' This made me cry, for I thought at once of the dear ones at home." The town isn't burning. But it'll be two more days before she knows that.

At the second farmhouse, the soldiers change their minds. Shells are going to land right where they're standing, they say, while at the Weikert house the shells will pass overhead. So everybody runs back the way they came. Inside, during the shelling, the blinded gunner has panicked, sprung out of bed and come rolling out of the enclosed stairway. After that, somebody stays with him. The bread is fine, though. The soldiers took it out of the oven while the family was gone.

Between four and five o'clock, soldiers around the house start saying the Rebels are on this side of Round Top, heading for the Taneytown Road. Tillie goes around the house, looks toward Round Top, and sees them coming. Then she hears a fife and drum from the other side of the barn, and the Pennsylvania Reserves come through at the double-quick, between the barn and Round Top. She watches Confederates fall as they climb a stone wall, and then the rest are driven back. She's hoping to spot one man in that charge. Her brother is in the First Regiment of the Reserves.

All afternoon, James Longstreet's Confederates have been attacking the Union left, at the Peach Orchard, the Wheatfield and Devil's Den. On Little Round Top, the hill just west of the Weikert farm, Union brigades got there in time, Strong Vincent's, with Joshua Chamberlain's 20th Maine, and then Stephen Weed's, with Charles Hazlett's battery. That hill is what everyone remembers about July 2nd. Weed is hit there by a Confederate sharpshooter. Lieutenant Hazlett bends over him to hear what he's saying and is killed too. Vincent is mortally wounded. Tillie only knows that fight from what she's told afterward. But before the night is over, part of what that hill cost is going to be carried down to the Weikert house.

Further up the line that evening, the 1st Minnesota charges to plug a gap. About 262 men go in. About 215 of them are killed or wounded, roughly 82 percent, one of the highest losses of any regiment in the war. Their colonel, William Colvill, is going to spend the next several months in a bed in the Pierces' house in town.

And that night, in the Weikert basement, an officer asks Tillie for a promise.

## The candle

By night, the wounded fill the Weikert house, the orchard and the yard. Tillie goes among them with bread. In a small room in the southeast corner of the basement lies a badly wounded officer, and his attendant has to go and get him some bread. So Tillie sits beside him and holds a candle until the attendant gets back.

Put yourself in that little room. The only light is the candle in Tillie's hand, and every room above you is full of wounded men. The officer looks up into her face, very earnestly, and asks if she'll promise to come back in the morning to see him. She promises. And when she leaves him that night, his last words to her are, "Now don't forget your promise."

Friday, July 3rd. The first thing Tillie does when she wakes up is hurry down to that little basement room. The officer is dead. His attendant is still at his side, and he tells her who the man was. "This is the body of General Weed; a New York man." She remembers the name because it sounds like Meade.

That's what she was told. Tillie insists that Stephen Weed, the brigade commander shot on Little Round Top, didn't die on the hill. Her book even says so in a caption, that he died at the house of Jacob Weikert. Several other accounts also say Weed died that night at the Weikert house, which fits a man she found dead in the morning.

She wrote, "I had kept my promise, but he was not there to greet me. I hope he greeted nearer and dearer faces than that of the unknown little girl on the battle-field of Gettysburg."

So a general whose brigade helped hold Little Round Top spent his last night in a farmhouse basement, and for part of it, the person holding the light was a schoolgirl who didn't learn his name until morning. And July 3rd has barely started. The worst thing she'll see at Gettysburg is waiting for her back at this house, at dusk.

## What the textbooks skip

Soldiers tell the family they've planted cannon on two sides of the house, and everyone has to leave. As they're climbing into the carriages, a shell comes screaming directly overhead. Tillie shrieks and jumps into the barn, where the wounded are lying, and one of them tells her that if it had hit her, she wouldn't have had time to jump. The shell lands in the next field and doesn't explode.

The carriages pass a line of Sixth Corps troops held in reserve, go a mile or so down the Taneytown Road, then cut across to the Baltimore Pike near Two Taverns. In a strip of woods on the way, soldiers tell them there was a cavalry fight right there an hour earlier. And that's where Tillie sees her first Confederate prisoners, a whole field full of them. A Union soldier gives her a hardtack biscuit. It's the first thing she's eaten all day. They end up at a farmhouse beyond the pike, packed with other people who've run from their homes.

So that's where Tillie Pierce is during the most famous hour of the battle. At around one o'clock, somewhere between 150 and 170 Confederate guns open fire on the Union center. Then between 12,500 and 15,000 Confederates cross the open fields in Pickett's Charge, with Pettigrew's and Trimble's men alongside Pickett's, and about half of them become casualties. That's the picture on the textbook page, and Tillie spends those hours out of sight of it, waiting beyond the pike.

When the roar dies down, toward evening, they drive back. Fences are thrown down everywhere, and knapsacks and blankets lie scattered across the fields. At the Weikert farm, the approaches to the house are covered with men, wounded, dying and dead, and the air is full of moaning. To reach the door, they have to pick their steps so they don't tread on anyone.

Inside, the house is full of wounded too. Mrs. Weikert's muslin and linen are torn up for bandages, and Tillie helps tear them. Out around the house, the surgeons have set up their amputating benches. One of them stands right under a front window, and that's where Tillie is standing as the light goes. This is what she saw from there. "I saw them lifting the poor men upon it, then the surgeons sawing and cutting off arms and legs, then again probing and picking bullets from the flesh."

Once a man is on the bench, a surgeon quickly puts a cattle horn over his mouth. She learns that's how they're giving the chloroform. It doesn't always work. When it fails, the men throw themselves about, shrieking. And still, some of the soldiers waiting their turn are begging to be taken next.

And there's one more thing she notices that evening. She wrote, "To the south of the house, and just outside of the yard, I noticed a pile of limbs higher than the fence. It was a ghastly sight!"

She called what she saw that evening butchery. She also wrote that she didn't want to dwell on pictures like these, and then she put them in her book anyway. The soldiers around the house tell the family the Rebels are whipped, "but at an awful sacrifice."

Back in town, her own family has been living through a battle of its own. She won't hear a word about it until a stranger proves he's talked to her mother.

## Linden trees

Saturday, July 4th. Cheering starts somewhere along the Union line and runs the whole length of it, from the Round Tops to Culp's Hill, where she used to picnic. That day, in heavy rain, Lee's army begins its retreat, with wagon trains of wounded stretching for many miles. On the same day, far to the west, Vicksburg surrenders.

At the Weikert farm, the work doesn't stop. The amputating, the nursing and the cooking go on for days, until the wounded can be moved to the corps hospitals. The dead are carried out in closed rough boxes and driven away.

There's a Union artillery captain at the farm who has promised Tillie he'll get through to her family. She isn't sure she believes him, so she sets him a test. If he's really been to her house, he can tell her how many linden trees stand out front. Every day, he fails. Then, on the evening of July 4th, he comes down to the basement kitchen with an answer she can't argue with. He doesn't care how many trees there are, he says, but her mother told him all about the stolen horse, and that Jennie Wade had been killed while baking bread for her sick sister.

Mary Virginia Wade, known as Jennie or Ginnie, was 20. On July 3rd, a stray bullet killed her while she was making bread in her sister's house on Baltimore Street, the Pierces' own street. She's usually counted as the only civilian killed directly by the fighting at Gettysburg. And for Tillie, it's the first word from home since Wednesday. Her mother is alive.

On Sunday, July 5th, Tillie climbs Little Round Top with Beckie Weikert and Lieutenant George Kitzmiller of the 1st Pennsylvania Reserves, the man Beckie will later marry. Kitzmiller has news about her brother. He fell very sick on the Peninsula, and he's in a hospital in Washington. He was never at Gettysburg at all.

A soldier from the Pennsylvania Bucktails shows them the bodies still lying among the rocks. Most of the Union dead have already been carried off, so the ones left are Confederates. Around them lie bloated horses, smashed cannon and caissons, and small arms by the thousands.

Tuesday, July 7th, in the forenoon, she finally goes home. The roads are impassable with mud, so she walks back across the fields with Mrs. Shriver and the two girls. The stench coming off the fields is sickening. Dead horses have swollen to nearly double their normal size. There's blood on the ground, fences and some buildings are gone, and in Evergreen Cemetery, where she used to watch the sun go down, monuments are broken and the arch of the gatehouse is shattered. A barricade and a battery sit across the Baltimore Pike.

At home, big bundles are packed and waiting, in case the family has to run. Tillie is still in the clothes she left in, and she's so covered in mud that when she walks in, her mother doesn't know her. Then she speaks. She wrote, "As soon as I spoke my mother ran to me, and clasping me in her arms, said: 'Why, my dear child, is that you? How glad I am to have you home again without any harm having befallen you!'" Down in the cellar, on the woodpile, her best clothes are exactly where she put them six days before.

Then she hears what happened at home. On July 1st, her father was helping wounded soldiers at warehouses in the north of town, hunting for liquor to ease their pain. On his way back, as the Union army retreated, a Confederate yelled at him, "What are you doing with that gun in your hand?" He shouted back, "I have no gun!" The man aimed and fired anyway. James Pierce dropped, and heard the zip of the bullet. A squad of five Confederates then marched him to his own porch, where he told them, "Now boys, I am home, and I am going to stay here."

Inside, he found five sick and wounded Union soldiers who'd taken refuge in the house, two of them badly wounded captains, and Tillie's mother nursed them through the battle. One was Corporal Michael O'Brien of the 143rd Pennsylvania, with a ball in his back and his right arm shattered at the elbow. One night, when Confederates came up from the cellar, he hid under a curtained settee.

The family lived in the cellar by day. Confederate sharpshooters were firing from the garret of the house next door, through holes knocked in its south wall, and the Union return fire left 17 bullet holes in the Pierces' upper balcony. Bullets came through the kitchen, right over the stove. Tillie's mother refused to go up and cook for the sharpshooters, and at night they raided her cellar for milk, cream and preserves.

Across the street, on the very first day, a shell hit the wall right beside the window where the Methodist minister's daughter, Laura Bergstresser, was watching. She wasn't hurt. Later, a shell was set into that wall to mark the spot. And on Saturday morning, that same minister rang the bell to announce the rascals had gone. Tillie's father ran out in his stocking feet, picked up a musket, rounded up several Confederate stragglers and handed them over to Union soldiers. Only afterward did he find out the musket was empty.

Tillie is home. The battle is going to stay in her house for months.

## What she kept

More than 20,000 wounded men are left behind in and around a town of about 2,400 people. Churches, schools, barns and homes all become hospitals, including the rooms of Tillie's own school. About a mile east of town, a large tent hospital opens, Camp Letterman, with help from the Christian and Sanitary Commissions, and for weeks Tillie goes out to visit. A woman lodging at the Pierce house, Mrs. Greenly, sits out there and watches her son die after an amputation. His last words are, "Mother! Dear Mother!"

The Confederate dead are laid in trenches, three rows deep, and most of them stay there until they're dug up and reburied in the South in the early 1870s. Next door to the Pierce lot, a long bowling alley is used for embalming bodies.

In the Pierce house itself, Colonel William Colvill of the 1st Minnesota is nursed for several months. He's so tall they have to lengthen the bed. He's been hit in the right ankle and the shoulder, and he refuses to let them take his foot. If the foot has to go, he says, he'll go with it. He leaves on crutches for St. Paul. Tillie writes that of his 262 men, only 47 remained after their charge.

On November 19th, 1863, the Soldiers' National Cemetery is dedicated, and Abraham Lincoln speaks for about two minutes, around 270 words. That speech is the Gettysburg most of us learn in school. The same month, Camp Letterman closes.

Tillie marries a lawyer, Horace Alleman, on September 28th, 1871, in the old College Church on Chambersburg Street, a church that had been a battle hospital too. They make their home in Selinsgrove, in another part of Pennsylvania. On July 1st, 1888, 25 years to the day after the battle began, Corporal O'Brien comes back to the Pierce house with his son. Tillie's mother and sister are both dead by then. Her father is in his 83rd year, and the bullet holes are still in the balcony.

The next year, at about 40, Tillie publishes her book in New York, through a publisher called W. Lake Borland. Every word of hers you've heard in this story comes from that book. By then she's watched old commanders from both armies come back to the field, Longstreet, Gordon, Hooker, Sickles and Slocum among them, and she's seen Union and Confederate veterans shake hands on the ground where they fought. She ends it by calling it simply what a girl saw and heard. Tillie Pierce Alleman dies on March 15th, 1914, and she's buried in Selinsgrove. The double brick house still stands at 301 and 303 Baltimore Street, and it's known now as the Tillie Pierce House.

She warned her readers that she only saw part of the battle. The part she saw was a farmhouse window on the evening of July 3rd, a bench underneath it, and a pile by the fence south of the house. She was 15. Her parents had sent her out there because it seemed safer than town.
