# Research notes — What a 15-Year-Old Girl Saw at Gettysburg Was Too Brutal for Textbooks

Single eyewitness: Tillie (Matilda) Pierce, "At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. A True Narrative", by "Mrs. Tillie (Pierce) Alleman" (copyright entered 1888, published 1889). Whole memoir read (archive.org OCR, Brigham Young University copy). Quotes below are checked against that text; only OCR noise was fixed (e.g. "lifdng" -> "lifting", "nioaninQfS" -> "moanings", "fori^ot" -> "forgot"). Original 1889 spellings kept ("boquets", "to-day").

VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim from her 1889 memoir; only obvious OCR errors fixed, never rephrased)
- "The horrors of war are fully known only to those who have seen and heard them. It was my lot to see and hear only part, but it was sufficient." — context: her introduction, written about 25 years after the battle.
- "What a horrible sight! There they were, human beings! clad almost in rags, covered with dust, riding wildly, pell-mell down the hill toward our home! shouting, yelling most unearthly, cursing, brandishing their revolvers, and firing right and left." — context: Friday 26 June 1863, first Confederate raid; she ran home from school and peeped through the shutters of her Baltimore Street house.
- "I hurried back, and the first thing that met my gaze as I passed the parlor was the table full of flowers. The soldiers had passed and we had not given them the boquets." — context: 1 July, about noon, back home after watching Union troops march toward the guns west of town.
- "The only preparation I made for the departure, was to carry my best clothes down to the cellar, so that they might be safe when I returned; never thinking of taking any along, nor how long I would stay." — context: 1 July, just before leaving town on foot with her neighbor Mrs. Schriver (about 1 p.m.).
- "while traveling along, were overtaken by an ambulance wagon in which was the body of a dead soldier. Some of the men told us that it was the body of General Reynolds" — context: 1 July afternoon, on the Taneytown Road south of town, fleeing toward the Weikert farm.
- "As they pass by I see his eyes are blown out and his whole person seems to be one black mass." — context: 1 July afternoon, Weikert farm; an artillery caisson exploded and threw a gunner into the wheat field; he was carried past her into the house.
- "Oh dear! I forgot to read my Bible to-day! What will my poor wife and children say?" — context: the same blinded artilleryman's first words as he was carried past her (1 July, Weikert farm).
- "Obtaining a bucket, I hastened to the spring, and there, with others, carried water to the moving column until the spring was empty." — context: 1 July afternoon, Weikert farm; thirsty infantry marching up the Taneytown Road. She then used the pump until night.
- "'Oh,' said he, 'this is nothing; you'll see worse than this before long.' 'Oh! I hope not,' I innocently replied." — context: late afternoon 1 July, the first wounded soldier she met (his thumb tied up) at the Weikert farm.
- "There were the groaning and crying, the struggling and dying, crowded side by side, while attendants sought to aid and relieve them as best they could." — context: evening of 1 July, the Weikert barn, which she entered with Beckie Weikert; both ran back to the house weeping.
- "Were they well? Were they alive? Did I still have a home?" — context: morning of 2 July, carrying water to troops hurrying toward town, with no news of her family in Gettysburg.
- "One of the men near by, being addressed with the remark that there was no telling how soon he would be put in one of them, replied: 'I will consider myself very lucky if I get one.'" — context: 2 July forenoon; rough coffins ("rough boxes") had been stacked along the road outside the Weikert garden fence.
- "I asked him to please excuse the tin cup I then held in my hand. He replied: 'Certainly; that is all right.'" — context: 2 July, at the Weikert gate/pump; the rider was General Meade, as the soldiers' cheers told her afterwards.
- "'Yes, that is Gettysburg and all the people in it.' This made me cry, for I thought at once of the dear ones at home." — context: 2 July afternoon, running across the fields under the cannonade; she saw a flash like sheet lightning toward town and a soldier said this.
- "The poor man looked so earnestly into my face, saying: 'Will you promise me to come back in the morning to see me.'" — context: night of 2 July, small basement room of the Weikert house; she held a candle beside a badly wounded officer while his attendant fetched bread.
- "I hastened down to the little basement room, and as I entered, the soldier lay there — dead. His faithful attendant was still at his side." — context: morning of 3 July, the first thing she did on waking.
- "I had kept my promise, but he was not there to greet me. I hope he greeted nearer and dearer faces than that of the unknown little girl on the battle-field of Gettysburg." — context: 3 July morning; the attendant then told her the dead man was General Weed.
- "My child, if that had hit you, you would not have had time to jump." — context: 3 July morning; a shell screamed overhead as the family boarded carriages to flee; she shrieked and leapt into the barn, and a wounded man there said this.
- "The air was filled with moanings, and groanings. As we passed on toward the house, we were compelled to pick our steps in order that we might not tread on the prostrate bodies." — context: evening of 3 July, returning to the Weikert farm after the fighting died down.
- "I saw them lifting the poor men upon it, then the surgeons sawing and cutting off arms and legs, then again probing and picking bullets from the flesh." — context: evening of 3 July, from a front window of the Weikert house; an amputating bench stood right under it.
- "I saw the surgeons hastily put a cattle horn over the mouths of the wounded ones, after they were placed upon the bench." — context: same evening; she learned this was how they gave chloroform, and it did not always work.
- "To the south of the house, and just outside of the yard, I noticed a pile of limbs higher than the fence. It was a ghastly sight!" — context: evening of 3 July, Weikert farm.
- "I don't care how many trees there are, but to convince you, your mother told me all about your horse being stolen, and that Jennie Wade had been killed while baking bread for her sick sister." — context: evening of 4 July, basement kitchen; a kind Union artillery captain had finally reached her home and proved it.
- "From the summit of Little Round Top, surrounded by the wrecks of battle, we gazed upon the valley of death beneath. The view there spread out before us was terrible to contemplate!" — context: 5 July, climbing Little Round Top with Beckie Weikert and Lieutenant Kitzmiller.
- "As soon as I spoke my mother ran to me, and clasping me in her arms, said: 'Why, my dear child, is that you? How glad I am to have you home again without any harm having befallen you!'" — context: 7 July, arriving home on Baltimore Street, so muddy her mother did not recognize her at first.

VERIFIED FACTS (from her memoir; one line each)
- The book: "At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. A True Narrative", signed "Mrs. Tillie (Pierce) Alleman"; copyright entered 1888, published 1889; she says she wrote it "as a legacy to my own offspring" and not to be "classed among the heroines". Her age is not stated in the book (born 11 March 1848, so 15 in July 1863 — see WIDER CONTEXT); a chaplain calls her "Little girl" and she calls herself "the unknown little girl on the battle-field of Gettysburg".
- Gettysburg was her native town; before the war it was quiet, known mainly for its Lutheran College and Theological Seminary. As a child she picnicked on the boulders of Culp's Hill, drank at Spangler's Spring, went berrying along the lane, and watched sunsets over South Mountain and the Round Tops from Evergreen Cemetery.
- Her home: a "double brick house" with a row of linden trees in front, on Baltimore Street at the corner of the "side street" (Breckenridge Street). She lived there with her father, mother and a sister; Confederates were told her father "had two sons in the Union Army".
- School: the Young Ladies' Seminary at the corner of High and Washington Streets (teacher Mrs. Eyster), where she later graduated; wounded soldiers were later placed in its rooms. She was married in the "old College Church" on Chambersburg Street, itself a battle hospital, and for many years lived "in another part of the State".
- Before the battle: on rumors of raids, older townsmen drilled "armed to the teeth" with rusty guns, swords, pitchforks and pick-axes; the town's Black residents fled down Breckenridge and Baltimore Streets to the woods on Culp's Hill, convinced capture meant destruction.
- Friday 26 June 1863 (first raid): during Friday afternoon literary exercises at her school, a "dark, dense mass" came from the Theological Seminary; Mrs. Eyster told the girls "Children, run home as quickly as you can"; Confederate horsemen rode down the hill toward her home firing revolvers; infantry then ransacked the town. The raiders demanded flour, meat, groceries, shoes, hats and ten barrels of whiskey, or $5,000 instead; merchants had already shipped goods away. That evening they burned the railroad bridge east of town and the cars, then left for York (about 25 miles northeast).
- The family's horse (a gentle, pretty mare Tillie often rode), sent out the Baltimore Pike with the hired boy Sam, was captured; when Tillie wept and pleaded, a Confederate said "Sissy, what are you crying about? Go in the house and mind your business." She later saw Rebels riding the mare up and down the street until it went lame.
- Her father asked the Confederate commander, Colonel White, for the horse back; White said he had heard her father was "A black Abolitionist" with two sons in the Union Army (information given by the hired boy's sister); he came back without the horse.
- Tuesday 30 June, before noon: Union cavalry (Buford's) rode through town up Washington Street and out toward the Seminary; she says "about six thousand men" (her figure; modern counts are lower). With friends at the corner of Washington and High Streets her sister started "Our Union Forever"; they only knew the chorus and kept repeating it.
- 1 July: she and her sister had prepared a table of bouquets for the troops the evening before; firing toward Seminary Ridge began "between nine and ten o'clock", then cannon and smoke; she came home near noon too excited to eat.
- 1 July, after the noon meal: neighbor Mrs. Schriver, whose husband was serving in the Union army, decided to take her two children to her father Jacob Weikert's farm on the Taneytown Road at the eastern slope of Round Top, about three miles south; Tillie's parents let her go because it seemed safer than town.
- About 1 p.m. they walked out Baltimore Street through Evergreen Cemetery; on Cemetery Hill Union soldiers already planting cannon told them to hurry because the Rebels might shell them; she saw smoke and shells bursting over Seminary Ridge.
- On the Taneytown Road, mud stopped them at a small one-and-a-half-story house on the west side of the road that "on the following day became General Meade's headquarters"; a Union soldier got them onto an already-full springless wagon; "The mud was almost up to the hubs." A day or so after the battle the same soldier found her and gave her a Confederate coat button with a piece of gray cloth, which she still kept.
- At the Weikert farm (1 July afternoon): Union artillery raced past toward the battle; a caisson exploded; the blinded, blackened gunner was carried upstairs and wrapped in cotton. On 2 July, terrified by the cannonade, he sprang from bed and rolled out of the enclosed stairway; after that someone stayed with him.
- Water: she carried buckets from the spring on the north side of the house until it was empty, then from the pump on the south side, until night. Late on 1 July the wounded arrived (limping, bandaged, crawling, on stretchers); before night the barn was full. In the cellar kitchen nurses made beef tea and cheered the weeping girls with funny stories; a chaplain told her "Little girl, do all you can for the poor soldiers and the Lord will reward you."
- 2 July morning: about 10 a.m. artillery and ammunition trains filled the space east of the house; coffins were lined up along the road; an officer struck a worn-out soldier crawling on hands and knees with his sword "three or four times" (the man had sunstroke and was revived after hours of work); comrades said "We will mark that officer for this." 2 July shortly before noon: she saw soldiers lying dead just behind the house, "picked off", she was told, by Rebel sharpshooters on Big Round Top.
- 2 July: she handed General Meade a drink from a tin cup at the gate (three officers on horseback; the soldiers cheered him; he bowed to her). Field officers then went onto the Weikert roof through a trap door and let her look through their field glasses: "a grand and awful spectacle". All afternoon Mrs. Weikert and her daughters baked bread that was cut up and handed to soldiers.
- 2 July mid-afternoon: sudden heavy cannonade on the Round Tops behind the house; soldiers sent the family running to a farmhouse about half a mile east, then sent them back (shells would land there but pass over the Weikert house); Mr. Weikert went back through the fire for his wife's new quilted petticoat; soldiers had taken the bread out of the oven for them.
- 2 July, between 4 and 5 p.m.: soldiers warned the Rebels were on "this side of Round Top" heading for the Taneytown Road; she saw them coming; with fife and drum the Pennsylvania Reserves charged at the double-quick between the barn and Round Top, and she saw Confederates fall climbing a stone wall before being driven back. She hoped to see her brother, who was in the First Regiment of the Reserves.
- Night of 2 July: wounded filled the house, orchard and yard; she carried bread to soldiers and sat with a candle beside a wounded man in the little room in the southeast corner of the basement; his last words to her: "Now don't forget your promise."
- 3 July morning: the man was dead; the attendant said "This is the body of General Weed; a New York man." She writes that Weed was mortally wounded on Little Round Top while helping at Hazlett's battery, that Lieutenant Hazlett was killed bending over him to hear his last message, and that Weed died at the Weikert house, not on the hill (the book's caption: "Died at the house of Jacob Weikert"). She remembered the name because it sounded like Meade.
- 3 July morning: soldiers said cannon were planted on two sides of the house; the family left in carriages; a shell landed in the next field without exploding; they passed Sixth Corps troops held in reserve, went a mile or so down the Taneytown Road, then crossed to the Baltimore Pike near Two Taverns; in a strip of woods where a cavalry fight had happened an hour earlier she saw her first Confederate prisoners, "a whole field filled with them". 3 July: a Union soldier gave her a hardtack biscuit, her first food that day; they waited at a farmhouse beyond the pike full of other refugees, and drove back in the evening when the roar died down: fences thrown down, knapsacks and blankets everywhere.
- 3 July evening at the Weikert farm: bodies of wounded, dying and dead at the approaches; the house full of wounded; Mrs. Weikert's muslin and linen torn into bandages; amputating benches placed around the house; chloroform given through a cattle horn sometimes failed, and men threw themselves about shrieking; some soldiers "fairly begged to be taken next"; a pile of limbs higher than the fence south of the house; she called it "cruel butchery". Soldiers said the Rebels were whipped "but at an awful sacrifice".
- 4 July: cheering ran along the Union line from the Round Tops to Culp's Hill; amputating, nursing and cooking went on at the farm for days until the wounded were moved to corps hospitals; the dead were carried out in closed rough boxes and driven away.
- A Union artillery captain promised to reach her family; she tested him by asking how many linden trees stood in front of her house, and he failed every day; on the evening of 4 July he came back with news from her mother about the stolen horse and Jennie Wade's death.
- 5 July: she climbed Little Round Top with Beckie Weikert and Lt. George Kitzmiller of the 1st Pennsylvania Reserves (whom Beckie later married); he told her her brother had fallen very sick on the Peninsula and was in hospital in Washington, so was not at the battle. A Pennsylvania "Buck Tail" showed them bodies among the rocks; most Union dead had been removed, those left were Confederates; she saw "Dead soldiers, bloated horses, shattered cannon and caissons, thousands of small arms."
- Tuesday 7 July, forenoon: she walked home through the fields with Mrs. Schriver and her children (roads impassable with mud); "the stench arising from the fields of carnage was most sickening"; dead horses "swollen to almost twice their natural size"; bloodstains; fences and some buildings gone; broken monuments in Evergreen Cemetery and its gatehouse arch shattered; a barricade and battery on the Baltimore Pike.
- At home: large bundles were packed in case the family had to flee; her mother did not recognize her at first under the mud (she had only the clothes she left in); her best clothes were still in the cellar on the woodpile, where she had put them six days before.
- Her father's battle (told to her): on 1 July he helped wounded soldiers at warehouses in the north of town and searched for liquor to ease their pain; on his way home as the Union army retreated, a Confederate shouted "What are you doing with that gun in your hand?", he answered "I have no gun!", and the man aimed and fired — he dropped and heard the "zip" of the bullet; a squad of five then marched him to his own porch ("Now boys, I am home, and I am going to stay here").
- Hidden soldiers: inside, her father found five sick and wounded Union soldiers who had taken refuge (two of them badly wounded captains); her mother nursed them through the battle. One was Corporal Michael O'Brien, Co. A, 143rd Pennsylvania (I Corps), wounded 1 July (ball in the back, right arm shattered at the elbow), who hid under a curtained settee when Confederates came up from the cellar one night; he returned on 1 July 1888 with his son, when her mother and sister were already dead and her father was in his 83rd year. Another was a captain of the 6th Wisconsin.
- The house under fire: the family lived in the cellar by day; Confederate sharpshooters fired from the garret of the adjoining neighbor's house through holes knocked in its south wall (she saw one fall back dead); Union return fire left "seventeen bullet holes" in the Pierces' upper balcony (still visible in 1888); bullets passed through the kitchen over the stove, and her mother refused to come up and cook for the sharpshooters, who raided the cellar at night for milk, cream and preserves.
- Neighbors: on 1 July a shell struck the wall beside the window where Laura Bergstresser, the Methodist minister's daughter across the street, was watching; she was unhurt (a shell was later set in the wall to mark the spot). On Saturday morning (4 July) that minister rang the bell: "Don't you think! the rascals have gone?" Her father ran out in his stocking feet, picked up a musket, rounded up several Confederate stragglers and handed them to Union soldiers; then found the musket was empty.
- Colonel William Colvill of the 1st Minnesota (very tall; the bed had to be extended) was nursed in the Pierce house for several months after the battle, wounded in the right ankle and shoulder; he refused to have his foot amputated ("if his foot must go he would go too") and left on crutches for St. Paul. She writes that of 262 men of his regiment only 47 remained after their charge on 2 July (book caption: 215 killed and wounded of 262). Nurse Walter S. Reed (Co. G) gave her a musket and bayonet he had bought; a Provost Marshal confiscated it, then sent it back because she was "such a good Union girl".
- After: for weeks she visited the large tent "General Hospital" about one mile east of town (Christian and Sanitary Commissions); a lodger, Mrs. Greenly, watched her son die there after an amputation, his last words "Mother! Dear Mother!"; Confederate dead lay in trenches "three rows deep" before being dug up and taken South; a long ten-pin (bowling) alley next to the Pierce lot was used for embalming bodies.
- Years later she watched Union and Confederate veterans (Longstreet, Gordon, Hooker, Sickles, Slocum and others) meet on the field: "I have seen the Blue and the Gray clasp hands". The book's last line of the story: "This ends what a girl saw and heard of the battle."

WIDER CONTEXT (well documented; disputed numbers given as ranges)
- The campaign: Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia invaded Pennsylvania in June 1863. George G. Meade took command of the Union Army of the Potomac on 28 June, three days before the battle.
- Strengths: about 94,000 Union (often given as 93,921) against about 72,000 Confederates (estimates roughly 71,000-75,000).
- Casualties over 1-3 July: the commonly cited total is about 51,000 killed, wounded, captured or missing (estimates about 46,000-51,000, because Confederate losses are disputed: Union about 23,000, Confederate about 23,000-28,000). The deadliest battle of the Civil War.
- Gettysburg had about 2,400 inhabitants in 1863, a market town where about ten roads met. Union and Confederate armies converged on it along those roads.
- The 26 June raid was by Jubal Early's Confederate division; the horsemen were Lt. Col. Elijah V. White's 35th Battalion Virginia Cavalry ("Colonel White" in her book). The 26th Pennsylvania Emergency Militia, with a company of local students and townsmen, skirmished with them west of town.
- The fear of Gettysburg's Black residents was justified: during this campaign Confederate troops seized free Black people in southern Pennsylvania and sent them south into slavery.
- 1 July: John Buford's Union cavalry held the ridges west of town; Maj. Gen. John F. Reynolds was killed in the morning; in the afternoon the Union line collapsed and retreated through the streets to Cemetery Hill. Confederates held the town until 4 July, which is why the Pierces hid soldiers in their house.
- 2 July: Longstreet attacked the Union left (Peach Orchard, Wheatfield, Devil's Den). Little Round Top, just west of the Weikert farm, was occupied in time by Strong Vincent's brigade (with Joshua Chamberlain's 20th Maine) and then Stephen Weed's brigade and Charles Hazlett's battery. Weed was hit by a Confederate sharpshooter and mortally wounded; Hazlett was killed bending over him; Vincent was also mortally wounded. Several accounts say Weed died that night at the Weikert house, which fits her finding him dead the next morning.
- That evening the 1st Minnesota (Col. William Colvill) charged to plug a gap in the line: about 262 men engaged, about 215 killed or wounded (about 82%), one of the highest regimental loss rates of the war.
- 3 July: after a cannonade beginning around 1 p.m. (roughly 150-170 Confederate guns), about 12,500-15,000 Confederates crossed open fields in Pickett's Charge (Pickett-Pettigrew-Trimble) against the Union center; about half became casualties. Tillie was not watching it: by her account she was at a farmhouse beyond the Baltimore Pike that afternoon.
- Jennie (Mary Virginia "Ginnie") Wade, 20, was killed on 3 July by a stray bullet while making bread in her sister's house on Baltimore Street; she is usually cited as the only civilian killed directly by the fighting.
- Aftermath: on 4 July Lee began his retreat in heavy rain, his wagon trains of wounded stretching many miles (the same day Vicksburg surrendered in the West). More than 20,000 wounded were left in and around a town of about 2,400; churches, schools, barns and homes became hospitals. The tent hospital about a mile east of town that Tillie visited was Camp Letterman (opened in July 1863, closed in November 1863).
- 19 November 1863: the Soldiers' National Cemetery was dedicated and Abraham Lincoln gave the Gettysburg Address (about 270 words, about two minutes). Most Confederate dead were exhumed and reburied in the South in the early 1870s.
- Tillie's own life: born 11 March 1848 (15 at the battle); father James Pierce (a butcher, shop and home at today's 301-303 Baltimore Street, now known as the Tillie Pierce House), mother Margaret, sister Margaret "Maggie", brothers James and William in the Union army. Married lawyer Horace P. Alleman on 28 September 1871 (Christ Lutheran Church, the "College Church"); lived in Selinsgrove, Pennsylvania; published her memoir in 1889 (W. Lake Borland, New York) at about 40; died 15 March 1914, buried in Selinsgrove.
- Hettie Shriver (her neighbor "Mrs. Schriver") lived at 309 Baltimore Street with daughters Sadie (7) and Mollie (5); the Shriver House Museum says Confederate sharpshooters used its attic. Tillie does not name the neighbor's garret she describes, so do not state on screen that it was the Shriver house.

STORY SHAPE (chronological, about 38 min, seen through her eyes)
1. Cold open + a quiet town (0-4 min): 3 July evening, a 15-year-old at a farmhouse window above an amputating bench, then rewind: Gettysburg in 1863, the butcher's daughter on Baltimore Street, school, picnics on Culp's Hill; the book she wrote 25 years later.
2. "The Rebels are coming!" (4-8): rumors, old men drilling with pitchforks, the 26 June raid seen through the shutters, the stolen mare, "Sissy", her father called "a black Abolitionist", the burning railroad bridge.
3. Blue cavalry and forgotten bouquets (8-11): 30 June, Buford's troopers and "Our Union Forever"; 1 July morning, guns over Seminary Ridge, the table of flowers never given.
4. The road south (11-15): best clothes to the cellar, cannon on Cemetery Hill, Reynolds' body in an ambulance, the house that becomes Meade's headquarters, the wagon in the mud, arrival at the Weikert farm under Round Top.
5. Water and the first wounded (15-19): the exploding caisson and the blinded gunner, buckets until the spring runs dry, "you'll see worse than this", the barn full of dying men, beef tea, the chaplain.
6. 2 July — the battle comes to the farm (19-24): coffins by the fence, the sword beating, a drink for General Meade, the view from the roof, dead men behind the house, the cannonade and the flight across the fields, "Gettysburg and all the people in it", the Pennsylvania Reserves' charge (context: Little Round Top, Weed, Hazlett, 1st Minnesota).
7. The candle (24-28): night of 2 July, the promise in the basement room; 3 July morning, the dead man is General Weed.
8. Too brutal for textbooks (28-32): the shell over the carriages, flight toward the Baltimore Pike, prisoners and hardtack (context: Pickett's Charge, out of her sight); the return at dusk, bodies at the door, the amputating bench, the cattle horn, the pile of limbs.
9. The valley of death and the way home (32-35): 4 July cheers and rough boxes, the captain and Jennie Wade, Little Round Top on 5 July, the 7 July walk through the stench, her mother not recognizing her, the clothes on the woodpile; what the family lived through (her father shot at, five hidden soldiers, bullets over the stove).
10. What remained (35-38): the tent hospital and Mrs. Greenly's son, Colonel Colvill and the musket, Lincoln's address, Corporal O'Brien's return in 1888, the book in 1889, "the Blue and the Gray clasp hands", "This ends what a girl saw and heard of the battle."

RULES
- Quote Tillie verbatim only from VERIFIED QUOTES; never invent dialogue. Other words she reports (soldiers, her mother, Meade) only as they appear in VERIFIED QUOTES or VERIFIED FACTS.
- Graphic details stated plainly, never relished: say what she saw, then move on (she herself writes "I do not desire to dwell upon such pictures").
- She wrote 25 years later: present what she was told as told to her (Reynolds' body, Weed's identity, sharpshooters on Big Round Top, "about six thousand" cavalry). Do not give her things she did not see: she did not watch Pickett's Charge, the Little Round Top fight itself or Jennie Wade's death.
- She was not a nurse: she carried water and bread, tore bandages, held a candle, sat with the wounded.
- Use only the numbers above (ranges where marked); no invented statistics.
- Do not repeat her dialect rendering of Black townspeople's speech; if they appear, explain that their fear was real (Confederates seized free Black Pennsylvanians in this campaign).
- The officer who beat the sunstruck soldier is unnamed: keep him unnamed.
