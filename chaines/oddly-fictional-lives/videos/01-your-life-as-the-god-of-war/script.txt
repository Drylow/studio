You are about to trade your life to a god because the man standing over you is about to end it.

Your army is losing. You have a wife and daughter waiting at home, and right now, you will agree to almost anything to see them again. Ares answers. But his help comes with orders you cannot take back. You will survive this battle, become the God of War, and eventually have to explain to your son why that was the worst thing that ever happened to you.

The bargain starts with your enemies collapsing around you. Ares gives you the Blades of Chaos, weapons attached to chains that bind you to his service. You can swing beyond the reach of an ordinary sword and tear through the men who were winning a moment ago. Your soldiers have a commander again. You have a god who expects you to keep working.

Your name is Kratos, a Spartan commander. Your wife, Lysandra, wants you to stop chasing war, and your daughter, Calliope, gives you a reason to return. You expect the blades to let you keep winning and eventually go home.

Ares wants a warrior who will follow him without hesitation. You have already proved that you are willing to kill. The difficulty, from his point of view, is that you still have people you care about. Your family could make you question an order. They could make you put the blades down and go home. So Ares arranges a situation in which you will lose them by obeying him.

You attack a village and enter a temple in the middle of the killing. You move as you have moved through other fights, trusting the weapons and the rage that have kept you alive. By the time you recognize your wife and daughter, you have killed them. Ares has tricked you into destroying the people you expected his bargain to help you return to.

There is no useful command to give here. You cannot order your soldiers to fix it, and another victory will not bring them back. Their ashes become bound to your skin, leaving you with the appearance that earns you the name Ghost of Sparta. People can recognize you before you speak. You will have to carry the reason for that name into every place you visit.

You turn against Ares and eventually offer your service to the gods of Olympus. You serve them for ten years, taking on the monsters and dangers they put in your path. You can keep moving through a fight and follow a task to its end. Sleeping afterward is where things become difficult again, because the nightmares continue even when you have done everything you were asked to do.

Athena eventually gives you a mission that might settle both problems. Ares is attacking Athens, and she wants you to kill him. You will finally have permission to go after the god who used you. The gods also offer forgiveness. You hear that promise and imagine being able to close your eyes without returning to the temple. But first, you need to survive a fight against your former master.

You seek out Pandora's Box and use its power to face Ares. You defeat him, removing the god who made the bargain and caused your family's deaths. For a moment, you have done the thing you spent years wanting to do. Now you can ask Olympus to keep its promise. Olympus grants the forgiveness it promised. That night, the same memories still wake you.

You still wake from the same nightmares. Your family is gone, and you remember being the person holding the blades. When you despair, Athena intervenes and offers you Ares' vacant position. You become the God of War and begin ruling from his throne on Olympus.

You support Sparta's conquests, giving yourself something to focus on. Olympus sees a problem that keeps growing. Athena warns you, and you ignore her. The other gods watch you throw your support behind Sparta and see someone who will not accept their limits. Becoming one of them has done very little to make them trust you.

Zeus moves against you during the fighting at Rhodes. The Blade of Olympus becomes part of the trap, and you surrender your divine power into it. You have been relying on that power to push through danger. Now you are vulnerable again, and Zeus kills you. He has made sure you cannot survive the encounter.

Gaia helps you escape death. You defeat the Sisters of Fate and return to confront Zeus. Athena steps between you when you are about to strike. She dies protecting him, and you learn that Zeus is your father. That makes the conflict even more personal, but it does not make you stop. You now have another family relationship defined by violence, another death to carry, and enough anger to begin a war against Olympus itself.

When you attack the Greek gods, the consequences spread beyond the people you are fighting. The world around them falls into chaos. The people of Greece have to survive the destruction you are causing. Yet you continue toward Zeus. You have spent so long treating him as the answer that stopping feels impossible.

You finally kill him. After everything it took to reach that moment, Greece has been devastated. The power of hope that came from Pandora's Box is still within you. You release it rather than give Athena what she wants. Your family is gone, and you have no way to return to the life you had there.

Eventually, your life continues in the Norse lands. You live in a cabin with Faye and your son, Atreus. Faye's Leviathan Axe becomes your weapon. You keep your past from the boy. It is easy to imagine how that starts. He asks about a scar, and you give him a short answer. He asks about where you came from, and you find something else that needs doing. You want him to know you as his father before he learns what other people called you.

Faye understands Atreus in ways you are still learning. After she dies, you have to manage the relationship without her. Her final request is for you and your son to scatter her ashes from the highest peak in all the realms. You prepare for the trip and take responsibility for carrying her ashes. Atreus walks beside you, and you keep struggling to find something useful to say about losing his mother.

Atreus carries a bow and has questions even when you need him to follow an order. You give him firm orders because you know what the trail can do to a child who hesitates. He is already grieving his mother, so every correction can feel like another failure. You need him to be ready because the creatures outside will not wait for you to become better at explaining things.

Baldur arrives at your home before you can settle into that task. You fight him, but he keeps coming through punishment that should have stopped him. Your cabin can no longer give your son the safety you wanted it to provide. You set off into the realms, where protecting Atreus means facing gods again and letting other people see enough of you to help.

The dwarf brothers, Brok and Sindri, work on your equipment. Freya helps you travel between realms. Mimir offers knowledge you need to reach your destination. After you free him and his severed head is revived, he joins the journey at your belt. You will have advice available even when you would prefer the walk to remain quiet.

The mountain also turns out to be the wrong mountain. You reach the highest peak in Midgard and learn that Faye's request points to Jotunheim instead. Getting there requires more help and more dangerous travel. Each delay keeps Atreus near the world of gods you hoped to keep from him. You keep preparing for attacks and dangerous crossings. You still haven't told Atreus what kind of person he is or why you keep avoiding his questions.

Atreus becomes seriously ill after his own divine rage overwhelms him. You carry him to Freya, and she explains that his condition is connected to his godhood and his ignorance of it. Freya has identified a mistake you made while trying to raise him. Atreus is unconscious, and your decision to hide his nature has left him needing treatment you cannot provide yourself.

Freya needs the heart of a creature from Helheim to treat him. Your frost axe will not serve you well there. The weapons you need are back at the cabin, where you have kept the Blades of Chaos out of your new life. You have to return, uncover them, and wrap those chains around your arms again. You know exactly what memories will come with them.

Your son cannot wait while you find a more comfortable solution. You take the blades into Helheim, complete the task, and return with what Freya needs. You stand nearby with the weapons hanging at your sides. Freya knows the treatment, so you leave her room to work and watch for any sign that he is recovering. Atreus survives. The memory of the temple remains. You will also remember carrying these blades into Helheim to save him.

You finally tell Atreus that you are gods. He responds badly to having that much new information at once. Being divine starts to sound to him like permission to do what he wants. You recognize the attitude, and it is unpleasant to hear it coming from your own son. You have to correct him while knowing you once took that reasoning much further. He will watch how you handle the next fight.

Baldur forces that problem in front of you again. His invulnerability has left him unable to feel, and he blames his mother, Freya. Contact with a mistletoe arrowhead breaks his protection, and you and Atreus defeat him together. You spare him at first. Then he tries to kill Freya, and you intervene. You kill her son to prevent her death.

Freya still wants revenge. Saving her does not make her able to accept losing Baldur. Your son has watched you kill him to stop him from murdering Freya. She is alive and grieving her son. You recognize her grief because you once stood over the bodies of your own family. Killing you will not bring Baldur back.

You and Atreus eventually reach Jotunheim and scatter Faye's ashes. You learn that she was a giant, and that she had a name for your son you did not know. Loki. The journey has brought you closer to him, but it has also revealed how much both of you still have to understand. Returning home does not mean the gods will leave you there.

Fimbulwinter makes surviving in Midgard harder. Atreus grows older, and he wants to understand his place in what is coming. You keep trying to hold him back as he pushes for a chance to act on what he knows about Loki. He is growing into someone who can leave your side and choose a course of action himself. When that happens, you will have to trust the things you taught him.

Thor and Freya have reasons to come after your family. The conflict approaching the realms brings you back toward war, and you cannot meet it as the man who once attacked Olympus. You have people relying on your decisions now. Your son has already seen what happens when you hide information from him. Working with him means accepting that he will have ideas you would never have chosen yourself.

After surviving Ragnarok, you enter Valhalla with Mimir. Here, you encounter reminders of the past you have spent so long avoiding. Tyr challenges you through repeated fights. The fights give him a way to make you pause and listen. You have to speak about the decisions you made, including the ones you would prefer he never brought up.

Eventually, you face the image of your younger self on the throne of the God of War. You remember what you expected when you first sat there. You had killed Ares and gained his position, so surely something in your life was supposed to improve. Instead, you used the power while carrying the same anger into every decision. People below you paid for that.

The younger man's actions caused suffering, and you have to acknowledge it when you look at him. You also need to work out whether the choices you made afterward give you a reason to try again. Atreus survived because you returned for the blades. He learned more about you because you finally spoke. Those were choices you made after Greece, and you have to account for them too when you decide what you are capable of doing next.

You approach the throne again. You know what happened the last time you accepted the position, and you know that accepting it now will give other people reasons to depend on you. There will be danger, requests you cannot answer with an axe, and decisions you will have to live with afterward. You have faced gods who could kill you. This time, you sit down knowing you will have to help people who cannot fight them.
