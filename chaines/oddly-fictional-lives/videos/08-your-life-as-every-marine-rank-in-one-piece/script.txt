Your first pirate attack starts while you're carrying a bucket.

You have joined the Marines to protect your island. Right now, you're hiding behind a barrel while somebody steals the ship you're supposed to be cleaning. Your supervisor wants you to sound the alarm. The pirate beside the bell would prefer that you didn't. He has a sword, and you have soapy water. You throw the water in his face, pull the rope and discover that the bell is considerably louder when your head is underneath it.

The guards catch him on the gangplank. You get your ship back, and your supervisor hands you another bucket. Apparently, catching a pirate doesn't excuse the first puddle.

Your name is Corin. You're an adult recruit imagining the whole climb through the Marines of One Piece, from the lowest uniform to the fleet admiral's office. The pirates will get stronger as you rise. You'll also have to decide what you're prepared to do with the authority you've been asking for.

The cleaning job doesn't put you on some secret command track. Chore duty sits below the proper ladder. Your first formal rank is seaman recruit, and your uniform is a white shirt, a blue neckerchief and a cap that never stays on during training. Chief Senn keeps knocking it off with a padded staff. When you ask how you're meant to stop him, he suggests moving your head.

Mara, the recruit beside you, manages it. Then she walks into a hanging rope. You agree not to mention either performance at dinner.

As a seaman apprentice, you finally go on patrol. The fishing boats look harmless until Mara notices that one sits much lower than the others. Beneath its catch are stolen weapons. You board it with your rifle shaking, follow Senn's instructions and keep the smugglers away from the hatch. Your job is small enough to understand. Keep that door shut until help arrives.

You do, even when somebody starts kicking it from below. Senn takes the smugglers ashore, and Mara finds your cap floating in their fish barrel.

By seaman first class, you can load a cannon without somebody checking your hands. During a chase, you aim at a pirate ship's rigging instead of the crowded dock behind it. The damaged sail drops, the ship turns sideways, and your boarding party gets across. A shopkeeper waves from the shore. You wave back until Senn reminds you that the cannon still needs loading.

That shot gets you noticed. It also gets you assigned to the gun crew every time there's trouble.

Petty officer means the new recruits start looking at you when the shouting begins. You know how unpleasant this is because you spent your first patrol looking at Senn. On your next boarding action, a recruit freezes halfway across the plank. You go back for him, give him something simple to carry and walk him over behind you. Later, he asks how you stayed calm. You were busy hoping he couldn't see your knees.

As chief petty officer, you're responsible for keeping a whole section useful. Food, drills, damaged equipment and the sailor who insists his rifle is clean because the outside looks fine. You inspect the inside. Something falls out that may once have been lunch.

The jokes stop during a storm pursuit. A pirate vessel called the Red Gull cuts across your bow, and its captain, Varr, orders cannon fire into your sails. A mast cracks above the recruits. You haul them clear while Mara frees a trapped gunner. Varr escapes into the rain. Your ship limps home with people who would have been left under the wreckage if you'd stayed at your own post.

Senn recommends you for master chief petty officer after the repairs and another run of hard assignments. Now you're the experienced voice an officer wants beside him when a plan meets actual weather. A new lieutenant gives an order that would turn your damaged ship broadside to the waves. You point at the broken mast, explain what will happen and offer another heading. He listens. You make it into harbour without losing the rest of it.

You like being useful. You're less pleased when Senn tells you that being useful has made you a candidate for a different kind of responsibility.

Warrant officer puts you between the crew you've lived with and the officers you've been answering to. People expect you to know how a ship works even when they're arguing about where it should go. You spend a convoy escort with a chart spread over a crate, helping the navigator find a passage wide enough for the slowest transport. Varr has begun raiding these routes. His fast ship can choose where to fight. Your supply ships can't.

When you become an ensign, you receive an officer's assignment and a white coat. You try it on where nobody can see. Mara sees anyway. She asks whether the coat can help carry the ammunition you've left outside.

Your first command is a boarding team. You find Varr's supply boat in a sheltered inlet, but the deck crew surrenders too quickly. You hold your team back and check below before bringing everyone aboard. The hold is packed with powder. A burning fuse runs toward it. You cut the fuse, move the prisoners ashore and have the boat searched from end to end. Varr has learned that Marines like an easy arrest.

Lieutenant junior grade brings your first voyage into the Grand Line. The ordinary compass won't tell you what you need here. The navigator follows a Log Pose from island to island, and you learn to ask before assuming the weather will behave like weather. One afternoon, hail drums against the deck while sunlight still hits the bow. You pull a laughing recruit under cover and watch a chunk of ice dent the railing where he stood.

Mara is getting good at navigation. You stop pretending your coat makes you good at it too.

That night, something larger than your ship rises beside the lanterns. A Sea King opens its mouth above the rail, and the recruits aim at teeth they couldn't possibly fit through the cannon ports. Mara calls for darkness on the exposed deck while the captain orders a turn. You get the lanterns covered and move the gun crew clear of the rail. The creature's head follows the light drifting away in the ship's wake. Your vessel slides behind a ridge of black rock before it turns back. You spend the rest of the watch listening for water that sounds too close. At breakfast, nobody laughs when you check beneath the table.

As a lieutenant, you lead a landing on an island where Varr has emptied the harbour warehouses. His lookout sees your boat, and the pirates start moving their prisoners uphill. You divide your team. The noisy half follows the road while you and Mara climb through a drainage channel behind the storehouse. You come out wet, smelling terrible and close enough to open the prisoners' door before the guards understand why the road has gone quiet.

You get the hostages to the beach. Varr gets away through the other side of the island. A rescued dockworker tells you where his next cargo is going. For once, the people you brought home give you more useful information than the man you caught.

Lieutenant commander puts you in a position to use it. You arrange a patrol around the next shipment, expecting another armed freighter. Instead, a fighter steps onto the rail and knocks your front rank across the deck with a single blow. You block his next strike and feel your weapon bend. The training that made you impressive in your old harbour has run out.

You order a retreat behind the gun shield, put a cannon between him and the boarding party and get your wounded back across. You survive by using the ship. Senn, older and deeply unimpressed by your broken weapon, agrees that it was the sensible decision. He also finds you an instructor.

Your next assignment includes training with Marines who can read an attack before you finish making it. Observation Haki feels unfair when you're the person being hit. Armament Haki looks more useful when your instructor hardens his hand and snaps your practice weapon. Neither arrives with a promotion letter. You spend exhausting sessions learning to sense an intention, hold your ground and stop swinging just because somebody has annoyed you.

As a commander, you get another chance against Varr's fighter. This time, you notice where his attention moves before his weapon does. You shift out of the attack, harden your grip and strike his blade aside. Your team closes the gap you create. He's still dangerous, and you're still frightened. You can finally do something useful while frightened.

Mara takes his surrender. Varr watches from his departing ship, and you see him recognise you before the sails hide his face.

Captain gives you a ship of your own. You walk aboard expecting the moment to feel grand. The cook wants to know what you're doing about the leaking storeroom, and somebody has already lost the key to your cabin. You spend your first evening helping them find it.

Then you begin choosing patrols. You take your ship to ports the larger vessels pass by, train the sailors who remind you of yourself and hang a chart beside your desk with pins marking Varr's raids. Mara, now an officer with her own work to do, visits and turns one of the pins around. The attacks follow the supply routes out of a government storehouse.

You inspect the storehouse and find crates carrying official seals beneath stolen canvas. The official in charge claims the pirates forced him to cooperate. His hidden accounts suggest that he liked being forced rather a lot. You bring him in with the evidence. A senior officer wants the matter handled without an audience. The dockworkers who saw your team open the crates have already provided one.

The arrest makes you unpopular with people who can delay your career. You keep patrolling while they do. When Varr attacks another transport, your crew arrives early because those dockworkers now tell you what they see.

On your next visit, the shopkeeper from your first cannon chase has a parcel waiting. Inside is a cap, carefully dried and patched along the brim. Mara apparently sent it ahead with the supply boats. The shopkeeper's family brings food down to the quay, and your crew eats sitting on the mooring ropes. Nobody asks you to give a speech. You stay until the lamps come on, listening to an ordinary harbour close for the evening. Your ship has given these people a reason to feel safe leaving their doors open. You'd like to keep doing that.

Commodore arrives after the investigation survives attempts to bury it. Your responsibility expands beyond a single deck. You coordinate ships, divide patrol areas and discover how quickly a sensible plan collapses when every captain thinks his own problem deserves the fastest vessel. You put Mara in charge of the supply convoy. She objects to the route you've chosen, lays out her weather notes and changes your mind in front of everyone.

You let her finish. You remember being the man standing beside a lieutenant with a broken mast behind him.

The convoy gets through. Varr loses the shipment he expected to steal, and his remaining allies begin leaving him. Before you can close in, you receive orders to take a wider command. Your fight with one pirate has become part of a sea full of other people's emergencies.

As rear admiral, you reach a harbour where the defenders have abandoned the outer walls. A giant pirate is tearing the cranes apart, and the evacuation boats are trapped under falling timber. Your captains want a target. You want a lane through the wreckage. You take a boat in with the engineers, use Armament Haki to survive the first impact and hold the pirate's attention while the crews clear a passage.

He throws you through a warehouse roof. You land among sacks of flour, cough most of the warehouse into the air and climb back out looking much less distinguished. The evacuation boats are moving. You can afford to be laughed at afterward.

Varr is captured during the operation. Mara brings him past your damaged boat, and he complains that you haven't treated a captain with proper respect. You ask whether he'd prefer the bucket from your first ship. He doesn't understand the question. Mara does.

Vice admiral puts you among people whose strength can change a battlefield. Your Haki training continues because the opponents have stopped looking remotely manageable. You spar with veterans, lose, recover and learn which fights need more than the confidence you brought into them. A coat worn over your shoulders doesn't make your ribs harder.

Your new work also brings you closer to headquarters. Officers who once ignored you now want your opinion about distant islands. You ask to hear from the captains actually there. A clerk looks surprised when you wait for the answers.

One report comes from Mara. Her convoy has found an island paying pirates for protection while claiming it has everything under control. The residents are frightened of both sides. Your orders allow you to break the pirate force. They don't explain how you're supposed to tell a frightened fishing boat from a fleeing raider at night.

You move your ships close enough to inspect the boats and give the civilians a marked route out. A pirate tries to use that route. You intercept him yourself while the transports continue. At dawn, the harbour is open and your exhausted crews are feeding people they were warned might be enemies.

The admiral reviewing the operation asks why you exposed your flagship. You show him the evacuation report and the captured pirate captain. He reads both. Later, when an admiral's position becomes vacant, your name enters a discussion you never expected to reach.

There are only three admiral positions. Getting one means becoming one of the Government's most formidable military officers. It doesn't hand you a Devil Fruit or make you somebody you used to watch from the back of a parade. In this imagined career, you earn the appointment through strength, years of command and the reputation your crews helped build.

Your first major deployment as admiral is against a pirate fleet using an island's harbour as cover. The Government wants a decisive result. A golden Den Den Mushi sits in its case, connected to the authority behind a Buster Call. You know what such an order can bring down on an island. On the ordinary receiver, Mara tells you that civilians are still inside the harbour.

You leave the golden snail in its case. You land with the boarding force, break through the pirate barricade and push toward the battery holding the evacuation ships in place. Your Haki turns a strike that would once have folded you into an opening for the Marines behind you. They take the guns while you hold the enemy commander on the steps.

The evacuation begins. Headquarters asks why the operation is taking longer than expected. You answer while ducking a blow that removes part of the wall. You'd like whoever wrote the timetable to come and hold this staircase.

The last transport clears the channel before your ships fire on the empty pirate positions. You return with prisoners, damaged vessels and an explanation you'll have to give in person. Some officers call the operation hesitant. The crews who went ashore with you keep volunteering for your next one.

Years later, the fleet admiral's office becomes vacant, and your appointment follows arguments you're glad you didn't have to sit through. You have reached the highest Marine rank. The World Government still sits above you. Becoming fleet admiral gives you command of the organisation, not freedom from the people who govern it.

Mara visits your office carrying a proposed deployment. She puts it over the neat ceremonial papers somebody has arranged across your desk. There's trouble at a small port, the kind that used to wait while larger bases dealt with larger threats. The local commander has requested help more than once. Nobody has sent a ship.

You recognise the harbour on the chart. You cleaned a deck there, lost your cap there and used a bucket against a man with a sword. You move a vessel from the reserve list and approve the departure while Mara is still standing beside you.

At the port, a nervous seaman recruit hears the alarm and looks toward the water. A Marine ship is already coming through the harbour mouth.
