# POV: You Marry a Korean Woman

The first time she touches you, she's moving your elbow away from an old man's ribs. You're packed into Line 2 at Euljiro 3-ga at 8:12 on Monday, checking if your boss answered your late-invoice message. The train smells like rain, coffee, and seaweed kimbap. She taps your sleeve, shifts you six inches, and the car relaxes around her. Then she sees the transit card in your fingers, notices the gate you're aiming for, and says, "This way." You follow because you're lost. Later, you learn that when Seo-yeon cares, she usually fixes the nearest problem.

## The elevator, 11 floors up
You don't see her again for 47 minutes, which feels long enough to become humiliating.

By the time you reach the software company near Euljiro, your shirt is damp, your laptop bag cuts into one shoulder, and you're carrying the ordinary panic of being 31, American, late, and unsure whether this assignment is temporary because you're useful or because nobody else wanted it.

The lobby smells like wet stone and burnt coffee.

In the elevator, she steps in with rolled architectural drawings under one arm and recognizes your backpack.

Her name is Seo-yeon. She's 28, an architect at the firm renovating your company's floors.

Before you can misread the buttons, she presses 11.

Then she looks at your coffee.

"That's not breakfast."

At the next floor, an older woman enters. Seo-yeon taps your elbow, shifts you six inches, and makes room without ceremony.

"Thanks."

"Don't block the door."

Rain runs down the Seoul glass. She keeps appearing at the wrong moments, and your embarrassment turns into curiosity.

## Three weeks of being corrected
Over the next three weeks, Seo-yeon keeps appearing in the lobby, the elevator, and the coffee shop below the office, always moving fast enough to make Seoul seem slow around her.

She catches you walking on the wrong side of an escalator, holding chopsticks like borrowed tools, and stopping near Jongno as if the city has a pause button.

She has opinions about everything you eat.

Your triangle kimbap is "emergency food." Your convenience-store lunch is "sad but efficient." Your first jjajangmyeon stains one cuff, which she studies like evidence.

You learn she works late because drawings change, clients lie about budgets, and building drawings throw expensive tantrums after 9 p.m.

At lunch, you use chopsticks with the confidence of a man defusing a small bomb.

The noodles win.

She tells you which bus exit lands closest to Cheonggyecheon, where to buy an umbrella that won't die in wind, and when you're speaking too loudly.

Her attention comes through instructions.

Your pride takes the hit. After a while, you start waiting for her to notice things.

The next one comes disguised as logistics.

## Chicken by the Han River
On Friday night, Seo-yeon texts you a location pin and three words.

"Don't be late."

You arrive at Banpo Hangang Park six minutes early and still feel accused.

She brings fried chicken and beer, part of the chimaek ritual filling the riverside with office workers, students, families, delivery scooters, and plastic bags tied against the wind.

The Han River is black beneath the bridges. Banpo Bridge lights ripple across the water as you share a mat too small for two adults pretending this isn't a date.

She asks why Seoul, why software, and why you're 31 without knowing what kind of life you're building.

You admit you expected six months and a better title.

"That's not a plan," she says. "That's a weather forecast."

Her phone buzzes. Her jaw tightens before she smiles at the message.

She is tired, but she refuses to call it tired.

Then she takes the chopsticks from your hand, positions your fingers, and leaves her fingertips there for one second too long.

The invitation arrives disguised as logistics. Now you have to learn her pace, one corrected step at a time.

## The noraebang test
Two weeks later, after hotteok burns your fingers and tteokbokki sauce threatens your white shirt, Seo-yeon leads you through Myeongdong.

Tourists crowd the cosmetic shops. Skewers steam beside the curb. She moves through the noise as if the city has quietly made room for her.

Then she turns down a narrow stairwell and opens a padded door.

The noraebang room has colored lights, sticky menus, and a songbook thick enough to hurt someone.

Seo-yeon chooses a sad ballad and sings it perfectly. The room goes quiet in a way that makes the disco lights seem rude.

Then she hands you the microphone and selects an old rock song too high for your dignity.

You destroy it.

Completely.

She laughs for the first time without covering her mouth, head tilted back, one hand on the table. The sound is so unguarded you realize you've been waiting for it.

"You have no shame."

"Not anymore."

She laughs harder.

Seo-yeon loves terrible singing because it's the one room where being exact isn't required.

You fall for her looseness, and immediately wonder how long it will last once she starts counting you.

## Day 100
On a Tuesday, Seo-yeon tells you Saturday is your 100th day.

You think she means 100 days since you met. Her expression says your math has personally disappointed her.

On her phone, a couple app has already turned the number into a small pink badge, baek-il, and the next boxes wait at 200 and 300. This is also, apparently, not optional.

She has counted from the Han River picnic.

You counted from when you stopped being afraid of her. The app does not care.

On Saturday, she produces matching lightweight jackets in a calm beige color.

You pretend to hate yours.

You wear it for the next four days.

That night, you walk beside Cheonggyecheon, where the stream cuts a lit path through downtown Seoul and office workers sit on stone ledges with their shoes near the water.

Seo-yeon photographs your sleeves side by side, not your faces.

"One hundred days means you kept showing up," she says.

Rent stops being the loudest worry; now you worry about becoming reliable in a calendar that remembers everything.

In a year, you'll understand what these counted days were preparing you to measure.

## Busan, her parents' table
She invites you to Busan on a Thursday, like she is scheduling an audit.

The KTX ride south from Seoul Station feels like a formal review of your character. Outside, the country blurs green and gray. Inside, Seo-yeon fixes your collar.

"Don't joke too much," she says.

Her mother feeds you before your shoes feel settled by the door. Grilled fish, soup, banchan, kimchi, rice, then more rice because you finish too much while nervous.

Her father asks about your salary, your plans, your family, your parents' health, your visa, and whether you intend to stay in Korea or collect experiences like souvenirs.

Seo-yeon stays quiet and lets the answer be yours.

At dinner, her father pours soju. Seo-yeon murmurs, "Two hands. Turn your head slightly away from him when you drink."

You do it badly.

His face gives nothing away.

Then he refills your glass.

Her mother looks at Seo-yeon, not you, and says softly, "Love still has to eat at home."

## The proposal on the river
Back in Seoul, the relationship becomes ordinary in the dangerous way.

Grocery runs. Late-night texts. Her correcting your route near Jonggak. You learning she buys myeolchi bokkeum when work has been bad, and cucumber kimchi when she's pretending it hasn't.

She works late. You start carrying a small umbrella because Seoul rain keeps returning, and because she once said adults shouldn't be surprised by weather.

On a cold night near Yeouido Hangang Park, you walk beside her after she cancels dinner twice because of deadlines.

"I'm sorry," she says. "The client changed the floor plan. Again."

By now, you know the changed floor plan is her way of apologizing first.

The river lights move behind her. Your hands shake so much the ring box looks mechanical.

"Stay with me," you say.

She looks at you for exactly three seconds.

"In Seoul?"

"Wherever you are standing."

She says yes.

Then she takes the ring, turns her hand under a streetlamp, and says, "It's slightly loose. We should resize it."

Even now, she says yes and immediately starts solving the fit.

You have three months before the wedding hall date, and the clock has already started.

## A Seoul wedding hall
The wedding happens in a Seoul wedding hall in Gangnam, fast, bright, organized, and so precise it makes your American idea of a wedding feel like a weather delay.

Guests arrive, bow, sign, hand envelopes to the reception table, and move with quiet efficiency. Photos happen on command. The ceremony runs so cleanly you wonder if romance has a project manager.

Seo-yeon looks calm until you notice her thumb rubbing the side of her bouquet.

"Are you okay?" you whisper.

"My father is watching," she says.

He is. So is everyone else.

You stand where you're told. This has become one of your main skills.

In South Korea, marriage does not usually fold a woman into her husband's family name. Seo-yeon keeps her own. Children usually take the father's surname, which everyone explains to you with the patience reserved for tourists and large dogs.

She remains Seo-yeon.

The forms update your address, tax status, and emergency contacts. Seo-yeon keeps the same family name she had that morning.

After the ceremony, her father shakes your hand for exactly long enough to make you breathe again.

Then someone leads you to the room where the family watches how you bow.

## Pyebaek, dates, and chestnuts
After the wedding, the pyebaek happens.

Hanbok. Low table. Cushions. Family elders seated like a court you hope is benevolent.

Her mother whispers, "This is pyebaek. Bow deeply, catch what they throw." Seo-yeon is already in hanbok, hands folded, while an aunt arranges dates and chestnuts like tiny verdicts on the table.

Because this is their family, the ceremony turns beautiful and chaotic almost immediately.

Seo-yeon bows with practiced grace.

You follow half a second late, which has become your cultural signature.

Dates and chestnuts fly.

You catch almost none.

One bounces off your wrist. One disappears into your sleeve. One lands on the floor with tragic confidence.

The whole family laughs.

Seo-yeon laughs too, but squeezes your hand under the cloth.

The elders joke about children. The cloth is nearly empty.

"You had one job," she whispers.

"I was emotionally busy," you whisper.

Catching symbolic chestnuts sounds simple until the chestnuts develop personal opinions.

## November kimjang
Married life begins in small domestic systems.

Shoes by the door. Rice in the cooker. Laundry rules you violate once. Seo-yeon keeps her own name on documents, work emails, and apartment paperwork.

In November, her mother calls you to Busan for kimjang, making kimchi.

You learn UNESCO put kimjang on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013, but standing in that kitchen, the important part is the sharing.

You salt napa cabbage. You mix gochugaru, garlic, ginger, scallions, and salted seafood paste under supervision. Plastic gloves don't keep garlic off you for a week.

Her mother corrects your technique without mercy.

Seo-yeon watches with satisfaction, because now her mother corrects you the same way Seo-yeon does.

The hands that once moved your elbow now press seasoning between cabbage leaves.

The vow starts to feel practical when you're standing in her mother's kitchen, useful for once, with your hands burning slightly.

Then the holidays arrive, and the questions stop being funny.

## Seollal, Chuseok, and the question
The first year of marriage is measured by holidays.

Chuseok brings food in plastic containers, and the quiet exhaustion of everyone trying to be generous in crowded rooms.

At Seollal, Lunar New Year, the family gathers again.

She teaches you sebae in the apartment, knees exact.

She says your first attempt looks like you're searching for a lost contact lens.

At her parents' home, you bow.

Nobody corrects you.

In this family, no public correction means you did fine.

Nieces and nephews collect envelopes like tiny accountants.

Then someone asks when you'll have a baby.

On TV, the news cites Statistics Korea: South Korea's fertility rate fell to 0.72 children per woman in 2023, the lowest in the world.

The TV talks in numbers.

In her parents' living room, her mother looks at Seo-yeon and waits.

Seo-yeon goes quiet.

You don't answer for her.

You sit beside her.

## Her father's hospital room
The hard moment comes through a phone call after midnight.

Her father is ill, and the KTX to Busan feels longer than the country.

In the hospital room, Seo-yeon becomes very still.

She goes still because this is the one problem she cannot organize, her father looking small under fluorescent light.

You buy porridge, bottled water, extra socks, and bad vending-machine coffee.

None of it fixes anything.

This time, there is nothing useful to fix.

Her father wakes and asks if you're still working hard.

He asks Seo-yeon if she is eating.

Even sick, he remains a parent with priorities.

You remember the first Busan dinner, the soju glass held in two hands, the head turned slightly away, the respect hidden inside a small movement.

Months pass with appointments, recovery, deadlines, and quiet meals.

Seo-yeon sometimes sings badly in the kitchen on purpose, as if testing whether joy still works.

Then she stands in the bathroom doorway holding a pregnancy test.

Direct as ever.

Terrified as ever.

"We need a bigger calendar," she says.

## Baek-il
The baby arrives after a long night in Seoul, rain on hospital windows, Seo-yeon crushing your hand hard enough to make your fingers reconsider loyalty.

On the hospital forms, your daughter is registered with your surname. Seo-yeon's name stays on every document the way it always has.

Three names live in the apartment.

You see all three names on mail, hospital forms, and tiny socks, and they all stay.

In June 2023, South Korea dropped the old "Korean age" for official documents, so your daughter is simply zero.

One less paperwork riddle.

At baek-il, her 100th day, family gathers with rice cakes, fruit, photos, and an infant refusing gratitude.

Seo-yeon says it softly.

"One hundred days means you kept showing up."

Her father is there, thinner but smiling.

Someone mentions the pyebaek chestnuts.

Later, rain taps the window.

A tiny transit-card charm hangs near the stroller.

Seo-yeon moves your elbow from the doorframe.

"This way."

"I know."

Her hand finds yours in the narrow hallway, and this time, you're exactly where she puts you.
