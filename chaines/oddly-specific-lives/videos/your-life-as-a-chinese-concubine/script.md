# POV: Your Life as a Chinese Concubine

Your mother asks the official at the Gate of Divine Prowess, "If they choose her, can I visit?" He looks past her and calls the next girl forward. The girl ahead of you gets hers back. The stone under your shoes is damp from last night's rain. You're thinking about the rice your mother left on the stove. Someone you can't see says, "Keep Suyan's tag." The official keeps your wooden tag. For now, it only means another summons. You step outside, and your mother sees your empty hand before she sees your face.

## The second call
Your mother gets no answer. She closes your fingers around the silver instead. "Count it," she says.

It's 1834 in Beijing. You're eighteen, Suyan of the Tatara clan. Your father serves as a Bordered Blue Banner officer, responsible for his company's horses and arrows. He also owes the noodle seller money. You can tell him exactly how much. Your embroidery is less useful.

The plain green jade hairpin in your hair belongs to your mother. The night before, she turns its little carved bat inward as she pins it in place. Now she watches you count the silver twice.

Every three years, unmarried Eight Banners girls are called for selection. This time, your name is on the list. You can't marry before you appear. Few girls become consorts. You've received food, silver and small gifts, though they might still send you home.

At home, you eat the food from your mother's covered bowl. Your mother waits for the second call before she'll discuss a marriage.

On the day you enter, an official gives your retired wooden tag back to you. "Keep it," he says. Your mother stays by the bowl when you leave.

## A side room, 1834
Your new room holds a kang bed, two maids and a title that sounds much larger from outside the door. You're a daying, the lowest rank, in a side hall beneath Concubine Wei. She occupies the main hall. The first time she notices you, she's reading your name on a list.

Across the Six Eastern and Six Western Palaces, Yulan leads you past Wei's main hall. She stops at a side-hall door and gives you its key. Another door opens behind you. You learn your part of the Six Western Palaces by counting doors.

Yulan, one of your maids, reads from your allowance list. "Thirty taels a year." She turns to the duty list and reads your maids' names. You write them in the ledger before your own expenses.

You set the ledger on the heated kang. Thread, tea, customary gifts for message carriers, flower-pot shoes. Old Liu, a quiet eunuch with a cricket gourd, shows you which gate opens at which hour. Later, one line on a duty list records an imperial summons. The next morning, your milk tea cools beside the ledger.

By winter, you've planned to spend four taels more than your allowance. You check the sums again and push the brazier toward the kang.

## The cost of a message
Before the courtyard stirs, you eat millet porridge and count coal beside the brazier. You keep your sleeves clear of the tea. After a while, you can tell who's coming by the way their footsteps turn at the gate.

One of your first maids leaves with permission. In 1836, Hualan takes her place. She's nineteen, from an Imperial Household Department bondservant family. Palace maids enter through a separate draft. Hualan may leave to marry someday.

You set aside Old Liu's customary gift before he goes to the gate.

Old Liu takes a message from you toward your family. He stops where palace rules require him to stop and makes no promise about an answer. His cricket gourd knocks softly against his sleeve as he turns back.

Your parents need special imperial permission to visit, and they rarely get it. Old Liu says no permission has been granted. You still haven't heard back. You count the coal left in the basket and wait for Old Liu at the gate.

On the back page of the ledger, you copy your father's debt to the noodle seller. You know how to make that number smaller, if a payment can reach him. On a winter morning, Old Liu sets a revised allowance list beside it.

## Fifty taels
Noble Lady Jin receives her promotion first. She entered the same year you did, and she remembers the name of every maid who comes through her door. Her new robe is a court-appropriate shade you haven't worn. When she sees you before an audience, she reaches over and fixes a fastening you've missed.

Your turn comes later. You're made a changzai, the rank above daying. Old Liu finds your new rank in the court's statute book. On paper, you move up before a coin can reach your father.

The palace history, the Guochao Gongshi, sets a changzai's allowance at fifty taels a year. You move to a slightly better side room and buy enough coal to heat it. One morning, you open your ledger and the ink moves across the page without stiffening in the cold. For a moment, you leave the debt column alone.

You buy flower-pot shoes with platforms that don't wobble. You still practice walking after the courtyard empties. Beneath your liangbatou, your own hair arranged over a flat support, you keep your mother's plain green hairpin. The carved bat stays turned inward.

You begin a letter to tell her about the warm room. There's no permission to send it, so you fold the page into the ledger. Before winter ends, you find yourself counting what the next rank will cost the people who come with it.

## A hundred taels
The first thing your new rank gives you is names to enter in the ledger. Their wages go beneath the names. Only after that does Hualan unfold your better robe.

You're a noble lady now, a guiren. On the same page, you enter your allowance at a hundred taels a year. The extra hands keep the tea warm and the accounts current. You learn their names before you learn which one folds the robe best.

In the afternoon, you sit for your first portrait. The painter asks you to hold still while Hualan straightens the horse-hoof cuff of your Manchu court robe. Your fingers start to count the brushstrokes. You stop at twelve.

Jin comes to see the painting when it's finished. She studies your face, then the fine edge of the cuff.

"It looks like you," she says. "Has your mother heard?"

"No."

That evening, the empress finds a copied figure in a palace account. She rubs her eyes, shows the keeper where the error begins, and asks you to check the new total. Yours is right. She remembers your name.

You put the old wooden selection tag in the ledger box. The portrait hangs where visitors can see your rank. Inside the box, three lines still give your father's name, your banner and your age when you enter.

Days later, the empress dowager asks who added the account.

## The birthday audience
By the empress dowager's birthday, Hualan has watched you practice your greeting until she can tell from the doorway when you'll forget your hands. You try again beside a plate of pastries you've prepared for the audience.

"Lower," she says.

You lower them. You're better with the pastry counts.

Jin receives another promotion before the audience. It stings when you hear it from someone else. The next morning, she leaves a scrap beside your tea with the order of entrance written clearly enough to read while walking.

Old Liu checks that order against the ranks: empress, imperial noble consort, noble consort, consort, pin, noble lady, changzai, daying. He taps your place with one finger. You carry Jin's scrap anyway.

The empress dowager sits beneath the soft light of the hall. She's small, and she notices that one pastry sits closer to the edge of its plate than the others. You move it when asked. She has already seen the account the empress corrected.

"Bring it here."

A figure has been copied into the wrong column. You show her where, add the column again and return the paper. She looks at the total, then at you.

"You've done this before."

"Yes."

Nobody mentions a reward. Back in your side hall, Hualan reaches for the paper to put it away, but you keep it in your hands a moment longer. The empress dowager knows your face now. By morning, Old Liu has heard something that has nothing to do with your accounts.

## War news, 1840
Old Liu brings the news with the tea. There's war with Britain over opium. It began in 1839. Old Liu says British ships have sailed north. Here, you hear it in bits, between the opening of a gate and the morning accounts. Old Liu sets down his cricket gourd and repeats only what he's sure he heard.

A major court ceremony still requires its entrance order. Promotions come in rank order, often in groups at such ceremonies. When your name is read, you advance to pin. You look toward Jin. She gives you the smallest nod.

A pin receives two hundred taels a year, and the maid allotment brings your attendants to six. A pin holds a palace's main hall. An attendant tells you when your new rank allows you to use a sedan chair. You walk across the courtyard to your new door because the ledger box is already in your hands.

Before the ceremony, Jin straightens your mother's green hairpin among the newer jewels.

"There," she says. "She'd know you."

Old Liu carries the rest of the box. Inside are your accounts and the wooden tag, packed beside the timetable you're beginning to keep for the larger household. The main hall doorway lets you both through without turning sideways. From its threshold, you can still see the path toward the north gate.

For a while, the household runs to the hour. The tea arrives when it should. Then a door closes, and everyone waits outside it.

## Morning, 1841
The door stays closed through the night. Someone refills the brazier. Hualan brings tea you leave untouched, and Old Liu moves the cricket gourd off the ledge so a hurried attendant won't knock it down. At dawn, you hear a baby cry.

Your son.

For his third-day bath, attendants lay out gifts and enter payments in the household account. You ask twice whether the cloth is warm enough. Hualan says it is. You record your household's payments in the account, without knowing what each attendant receives.

A wet nurse and a nanny begin their duties. The nurse checks his cloth before she lifts him. You know the woman in front of you by the way she holds your son steady while you lean close enough to see the fold of his ear.

Hualan brings the hours when you may see him. You have them memorized before she reaches the end of the page. That afternoon, you hear him through the wall and ask whether it's time.

"Soon," she says.

Your promotion to consort, or fei, follows. The same palace history gives a consort three hundred taels a year. You rule a fresh page for the new household expenses, then copy your son's hours into the margin where you'll see them first.

The following year, a revised account arrives with fewer candles listed.

## Candles, 1842
The revised account is short on candles, and you're still holding it when news of the treaty reaches the palace. The war is over in 1842, and China must pay an indemnity under the treaty. A court order calls for reduced household expenses. In your hall, someone has chosen the light.

You bring the figures to the empress dowager's rooms and find her by a window. She runs her finger down the candle column while you check the totals. Outside, the courtyard is still bright enough to see the seams between the stones.

"Is this correct?" she asks.

"Yes."

She looks toward the room behind you. "Finish before dark."

Hualan knows exactly how many candles remain. Her release to marry comes stamped with approval, and she lays her clothes beside an empty bundle a week before her twenty-fifth birthday. She reads the notice twice. The notice gives no forwarding address. You read it again, looking for one.

You enter twenty-five taels on a loose page of your ledger. It's one month of your allowance, and it's yours to give. Hualan sees the figure while you're wrapping the silver.

"That's too much."

"Can you carry it?"

She tests the weight in her sleeve. "Yes."

At the north gate, you stand where you can watch her pass out of sight. She turns once, with her bundle tucked close, then keeps walking. You wait a little longer than you need to.

That evening, the candle goes out while you still have figures to enter. You close the ledger. You don't know where to write to Hualan, and you find yourself wondering what you'd put in a letter to your mother.

## One afternoon
You write about coal first. Your mother knows what a room feels like when the brazier runs low. Then you write about your son, who comes from lessons with one sleeve tucked into his cuff and the other hanging loose. At the bottom, you ask whether your father still owes the noodle seller.

You read it over. You cannot send it as written, so you fold the page into the ledger box beside your old selection tag.

Months later, permission comes for your parents to visit. It gives you one afternoon. They come without servants, carrying nothing you can see.

Your father bows when he enters. He holds it long enough for you to realize he's waiting for you to tell him to rise.

"Please," you say.

Your mother sits close and asks what your son eats. She has heard his name, but she wants to know whether he finishes his meals. Your father notices the green jade hairpin beneath your other ornaments.

"Has the bat come loose?"

You take it out so he can check. His hands are steadier than you expect. Before they leave, your mother leans close. "The noodle seller is paid," she says. "Your selection silver. Every coin, counted twice." You can still see its old figure on a page of your ledger.

You think of the permission that brought your parents in when Jin's palace file reaches you. She's demoted, and her maid is sent away that day. The file doesn't say why.

She comes for a small account she left with you. You look toward her side room, where the maid's bedding is gone. The kind thing you meant to say won't come.

"What's the correct total?" you ask.

Jin takes the page. "Thirty-six."

You enter it. Jin's empty side room stays with you, so you go looking for your son. At the practice ground, he goes after three arrows he has sent wide.

## The westward order, 1850
Through the late 1840s, your son studies Manchu and practices archery. He shoots wide often enough to learn the far edge of the ground. You count the arrows he has to retrieve. After a while, he counts with you, and once he catches an arrow you've missed.

One afternoon, an attendant moves you aside for a yellow sedan chair. A voice beyond him asks the boy's name. The emperor repeats it incorrectly.

Your son supplies it again. The chair continues down the corridor, and you straighten the fastening at the boy's throat before he goes back to lessons.

In January 1850, the empress dowager calls for an account. You find a copied figure and point to it; her nail has already left a mark beside it. When she dies, white mourning cloth covers the courtyard.

In 1850, the emperor dies. Attendants begin to inventory your main hall. They record the furnishings, the lamps and the contents of each cabinet while you stand beside your ledger box, still deciding what to pack first. Your son's practice tally is inside it. So is the wooden tag.

The inventory sends your household west to Shou'an. On the next page, your title sits one rank higher under the new emperor. The change appears on your papers. At thirty-four, you ask to thank the new emperor. An attendant tells you to send the papers instead.

Your son stays in the princes' quarters. You take the box west, with your mother's hairpin still in your hair. By morning, your new rank has been entered in the records, and the room at Shou'an is cold enough to stiffen the ink.

## Shou'an
On your first morning at Shou'an, you warm the ink beside the kang before opening the ledger. There is a shelf for it, and a courtyard whose sounds you must learn. One pair of footsteps turns toward your door. Another passes on its way to the gate. You once learned the Six Western Palaces this way.

Your new title brings a larger allowance. The shelf is useful. So is the coal.

Your son grows, marries and establishes his own mansion. He tells you its address, and you copy it carefully. You fold the address and tuck it beneath the ledger in your room at Shou'an. His household grows at that address. Yours remains here.

In 1861, news reaches your room that Cixi has become regent. She entered the palace at a low rank. Emperor Xianfeng has died, and their son now sits on the throne. The news arrives with other court news; you return to the month's accounts.

Eventually, Old Liu's cricket gourd stops appearing at your door. A different attendant brings the tea and learns where you keep the cups.

Years pass. Your son has a daughter now, and beneath a stack of fresh pages, you find the first crooked tally he made of his arrows.

## The gate burns, 1889
One night in January 1889, when you're seventy-two, the sky over the outer court turns orange. An attendant comes to your window with his coat half fastened. For a moment, the walls that have held your whole adult life look as though they might fail to hold themselves.

The attendant says the Gate of Supreme Harmony is burning. You watch the glow until it fades. By morning, snow settles on ash. You revise the household account, as you've done for years. When an emperor orders another promotion, you plan repairs. The clerk reads the order to you once, then holds it closer to the lamp to check the seal. The clerk enters your new rank carefully, then reads aloud the silver due to your household. There's more than you need for tea, coal and the repairs you keep putting off.

"Start with the box," you tell the attendant.

Its hinge has cracked. He takes it away and brings it back sound, the little lid opening without a catch. Inside lies the wooden tag returned to you when you entered. You find your son's first timetable under the tag and make room beside it for a page bearing his daughter's name.

Your father's name remains clear. So does Bordered Blue Banner. So does eighteen.

## The north gate, 1891
On your seventy-fifth birthday, special permission brings your son and his grown daughter to Shou'an for an audience. The attendant checks the order twice. You have never had a standing right to receive family here, and your son waits until he is called, though his hair is nearly all grey.

His daughter steps in behind him. She has your mother's careful way of looking at a room before choosing where to stand. You keep one hand on the ledger box while they offer the required greetings. Once the attendant withdraws, your son notices the old tag beside it.

He reaches for it before you read it aloud. You let him hold it while you point to his grandfather's name and post, then Bordered Blue Banner, then the age written at the bottom. Eighteen. He traces the worn edge with his thumb. You tell him it's his to take.

Your granddaughter bends her head. You remove the plain green jade hairpin from your own hair and place it in hers. The small carved bat catches on a loose strand, and your fingers take longer than they used to.

"Is it straight?" she asks.

"Count everything. Keep this."

"She will," your son says.

At the Gate of Divine Prowess, your granddaughter walks out through the north gate with the green hairpin in her hair.
