# Recherche et consignes — POV: Your Life as a Chinese Concubine

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS about her world (use several, rounded, each inside a scene, with the source named in the sentence; never invent other statistics):
- Under the Qing, the Ministry of Revenue registered every unmarried girl of the Eight Banners for a palace selection held once every three years; a banner girl could not marry before she had been through it. Historian Mao Liping (Renmin University), studying the Qing palace selection archives, found that very few candidates ever became consorts: most went home and married, and every candidate received food, silver and small gifts for coming.
- Candidates' ages (BACKGROUND ONLY, never said on screen): Chinese reference works give 13 to 16 by Chinese reckoning, some 13 to 17, with 17 and over treated as "overage"; National Geographic says Cixi was 16 when presented in 1852. In OUR story the heroine is 18 at the selection and older in every later scene.
- Chinese reference works on the selection, drawing on 19th-century court notes, describe candidates entering by the Gate of Divine Prowess (Shenwu Gate) at the north of the Forbidden City and waiting by the Shunzhen Gate, led in by eunuchs in small groups; each girl had a wooden name tag giving her father's post and name, her banner and her age. "Keeping the tag" meant she was called back; "dropping the tag" meant she was free to go home and marry. Mao Liping adds that officials checked the girls' clothes and jewellery for Han Chinese fashion and stressed Manchu family names.
- The ladder (eight ranks, top to bottom): empress (huanghou), imperial noble consort (huang guifei), noble consort (guifei), consort (fei), concubine (pin), noble lady (guiren), first-class attendant (changzai), second-class attendant (daying). From 1748 the court fixed the numbers at one empress, one imperial noble consort, two noble consorts, four consorts and six pin at a time; noble ladies and attendants had no limit.
- Yearly allowance in silver, from the "Guochao Gongshi", the palace history compiled under the Qianlong emperor from 1742: empress dowager 2,000 taels plus 20 taels of gold; empress 1,000; imperial noble consort 800; noble consort 600; consort 300; pin 200; noble lady 100; changzai 50; daying 30. (Round in narration: "thirty taels a year", "a hundred taels".)
- Maids came with rank: Mao Liping says the number of palace maids was set by a woman's rank; Chinese historians' summaries give 2 maids for a daying, 4 for a noble lady, 6 for a pin or consort, 8 for a noble consort, about 10 or more for the empress (sources differ at the top; never say an exact number for the empress).
- The consorts lived in the Six Eastern Palaces and Six Western Palaces on either side of the inner court (Palace Museum guide). Mao Liping explains that a pin or higher became mistress of a palace's main hall, while noble ladies, changzai and daying lived in the side halls of those same palaces.
- Palace maids were a separate draft: daughters of the Imperial Household Department's bondservant (booi) families, chosen every year. Mao Liping says that from the Yongzheng reign onward maids could mostly leave to marry before 25, and once out they could never come back to the palace or keep any contact with the consorts. Historian Evelyn Rawski ("The Last Emperors") quotes bondservant fathers complaining that a daughter was lost to them until she was 25.
- Family: Mao Liping found that only a consort's own parents could ever visit, by special imperial permission, rarely (not recorded in every reign), with no servants allowed in; there is no record of any Qing consort ever going home for a visit (the famous home visit in "Dream of the Red Chamber" is fiction).
- Promotion was mostly step by step: Mao Liping quotes the Qianlong emperor saying consorts rose "in order", not by favour, and notes that group promotions came with great court ceremonies. Demotions happened too: Mao Liping cites an 1840 palace file in which a maid was sent away and, the same day, her mistress, a changzai, was demoted to daying. The Imperial Household Department had to check the real reason and the maid's condition before releasing her.
- Birth of a son could change everything: Britannica calls Cixi a low-ranking concubine of the Xianfeng emperor who bore his only son in 1856; she had entered the palace in 1852 as "Noble Lady Lan", was promoted on the very day her son was born, and became regent for him in 1861. Palace Museum researcher Shen Xin found that at that birth the two midwives each received 10 taels at the bath ritual on the third day. The South China Morning Post describes imperial wet nurses being chosen under strict rules of age, looks and health.
- Power: Mao Liping notes that Qing consorts were barred from government; the only two empresses dowager who ruled were Xiaozhuang and Cixi. The Qianlong emperor's mother had entered a prince's household in 1705 with the low title "gege", became empress dowager, lived to 85 (died 1777), and her son built the Shoukang Palace for her in 1735-36 (Palace Museum).
- After an emperor died, his consorts left the Six Palaces for the widows' palaces on the west side, Cining, Shoukang and Shou'an; Cining was set aside for surviving consorts in 1653 (Palace Museum guide, China Daily). Mao Liping says each new emperor raised his father's consorts one rank, and that widowed consorts under 50 were not allowed to meet the new emperor; after the mid-Qianlong reign there is no record of a consort going to live with her grown son. No source found for Qing consorts becoming nuns: do not use.
- Real cases (findings only): Noble Consort Cheng entered the palace in 1828 as a noble lady, was demoted to changzai the next year, restored, promoted to pin, demoted again, then promoted by three later emperors after her husband's death and died in 1888, aged 75, in the Shou'an Palace. Mao Liping cites Consort Ding, a widow of the Kangxi emperor, who lived to 97 and on her 90th birthday received 1,000 taels.
- Harem size varied: counts differ, but the Kangxi emperor is usually credited with the most recorded consorts, more than 50; the Guangxu emperor had three. The South China Morning Post's Forbidden City series says the early Qing cut the palace eunuchs from tens of thousands under the Ming to about 3,000.
- Outside news (era markers): Britannica dates the First Opium War with Britain to 1839-42, its indemnity adding to discontent, and the Taiping Rebellion from 1850. The Hong Kong Palace Museum says the Gate of Supreme Harmony burned in January 1889, a month before the Guangxu emperor's wedding.

CRISIS ~80%: 1850, the emperor dies; you leave your main hall for the widows' quarters on the west side (Shou'an); the new emperor raises you one rank in his father's honour (Mao Liping), but at 34 you are too young to be allowed to meet him; your son, still a boy, stays in the princes' quarters and later marries and moves to his own mansion; you will never live with him. Outside: 1851, rebellion in the south; 1861, a woman who had entered as a noble lady, your old rank, becomes regent (Cixi, as a finding only). ENDING 90-100%: January 1889, you are 73; at night the sky over the outer court glows: the Gate of Supreme Harmony is burning a month before the young emperor's wedding (Hong Kong Palace Museum); in the morning, snow on the ash. A later emperor raises you one more rank: more taels than you can spend, nothing left to buy. Your son, grey now, visits on your birthday with his grown daughter; you take out the old wooden selection tag from the ledger box (your father's name, your banner, "eighteen") and give it to your son, and the jade hairpin to his daughter: "Count everything. Keep this." Last image: the plain green hairpin in a young woman's hair walking out through the north gate, the way Hualan did.
