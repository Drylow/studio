# POV: You Marry a Canadian Woman

Your car gives up in front of her, and she knows what you forgot before she knows your name. She spots the cord hanging from your grille and the empty outlet beside your Winnipeg parking spot. It's seven on a January morning. Your pharmacy shift starts in forty minutes. Frost coats the windshield. Her blue parka fills your window. "You didn't plug it in," she says. You tell her about the shift. She takes off one mitten and reaches for her keys. "Call them. I can drive you." You learn her name at the second red light.

## The second red light
She waits while you call the pharmacy, then pulls away before you can apologize again. When traffic stops again, she says, "Claire Tremblay-Okafor." You give her your name. Beneath her open parka, you see a paramedic uniform. She catches you looking at the uniform. "Long night."

You moved from Arizona in November for a job in Winnipeg. December meant training for the pharmacy job. This morning is your first shift at the pharmacy counter, where you know exactly what you're supposed to do. The cord hanging from your car is another matter.

"It's for the block heater," Claire says. "Plug it in when it's this cold. It helps the engine start." She says it the way someone explains where to find a light switch. Outside, it's minus thirty-two, and the air chills the inside of your nostrils when you breathe. Nobody in Arizona ever told you that at minus forty, Celsius and Fahrenheit finally agree, and people here mention it the way you'd mention traffic.

You make the shift with six minutes to spare. At the pharmacy, you thank her. She asks when you're done. You tell her. "I'll meet you at the car," she says.

She does. By afternoon, the sun is low over the parking lot. Claire brings jumper cables, gets the engine running, and points to the outlet beside your spot. She waits while you find the plug yourself.

You want to ask why she's spending more of her day on this. Instead, you thank her again. "Get home before you turn it off," she says.

Later, you can still picture her pulling off that mitten to reach for her keys before she even knows your name.

## Two weeks of winter
Two weeks later, you spot her at dusk beside the outdoor rink at Riverview Community Centre. You're walking home with your hands buried in thin gloves. Claire's beer-league game has ended, but she's still at the boards, threading a new lace through a teammate's skate. The teammate holds it steady and lets her work.

"You found your way here," Claire says when she sees you.

"By accident."

She looks at your hands. "Where are your spare mittens?"

"In the car." You admit you're still trying to work out which layer goes where. She reaches into her bag and hands you a dry pair. You try to return them once your fingers warm up. "Keep them until next time," she says. You hadn't known there would be a next time.

After the game, you go with her to Tim Hortons. Claire sees you studying the menu as if there's an exam coming. "Tim Horton played hockey," she says. "He opened the first one in Hamilton in 1964." She tells you a double-double means two creams and two sugars.

You order one. It's sweeter than you expect, and warm enough that you carry the cup with both borrowed mittens. On the walk back, you practice the words under your breath.

"I can hear you," she says.

You tell her you want to get it right. She looks at the mittens, then at the gloves still sticking out of your coat pocket. "You've learned the coffee before the gloves."

At the parking lot, you ask if she'd like to have dinner sometime. Claire checks the time before she answers. "Let me see what my shifts look like."

## The late shift dinner
Your shifts don't line up for a while, but Claire finds an evening she can promise you. Dinner is late, near The Forks. Lights run along the riverfront, and your wet boots sit under a table just wide enough for two plates. You get there early enough to see her come in right on time.

She's thirty-one. Her mother's family lives near Drummondville, in Quebec. She tells you both things while deciding whether she wants another cup of tea. When you ask about next Tuesday, she puts the menu down.

"My shifts can change. Tuesday evenings are hard to promise."

You nod.

"If I cancel, I'll tell you."

"Okay."

She watches you for a moment. Outside, somebody crosses the snowy walkway with their shoulders up around their ears. Claire wraps both hands around her cup. "People hear 'cancel' and decide I didn't want to come in the first place."

You can see how tired she is. She has still made time to sit across from you, and you're glad you asked. "I'll wait for you to tell me," you say.

Her phone lights up beside her plate. She reads the screen, turns it face down, and finishes dinner with you. You talk about your first month in Winnipeg. She asks whether you've learned to plug in the car. You have.

When you ask to see her again, she opens her calendar. "Saturday. Eleven in the morning. I can keep that."

You leave together, and she takes your hand on the icy stretch toward the parking lot. Saturday is four days away. You put it in your calendar before you start the car.

## Thanksgiving in October
By spring, you know which grocery store stays open after Claire's late shifts. You meet her there under lights that make everyone look tired, and she hands you half the list. Sometimes dinner is whatever you can carry home in one bag.

In June, she gets you onto skates again. Your first lap around the indoor rink takes forever. Claire finds a seat by the boards while you work out how to stop.

"How was that?"

"Slow."

"Then you saw plenty."

By August, you walk beside the Assiniboine River after sunset. The air smells like cut grass and water. You reach for her hand before the path gets slippery, and she turns her palm toward yours.

In September, she asks you to meet her parents. They're coming to Winnipeg for Thanksgiving, and you spend the next month wondering how much of Arizona you need to explain over dinner.

Claire circles the second Monday in October on your calendar. Canada has kept Thanksgiving on that Monday since a proclamation in January 1957, so her family is carving turkey while your mother in Arizona is still planning for November. "My aunt's house. One o'clock." At dinner, her mother puts a slice of tourtiere on your plate and asks if you've had enough before you've picked up your fork.

Her father says something to her mother in French. She smiles. You smile too, though you're a sentence behind. At breakfast, you turn the cereal box from English to French while Claire's father makes coffee. English and French have both been Canada's official languages since the Official Languages Act of 1969, and here even breakfast comes in both. You ask him what one word means. He tells you, then asks if you want more toast.

Dinner gets louder when the poutine arrives. The fresh cheese curds squeak against your teeth. Claire's uncle mentions Fernand Lachance's snack bar in Warwick, back in 1957. Her cousin says Drummondville has a claim too. Her uncle shakes his head. Her cousin won't budge. Claire watches you try to follow the argument.

"You could've warned me."

"You would've missed the first round."

Later, her mother finds you carrying plates to the kitchen. "Has Claire told you how many shifts she works?"

"She tells me when she's free. I meet her then."

Claire is close enough to hear you. She takes two plates from your hands and carries them beside you.

## The call from Quebec
In December, Claire calls while you're closing the pharmacy. Her mother has been taken to a hospital in Quebec, where she's visiting family. The doctors expect her to recover, but her father needs help while she's there.

Claire has already spoken to her supervisor. She tells you when she's flying east and which shifts she's moved. You listen, holding your keys against the cold window by the staff door.

"Could you water the plants?"

"Of course."

"And there's a parcel coming."

"I'll get it."

The next morning, you let yourself into her apartment. The soil in one pot is dry. Her spare skate lace lies across the table, where she repaired it after the last game. You water the plants, collect the parcel from downstairs, and send her a photograph of the lace. She sends back a small message: "Keep that. I'll need it."

That evening, you call during her travel day. It rings out. You look at the flight information, then put your phone down and make dinner you barely taste. When she calls, she's at her father's place near Drummondville. You can hear dishes in the background.

"I can't always answer."

"I know."

She goes quiet for a moment. "Mom asked about you."

"What did you say?"

"That you found my parcel."

On Thursday, Claire asks if you can come for the weekend. You take a bag small enough to carry through the airport without waiting for it. At the hospital, her father meets you in a corridor where the lights stay bright even late at night. He speaks slowly in French. You catch Claire's name and lose the rest.

"I'm sorry," you say.

He starts again in English. Claire looks up from the chair beside her mother's room. You thank him, and he sits with you while she goes in.

Her mother comes home before you leave. On your last morning, Claire stands at the kitchen counter with a cup she hasn't touched. Through the window, her father is clearing snow from the steps.

"I was scared," she says. "Even when they said she'd be okay."

You stay beside her. She tells you about the call from the hospital, and about the long wait before she could see her mother. When she reaches for the cup, you move it closer.

## Sugar-shack spring
The next spring, you go back to Quebec with Claire. Her mother's family gathers at a cabane a sucre near Drummondville, where damp coats hang close together and the long tables fill before everyone has found a seat. Her mother waves you over. There's room beside her.

Claire brings you outside to a trough of clean snow. Someone pours hot maple syrup across it in narrow lines. You watch it settle into taffy while Claire presses a wooden stick against one end of hers.

"Wait a little," she says. "Then roll."

You start too soon. Yours slides away from the stick.

Her mother steps up beside you, takes one look at Claire's, and tells her she's lifting hers too early as well. Claire argues for three seconds. Her mother demonstrates, rolls the taffy neatly, and hands it to you. Then she goes back inside to see whether anyone has saved her a place near the coffee.

At the table, her father passes you a dish of baked beans cooked in maple syrup. Her uncle tells you Quebec makes roughly seventy per cent of the world's maple syrup, according to its producers, and that they keep a strategic reserve of it. You assume he's joking. He isn't. Claire's cousin asks him to pass the bread. Across from you, her mother points out that your second stick is dripping onto your plate. You catch it this time. She gives you a napkin and keeps talking to her sister.

After lunch, you help carry a stack of empty trays. Claire's relatives call to one another over the scrape of chairs. You understand enough French to take the trays where they're wanted, though you still need her father to repeat the name of the room. He does, and leaves you to carry them in.

Outside, the sun has softened the snow beside the path. You have the things her mother has sent back with you tucked in one arm: a jar, a wrapped piece of cake, a bag of maple candies. Claire takes your bare hand.

At the car, she glances toward the roof. You follow her eyes. One of the mittens is sitting there beside the keys, where you put it while opening the door. You pick it up before she says anything.

"Got it," you say.

"I see."

On the drive away, you ask when you can come back. She checks her shifts, then reaches across the seat for your hand.

By the first hot evening in Manitoba, she's taking you somewhere her family goes to get away from their calendars.

## The dock near Gimli
The cottage near Gimli has a screened door that slaps shut behind you, unless the bag in your hand catches it first. Mosquitoes still find the gap. Claire's father has left a note on the kitchen table explaining where the spare sheets are, though Claire knows. She reads it anyway and smiles.

Outside, Lake Winnipeg runs past the end of the dock. A loon calls from somewhere you can't see. The boards hold the day's heat under your bare feet.

Your first job is carrying supplies in from the car. You bring the cooler to the kitchen, then stop before going back.

"What do you need next?"

"The bag with the towels. And the water."

You find both. Claire watches from the doorway while you set the bottles in the fridge. She doesn't say anything about it. She just hands you the cupboard key.

By evening, you've eaten sandwiches at the little table and learned that the porch light draws more mosquitoes than it discourages. Claire comes down to the dock with two mugs of tea. She sets hers beside yours and sits close enough that your shoulders touch.

"I like it here," she says. "I keep thinking I should come more."

"You could."

She looks out at the water. Her next stretch of shifts starts Tuesday. Her mother wants a visit in September. Your pharmacy has already asked about the holiday rota.

"I know what next week looks like," she says. "I don't always know where to put everything else."

You turn toward her. "Do you want to keep doing this with me?"

"Yes." She looks at you properly. "I want you there when I get home. Even when I don't know when that is."

The ring has spent the drive in the front pocket of your bag, under a clean pair of socks. You go up for it and come back trying to breathe normally. She sees the little box before you reach her.

"Claire, will you marry me?"

"Yes," she says, while you're still holding it out. "Come here."

She holds your hand steady while you put the ring on. Across the water, the loon calls again.

"Have you told my mother?"

"No."

Claire laughs and takes out her phone. Her mother answers while you're both still on the dock, and by the time Claire says your name, you can hear her father asking to be put on speaker.

## The wedding near Drummondville
The following autumn, rain sends your wedding in Saint-Cyrille-de-Wendover indoors. You're carrying extra chairs from the covered porch when Claire's father catches the other end of the stack.

"You have a shirt to keep clean," he says.

"So do you."

He looks down at his sleeves, already damp. "Too late."

Inside, your family from Arizona helps make room between the tables. Claire's mother moves the flowers out of the draft by the door. Somebody finds dry cloths for the chair seats. By the time the guests arrive, everyone has a place, and your uncle knows which of Claire's cousins will point him toward it.

You see Claire just before the ceremony. She's standing near the window, holding her mother's hands. When she notices you, she lets go with one hand and gives you a small wave. You wave back. Her mother sees that too.

Your vows are short because you both know what you're promising. Claire looks at you and remembers the January morning you nearly missed your shift. You tell her you remember the end of that day, when she came back to your parking spot and helped you start the car.

"I'll come back," she says.

"I know you will."

Afterward, her father stands to speak. He starts in French, and you catch Claire's name, her mother's, and a sentence about how she keeps her word. When he switches to English, your parents lean in. Claire's relatives turn to them during the next French joke, making sure they get it before everyone stops laughing. Her father waits, pleased with himself, and carries on.

At dinner, your mother asks Claire's mother for the name of the pastry on her plate. She says tourtiere slowly enough for your mother to try it. They spend the next few minutes talking about what each of them makes when family comes over. You listen while Claire steals a bite from your plate.

Outside, the rain has eased, but the air is cold for photographs. Claire takes off one mitten and straightens your collar. Her fingers are chilly against your neck.

"There."

"You need your mitten."

"I know." She pulls it back on and reaches for your hand.

A few days later, the chairs are gone and so are the guests. Back in Winnipeg, her next start time is on the fridge beside yours.

## The first married week
On your first Monday back in Winnipeg, the laundry is still in the suitcase, and the fridge holds mustard, eggs, and a piece of wedding cake wrapped by Claire's mother. You both leave before the grocery store opens. By the time you're home, Claire has a shift starting in nine hours and you have socks drying over the back of a chair.

You go out for food while she sleeps. When you get back, you put the bags away without waking her.

Some mornings, you leave coffee ready. When she's come home late, you close the bedroom door and let her have another hour. She knows when your pharmacy shift ends now. Once, you find a message asking if you want her to pick up bread on her way home. She sends it five minutes before you leave work.

On Thursday, though, there's no message. You finish your shift, buy groceries, and check your phone outside the store. Claire said she'd call when she was free. By nine, you're sitting at the kitchen table with the milk still on the counter.

She gets home tired and sets down her keys. "You waited up."

"You said you'd call."

"I know. I'm sorry."

You put the milk in the fridge. "I was worried."

She sits down, still wearing her coat. "I couldn't get to my phone. When I had a minute, I didn't know when I'd have the next one."

You pull out the chair across from her. "I don't need a whole conversation."

"I can send you 'late' when I get a chance," she says. "If I can't, I'll call when I finish. Can we do that?"

"Yeah."

She reaches across the table for your hand. You tell her the groceries are mostly things that can wait another day. That makes her laugh.

On Saturday, an envelope arrives from her mother. Inside is a handwritten tourtiere recipe, with a note asking how the two of you are eating. Claire reads it aloud while you clear space on the counter.

"Should the filling cool before it goes into the pastry?" you ask.

"Yes. Help me with the onions?"

You do. Claire rolls the pastry while you watch her turn it without tearing an edge. Later, you wash the flour from the counter and leave a slice for her next shift.

When you visit her family again, the recipe comes with you, folded inside the grocery list.

## A weekend with her family
At a grocery store outside Drummondville, Claire's father hands you the list her mother wrote that morning. Claire is helping her mother at home, so it's just the two of you under the bright lights of the produce aisle.

You recognize enough French on the labels to find the carrots and onions. You even find the thyme without turning the packet over. Her father notices, but he lets you keep looking.

At the spice shelf, you point to the next item on the list.

"Bay leaves?"

"Feuilles de laurier," he says.

You find them wedged between two things you can't pronounce. He adds them to the basket. At the checkout, you remember the words and ask the cashier for one more packet. Claire's father waits until you're outside to tell you her mother only asked for one.

"Now she has enough," he says.

Her cousin from Toronto is there for dinner, coat still damp from the drive. She tells Claire about a neighbor who keeps returning her baking dishes with food in them. Claire's mother wants to know what came back this time. Her cousin tells them the whole story while you set the table.

You know where the extra forks live now. You bring them out before anybody asks.

Over dinner, Claire's father asks what your family does for Christmas Eve in Arizona. You tell him about driving through Winterhaven in Tucson to see the lights. Your dad insists on parking several blocks away because he says the walk is part of it. Every year, he brings a thermos that leaks cocoa onto the car seat.

Claire looks up from her plate.

"Does he still make room for you in the car?"

"He says he does."

She asks which lights you remember best. You tell her about a house with little paper bags glowing along the walkway, and about your father standing there until everyone else is ready to move. Claire's never heard you talk about that night. You show her a picture on your phone. She spots your father by the walkway. "He's waiting," she says.

Before you leave, her mother hands you the tourtiere recipe again. She's marked a correction in the margin beside the pastry. You put the page in your bag, where it won't get crushed.

Back in Winnipeg, the first snow stays on the ground.

## The rink after dark
Claire plays on Saturday night at the St. James Civic Centre. You sit close enough to the glass to hear skates bite into the ice. Her teammate loses a lace halfway through the game, the same one Claire helped at the outdoor rink when you first met her again.

The teammate looks toward the bench. Claire already has a spare in her hand. She passes it over and taps the boards twice before heading back out.

Afterward, you wait by the doors while the players come through with wet hair and heavy bags. Claire reaches into her bag for her mittens. You hand her the dry pair you brought from home.

"These are mine," she says.

"I figured yours would still be wet."

She puts them on. Outside, the wind gets through the gap between your collar and your scarf. Claire walks beside you, quiet all the way to the car.

At home, she stands in the kitchen with her bag still over one shoulder. She has another early shift on Monday. There's a dish towel on the counter and a pile of clean clothes waiting to be folded. She looks at both.

"I know you do things," she says. "I just get tired of being the one who remembers them first."

You set your keys down.

"I'll handle the laundry tomorrow."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

She nods and goes to shower. You gather the clothes before you go to bed, including the things that need a cold wash. On Sunday, you run the loads, check the pockets, and put the towels away where she can find them. You leave her work clothes hanging to dry.

Claire comes home and opens the closet. She touches the sleeve of her uniform, then closes the door.

"Thanks," she says.

After dinner, the forecast comes on while you rinse two plates. The temperature is dropping hard overnight. Claire stops beside the window. Down in the parking lot, the lights catch the little outlet beside your space.

She looks at it for a moment, then turns toward the sink.

You see where she looked.

## Before bed
Before bed, you check tomorrow's pharmacy shift on your phone. Claire starts earlier than you do. Her blue parka hangs by the door, one sleeve turned inside out from the way she took it off.

She's asleep with the bedroom light still on. You turn it off and stand there for a second, listening to the heat come through the vent.

Your keys are on the kitchen counter. You take them and go downstairs.

Outside, the cold finds the skin above your scarf. The parking lot is nearly empty, and your shoes make a dry sound on the packed snow. You reach the car and run your fingers along the grille until you find the block-heater cord.

It catches for a moment. You pull it free, check that it reaches, and fit the plug into the outlet beside your space. It stays there. You give it a small tug anyway.

Upstairs, you set your keys beside Claire's. The dry mittens are on the chair where she left them after the game. You put both pairs on the shelf by the door, close enough to grab on the way out.

Claire hasn't moved. You leave a glass of water on her side of the bed and lie down carefully. Outside the window, the parking-lot lights shine on the cord between the car and the outlet.

At seven, frost covers the windshield again.

## Seven in the morning
You step into the parking lot with your coat on and the keys from beside Claire's in your hand.

The cord comes free of the outlet with one firm pull. You tuck it behind the grille, where it won't drag, and turn the key. The engine starts before you have time to hold your breath. You let it warm while you scrape the windshield, including the corners you once decided you could probably see around.

By the time Claire comes downstairs, the glass is clear. She's wearing the blue parka. Her hair is still damp from the shower, and she stops beside the empty outlet before she opens the passenger door.

"You remembered."

"Yeah."

She looks at the car, then at you. Her shoulders settle a little. She gets in without asking whether you checked the forecast or found the scraper. You hand her the travel mug you filled upstairs, and she takes a sip before pulling off her mittens.

The streets toward her station are slow with morning traffic. At the second red light, she reaches into the shelf below the dashboard and finds the dry spare pair. You put them there because she'd need them on the walk from the car. She turns one over in her lap, then lays her hand on yours.

You remember the red light where she told you her name. Her hand stays on yours while you wait. The light turns green, and you drive on toward the coffee stop.

## The coffee stop
You pull into the short line of cars and keep the heater low. Claire holds her travel mug between both hands. One mitten rests beside the spare keys in the cup holder; the other has slipped between the seat and the door. You leave it there for now. She has coffee, but you know she'll want another for later. You check the clock. She has time.

Through the clear windshield, you can see the order speaker ahead. There's no little scraped hole to aim through. Claire follows your glance toward the speaker. "What do you want?"

"I'll order."

She turns toward you. "Okay."

The car ahead moves up. You ease forward and stop, close enough that you won't have to lean across Claire to be heard. She watches you reach for the window switch, and her mouth lifts at one corner. She doesn't supply the words.

You remember her dry gloves at the rink. You ask if she wants anything else.

"Something to eat, if they have it."

You choose her usual from the menu. She nods. You order her coffee and breakfast, then your double-double.

At the pickup window, cold air catches your hand. Claire takes her coffee and wraps your stiff fingers around her warm travel mug. You reach beside her seat for the fallen mitten and pull it over her free hand.
