# Recherche et consignes — POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Pirate

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS about her world (use several, rounded, each inside a scene, with the source named in the sentence; never invent other statistics):
- The league: historian Dian Murray (summarised by JSTOR Daily) counts about 400 junks and 70,000 people in the confederation by 1804; Richard Glasspoole, held captive in 1809, heard "near seventy thousand men", some 800 large vessels and nearly a thousand small ones; Robert Antony (Review of Culture, Macao, 2019) puts the Red Banner fleet alone at nearly 20,000 pirates and several hundred ships. Fleets went by flag colour (six chiefs in Yuan Yonglun's Chinese history of the pirates, translated 1831; Glasspoole counted five). Say "hundreds of junks, tens of thousands of people".
- 1807: Yuan Yonglun writes that Zheng Yi died in a heavy gale late in 1807 and his widow put the crews under Zhang Baozai while she remained commander of the whole; Antony says the two jointly commanded the Red Banner fleet. Zhang Baozai was the son of a Tanka fishing family, taken by Zheng Yi as a teenager (Antony).
- Paperwork: Yuan Yonglun says Zheng Yi Sao "was very strict in every transaction; nothing could be done without a written application", all plunder was entered in a storehouse register, a pirate kept two parts in ten and eight went to the common fund, and the purser of the storehouse was nicknamed "Ink and writing master". Plunder was politely called "a transhipping of goods".
- Rules (non-graphic only): going ashore without leave was punished, a second time with death; taking from the common fund without permission was a capital crime; villagers had to be paid for rice and wine (Yuan Yonglun). Marrying without permission was forbidden (Yuan Yonglun), and Glasspoole wrote that no man could keep a woman aboard "unless married to her according to their laws".
- Families aboard: Antony writes that many Dan (Tanka) "water people" lived their entire lives on boats, the water home to the whole family; some women married into piracy, others were born into it, and women proved as capable as men in battle and ship's duties. Glasspoole saw each man given a berth about four feet square, where he lived with his wife and children.
- Gods: Antony describes pirates lighting incense and praying to Mazu before a raid. Glasspoole says they consulted their idol "on all occasions" and once refused to attack a town because it had not promised success; a pig or fowls offered to it were divided among the crew after a few hours; the chief's wife sprinkled him with garlic water as a charm against cannon shot.
- Daily life (Glasspoole): coarse brown rice, salt fish, three weeks of caterpillars boiled with rice; cards in every free hour; junks hauled onto a Lantau beach to repair their hulls; a mast shot away replaced with one taken from a smaller junk.
- Protection: Glasspoole notes that only boats carrying "a pass from the Ladrones" dared to carry messages, and that a town asked for a yearly tribute of 10,000 dollars agreed to pay 6,000. Antony says pirate bosses openly ran "tax bureaus" in Macao collecting ransoms and protection fees; historian James Carter (The China Project) says the pirates taxed the salt trade.
- Salt: Antony describes hundreds of salt junks sailing each year between the salt fields of Dianbai and Canton, and a 1801 raid that plundered eighteen salt junks at Shuidong.
- Ransom: Antony cites a 1809 case where pirates put one captive ashore to carry the ransom note to the ship's owner. Glasspoole, an officer of the East India Company ship Marquis of Ely, taken near Macao in September 1809 with seven men, had to write the demand himself (100,000 dollars at first); the ransom finally paid was scarlet cloth, opium, gunpowder, a telescope and dollars; the chief complained the telescope was not new and took 100 dollars instead. Captivity: eleven weeks and three days.
- Seasons: Antony says piracy rose each year from about April to August, with the southwest monsoon and the trading fleets, and fell sharply around November and December.
- Pressure, 1808-1809: Carter says the pirates destroyed 63 of the 135 navy ships guarding Guangdong in 1808. Glasspoole describes the November 1809 blockade near Lantau by 93 war junks and six Portuguese ships: eight fire-ships sent in at night were towed ashore, put out and broken up for firewood; after nine days the fleet sailed out without losing a junk.
- Bai Ling, governor-general from 1809 (Antony, Carter), used rewards and amnesties; several thousand pirates defected (Carter). Yuan Yonglun records the Black Banner chief Guo Podai surrendering around January 1810 with about 8,000 men, 126 vessels and 500 large guns, and being made a junior officer.
- Surrender, April 1810: Carter and Yuan Yonglun say Zheng Yi Sao went to Canton herself with women and children to the governor-general to settle terms; the fleet then submitted at Furongsha near Xiangshan; every vessel received pork and wine, every man a paper bill for money, and each could join the government forces or go home to the land (Yuan Yonglun). Totals differ: Glasspoole heard about 16,000 men; Macao Magazine (2019) gives about 280 vessels, 2,000 guns and more than 25,000 people (say "well over fifteen thousand people, a couple of hundred junks").
- Afterwards: Zhang Baozai became an officer in the Qing navy, fought the remaining pirates and died in 1822 (Antony). Zheng Yi Sao moved ashore, ran a gambling house in Macao and later in Canton, and died in 1844 (Antony). Yuan Yonglun says the emperor rewarded Bai Ling with a two-eyed peacock feather.
- Outside world: Britannica says the Canton system confined Western trade to Guangzhou from about 1760 to 1842, the East India Company buying tea above all. Frederic Wakeman (East Asian History, 2004): in September 1808, during the Napoleonic wars, Admiral Drury landed 300 marines and sepoys at Macao to "protect" it from the French; Canton stopped all English trade until they left on 20 December 1808.
STORY SHAPE: Pearl River delta and the Guangdong coast, summer 1805 to 1811. "You" are FICTIONAL: "you" (the narrator always says "you"; you have NO name in the story; your employer is a separate, unnamed salt merchant ashore), 24, bookkeeper on a salt junk running between Dianbai and Canton (Antony); beautiful handwriting, honest to a fault, seasick every single voyage, keeps an abacus and a brush case in a waxed bag. She is FICTIONAL: Captain Sui, 26, born on a Tanka boat, commands one junk of the Red Flag fleet, dry and short with words, never seasick, thinks land is "badly tied down". HOOK (0-15%): summer 1805, raiding season (Antony); three junks with batten sails come alongside, nobody fights, the salt becomes "a transhipping of goods" (Yuan Yonglun); Sui reads your cargo list twice and decides you are worth more alive than ransomed; your first job: writing the ransom note to your own owner, carried ashore by the cook (Antony's 1809 case). You are sick over the side; she hands you rice without comment. 15-45%: the four-foot berths, families, children on deck (Glasspoole, Antony); the register, two parts in ten (Yuan Yonglun); you become the junk's "Ink and writing master"; you write passes for fishing and salt boats (Glasspoole, Carter); a town asked for 10,000 pays 6,000 (Glasspoole); she teaches you knots, tides, monsoons, clouds, how to stand on a moving deck; the Mazu shrine, incense before each voyage, a pig offered then shared (Glasspoole); 1807, a gale: Zheng Yi is dead, his widow and Zhang Baozai run everything (Yuan Yonglun), seen only as a distant flagship and a stack of new rules; the first application you see Sui sign is for more rope. 45-50% LOVE AND WEDDING: you choose to stay; you write the application to marry because nothing happens without one (Yuan Yonglun, Glasspoole: married "according to their laws"); she corrects one character; permission comes back stamped; a small ceremony at the shrine, a pig shared with the crew; she gives you a dry jacket, you give her a new register bound in red cloth. 50-55%: married life: one shared berth, rice and salt fish, cards at night (Glasspoole), typhoon season tying everything down twice, she lets you steer once. 55-85% (scenes): (1) 1808, 63 of 135 navy ships gone (Carter), the crew too pleased, you worried; (2) autumn 1808, Drury's troops at Macao, Canton trade frozen (Wakeman), tea stuck in warehouses; (3) the junk hauled onto a Lantau beach to scrape the hull (Glasspoole), the one night she sleeps on land and hates it; (4) 1809, Bai Ling arrives (Antony, Carter), amnesty notices on temple doors, two of your crew quietly leave; (5) September 1809, a foreign prisoner in a wet coat seen across the water (Glasspoole, finding only, never speaks); his ransom: scarlet cloth, dollars and a second-hand telescope (Glasspoole); (6) DANGER ~65%: November 1809, the blockade at Lantau, 93 war junks and Portuguese ships (Glasspoole), cannon smoke far off, non-graphic; the mast is shot away and replaced from a smaller junk; Sui sprinkles you with garlic water (Glasspoole), you are not sure it works, nobody is hurt on your junk; (7) the fire-ships drift in, are towed ashore and become firewood (Glasspoole); she cooks rice on them; (8) the ninth day, the fleet sails out; you write the damage list with shaking hands and she checks your sums; (9) winter, the season goes quiet (Antony), you teach her to write her own name. CRISIS ~80%: January 1810, the flag fleets quarrel, the Black Banner surrenders with 8,000 men and 126 vessels (Yuan Yonglun); rumours, half the crew wants pardon, half wants one more season; you are asked to copy vessel lists for the talks; Sui admits she is afraid of land, not of the navy. ENDING 85-100%: April 1810, women and children go to Canton first to settle terms (Carter, Yuan Yonglun); at Furongsha the junks submit, pork and wine for every vessel, a paper bill for every man (Yuan Yonglun), in handwriting worse than yours; join the government ships or go ashore (Yuan Yonglun): you choose land, together; she sways for three days. 1811: your letter-writing stall on the Canton waterfront, her ferry sampan; findings: Zhang Baozai an officer, Zheng Yi Sao's gambling house ashore until 1844 (Antony). Last image: your marriage application, stamped by the fleet, pinned above the stall, the one paper you were never asked to give back.
RULES: The narrator "you" (never named, never split into a second character), Captain Sui and the crew, owners, prisoners and clerks are fictional; real people (Zheng Yi Sao, Zhang Baozai, Zheng Yi, Guo Podai, Bai Ling, Glasspoole, Drury) only as findings or distant figures, never speaking, never close up; Sui is an adult (26 in 1805) in every scene; marrying and staying is both your free choices. NO gore, torture, executions, sexual violence or anything sexual; the rules about captive women, the sale of captives, the head bounties and the beatings in Glasspoole's narrative are NOT used; raids and battles stay distant (sails, smoke, a damage list), no deaths shown; never glamorize killing or piracy: the real cost (villages paying, prisoners waiting) said plainly; opium only as a word in the ransom list, never shown. No Chinese caricature: no fake accents, no "ancient proverb" voice, no slanted-eye drawings, no gong sounds. Correct look for 1805-1810: wooden junks with batten sails of matting, eyes painted on the bow, small cannon, sampans; men in Qing-era loose jackets and trousers, shaved forehead and long queue; boat women in loose jackets and trousers, hair in a bun or braid, bare feet on deck, wide bamboo hats; Mazu shrine with incense aboard; Macao with Portuguese churches and forts; Canton waterfront with junks and Western ships. Advertiser-friendly; calm, dry, funny.

EXTRA RULE: the berth size (about four feet square) is said once only. Mention opium only as one item on Glasspoole's ransom list, never shown.
