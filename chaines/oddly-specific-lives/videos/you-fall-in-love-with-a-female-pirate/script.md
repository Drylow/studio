# POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Pirate

Captain Sui reads your cargo list twice while you're throwing up over the side of the salt junk she's taken. Your employer in Canton will make you pay for the books. Sui slaps the mast with your list, and the man holding your collar lets go. "Write," she says, putting a clean sheet beside the inkstone. "Tell your employer you're alive." She watches you form the first character, puts your list in her coat, and walks toward the stern. Your ransom ought to be enough. So why does she keep your cargo list?

## The ransom note
Sui sets your cargo list beside a stack of sacks. "Check the total," she says. Off Lantau in the summer of 1805, three wooden junks with batten sails rock beside your employer's salt junk. Nobody reaches for a weapon. You reach for the waxed bag that holds your abacus and brush case, then lean over the side because the sea has found you again.

You're twenty-four. You've been sick in front of strangers before. This time, one of them has your cargo list.

Captain Sui reads the note you write to your employer. She gives it to the cook, who takes it ashore in a small boat. You can't see who receives it. Right now, you watch the boat get smaller and wonder what your employer thinks you're worth.

Sui calls you back to the sacks.

"Count them."

You do. The first stack agrees with the list. The second doesn't. You check the marks on each sack, then turn a page you thought she hadn't noticed. Two entries disagree because your employer records one shipment twice.

"That salt isn't here," you say.

"I thought so."

She's been waiting for you to find it. Her crew has to account for what comes aboard, and she's seen enough of your handwriting to know you can follow your own figures.

She taps the list. "He says it's all here. Perhaps that comforts him."

You're too frightened to ask whether she finds that comforting. You certainly don't.

The deck lifts. You lose your breakfast again. When you look up, Sui has set a bowl of rice within reach.

You correct the figures while it cools. The cook returns with your employer's reply.

## A place to sleep
Your employer pays the ransom. Sui reads his reply, folds it once, and tells you the next landing is safe. She'll put you ashore there.

You ought to feel better. Instead, you look at the corrected list and ask who keeps the accounts after you go.

"I do," she says. "Badly, when I'm busy."

She offers wages for the work. You ask for one voyage, and the right to leave when it ends. She agrees before you can make the terms sound brave.

Below deck, Sui shows you a berth about four feet square. A man sleeps here with his wife and children. You can't work out where they put their feet. Here, the books are wrapped above the bedding, out of the damp. You have to move a child's little water bucket to sit down.

On deck, two children carry water in smaller buckets and get under everyone's feet. A woman repairs a torn sail beside the mast. Sui asks how long she needs, listens to the answer, and changes the sailing hour. The families aboard are Dan, also called Tanka. Their children sleep through the deck's rolling.

"I was born on one," Sui says.

You wait for her to add something. She goes to check the sail.

That night, you tie your waxed brush case beside the berth. The junk rolls. Your knot opens almost at once. Sui catches the bag before it slides, reties the line, and goes back on deck.

At the safe landing, she points out the path to shore. Nobody stands in your way. You stay aboard for the voyage you chose.

By morning, you're following a bundle of account books to another junk. There are boats waiting beside it, and each one needs something.

## Ink and writing master
The storehouse junk smells of wet rope and dried fish. You sit beside the register while sacks, tools and cloth come aboard. Each item gets entered before anyone takes a share.

You enter the shares in the register yourself. The purser shows you the fleet's written rules. Two parts in ten go to whoever brought in the haul, eight to the common fund. Requests for rope come in writing too. At the desk, you begin to understand why Sui wants your hand. The same stores have to serve boats you haven't even seen.

A purser leans over your shoulder. "That number's right."

You turn the register toward him. One sack appears on two lines. He checks the marks on the sacks themselves, crosses out an entry, and takes your brush to write the correction.

"Ink and writing master," he calls you.

Sui uses the name once, when you drop your abacus beads across the deck. She helps you gather them while you refuse to look at her.

Your first written application asks for rope. Sui signs it. Later, the rope arrives.

"Why do you need permission?" you ask.

She points to the junk beside yours. Its crew waits for rope too. You file Sui's application where both boats' requests can be found.

A few days later, men from a coastal village come aboard with payment for protection. One of them asks whether the amount buys his fishing boats safe passage. You write down the answer and see how carefully he watches the brush. He pays because refusing feels less safe.

That evening, you check the sum twice. Sui waits for you to finish before she signs it.

Your handwriting has earned you a place aboard. Now there's a village name beside it, and another blank line below.

## The pass
The village name is still wet when a fishing boat pulls alongside. Its owner needs to carry messages through waters your fleet controls, and he keeps glancing at the empty space where you usually put a seal. You draft the pass while he waits. "Without your seal, they'll turn me back," the fisherman says. "I need to get these messages through." You understand why the fisherman wants one. You wish he didn't need it.

By noon, three representatives from a nearby town sit across from Sui. They have brought their own figures, written in a neat hand. The eldest asks for a lower yearly payment. Sui reads their figures and asks how many boats the town sends out each month.

"Fewer than last year," he says.

"Then write fewer."

You lower the amount. He reads it again before he puts his mark beside it. The eldest folds the paper carefully. His shoulders loosen, but he checks the smaller figure once more before leaving.

At the landing, Sui sees one of her crew carry off rice and wine without opening his purse. She calls him back. "Pay for the rice and wine," she tells him. Sui makes the man pay while the representatives watch.

"They brought us money already," you say when they've gone.

"I know."

You take the pass back to your table. The figure on it is correct, and the fisherman needs it. Neither fact makes his choice feel free.

"Why keep me at the books?" you ask. "I keep questioning your figures."

"Because you challenge them."

She leaves you to finish. The pass dries on the table while your brush case slides across the pitching deck.

## Learning the deck
You catch the brush case with your foot and nearly follow it overboard. Sui catches your sleeve.

"Try the line," she says.

Over the following months, she shows you a knot that holds when the deck rolls. She teaches you to watch the water against a piling for the change in the tide. You learn to bend your knees instead of grabbing whoever happens to stand closest. You still get sick every voyage.

"Land is badly tied down," she tells you after you complain about the motion.

You wait for her to admit that's a joke. She doesn't.

Before one sailing, incense burns at the small Mazu shrine aboard. Sui gives a child room to set down the incense without spilling it. Later, after a pig is offered, the cook divides the meat and makes sure everyone gets a portion. Your stomach accepts its share cautiously.

Rain comes through a seam above the sleeping families. You move your waxed bag away from a child's bedding and put a bowl under the drip. Sui notices. That night, while you check the stores, she sits beside you.

"When we haul the junk ashore, I can't sleep," she says. "Everything stays still."

You hold your brush over the page. For once, she has told you something an order cannot fix.

Your one voyage ends at a landing with a safe road inland. Your wages are ready. Sui sets them beside your brush case and waits while you count.

"I'd take pay for another," you say.

"How long?"

You look at the road, then at the line she taught you to tie.

"One more voyage."

She nods. You keep your footing when the junk pulls away. By late 1807, even that begins to feel like a small skill.

## The gale
The word comes from a distant flagship in late 1807. Zheng Yi has died in a heavy gale. You hear it from a messenger who has to repeat himself over the wind.

Sui reads the message beside the mast. Her crew waits for her to speak, though nobody has asked them to. She tells them when they sail and which stores to load. Afterward, she reads the page again.

New instructions follow under Zheng Yi's widow's authority. The instructions reach your junk on paper. You read them to Sui. The crews answer to Zhang Baozai, while Zheng Yi's widow commands the whole Red Banner fleet with him. Sui still has to make them work with the people and rope she has aboard.

Before the next sailing, she checks the mast herself. She presses one palm against the wood, then calls down to you.

"Where's the rope application I signed?"

You show her the file you keep for stores requests. Both boats' applications are there, where the crew can find them. She takes the page she needs and sends a man for rope.

"You kept it," she says.

"You told me to."

"I told you to file it."

You hear the difference. It stays with you while you check the next stores count.

A crewman watches you fold your pay into your waxed bag. "You'll go ashore now," he says.

You almost tell him yes. The command has changed, and Sui answers for this junk each time she sails it. Your entries help decide what her crew has enough of, which boat gets a pass and whose name stays on the provisions list. You can leave those pages to someone else.

At the next port, there is a ferry, a day's pay in your bag and enough time to catch it.

## The shore you pass
The ferry is still tied up when Sui puts your wages on a crate beside the landing. Morning light catches the water between the boats. The ferryman is eating rice and shows no interest in you.

"You have time," she says.

You put the coins in your waxed bag. Nobody watches your hands. Nobody stands between you and the ferry. That makes it harder to speak.

"I'll stay another season."

Sui looks toward the junk, where a child is trying to lift a water bucket with both hands.

"Then the crew needs to know where to find you. If a pass is questioned, I need the man who wrote it."

"I'll be there."

"And the stores?"

"Those too."

She nods, and goes to help with the bucket. You carry your bag back aboard. By noon, the ferryman has taken someone else across.

The southwest monsoon brings more salt boats along the coast. From April through August, salt boats bound from Dianbai to Canton bring news of raids faster than you can enter their names. You see the season in requests for passes, in rope worn through before its replacement arrives, and in the fresh pages you sew into the register.

One evening, you stand beside Sui at the stern while the water darkens. She puts a line in your hand and tells you to wait for the pull to change. You feel it too late. She sets her hand over yours.

"Again."

This time you feel the weight shift before the junk turns. She leaves her hand there while you take the line yourself. The junk comes around slowly. Her hand stays on yours until you feel the line ease.

## Too pleased
The next accounts reach farther than your junk. In 1808, a boat brings news of navy losses across Guangdong. The man aboard says pirates have destroyed dozens of the ships guarding the province.

Someone opens a jar of wine. The crew calls from deck to deck, asking which navy ships remain. You wait until they finish passing the jar, then ask who has signed for the rice taken aboard that morning. The register stays open while the celebration moves around it.

Sui comes to your table. "You can drink."

"After the count."

"You sound pleased with yourself."

You look up. She is smiling, which makes the unfinished column difficult to read.

By autumn, the Canton waterfront is quieter. A merchant tells you British troops have landed at Macao, and English trade at Canton has stopped. His tea waits in a warehouse across the water. A merchant needs a pass to move a boat. The boatman wants payment before he risks taking it.

You work out a figure that charges them both for the delay. Sui reads it, then lays your brush across the page.

"They're already paying for it," she says.

You make a new entry. The merchant argues over the amount anyway, and Sui lets him finish before she signs.

That night, you find a mistake in her stores figures. She checks the sacks herself, returns, and changes the number in her own hand. She asks you to read it once more. You do. For a while, the two of you sit over the books as the tide drops. By morning, the crew is talking about a scrape along the hull that cannot wait for another voyage.

## One night on Lantau
They haul the junk onto a beach on Lantau to scrape and repair the hull. Men set supports beneath the timber. By late afternoon, the bottom is exposed and the crew can point to the seam that has been taking water.

Sui walks its length with the carpenter. She listens to his estimate, asks what he needs, and tells you to put it on the stores list. After dark, she stays ashore to see what the morning's work will require.

You wake beneath an awning and find her beside a coil of rope, looking out at the junk. Its mast is still against the sky. The beach is quiet enough to hear someone turn in their sleep.

"Nothing moves," she says.

You sit beside her and pick up the loose end of the line. You attempt the knot she taught you at the stern. It comes apart in your hands.

"That one won't hold," she says.

"I had a feeling."

She takes the rope, ties it slowly, then loosens it for you. You try again while she holds the standing part. Your fingers brush hers each time you pass the end through.

You want to ask what happens when there's nowhere to sail. You want to ask where you belong when she has to choose. She is watching your knot, waiting for you to pull it tight.

"I'll be aboard when the hull goes back in," you say.

"I know."

At daybreak, the carpenter asks for more timber and the cook asks for rice. You write a new stores list while Sui walks the repaired seam. When the tide comes in, the crew pushes the junk back into the water.

Above the landing, someone posts a notice offering pardon.

## The notice
The notice above the landing stays dry beneath the eaves while rain runs down the backs of men leaning in to read it.

In 1809, Bai Ling becomes governor-general. The notice offers rewards to captains who surrender and pardon to men who leave their fleets. You know him as a name at the bottom of official papers. The ink holds up better than the paper does.

Two men from Sui's crew come to you after supper. One carries a bowl he hasn't touched. They ask you to read the notice aloud, including the part about what a man must surrender. You read it twice. By morning, their bedding is gone.

Sui looks at the empty berths. She doesn't send anyone after them.

"Take them off the provisions count," she says.

You scrape out two names. You subtract their rations for tomorrow. The rice you save comes to less than you expect.

In September, another junk comes close enough for you to see a foreign prisoner in a wet coat. He stays by the rail and holds up an empty bowl as the boats pass. A man beside him calls across for rice. You check the stores page. The rations from the empty berths won't feed everyone waiting at that rail.

You return to the count. Other junks hold prisoners too. They need feeding. You add their portions beside the scraped-out names.

Sui reads over your shoulder.

"You have the prisoners?"

"They're there."

She checks each mark anyway, then leaves the page with you to copy. By nightfall, the cook knows how much rice to set aside. Across the water, someone lights a lamp on a junk that hasn't moved all day.

## Nine days near Lantau
In November, the junks gather near Lantau, and there are suddenly too many sails on the horizon to count from your deck.

A count reaches your crew: ninety-three war junks and six Portuguese ships in the blockade. You see them through morning haze, their hulls shifting in and out of sight. Cannon smoke hangs over the water. Each time a sail disappears behind it, you wait for it to come back.

A shot takes your mast. The deck tilts beneath your feet, and Sui calls for the rigging to be cleared. Nobody aboard her junk is hurt. A smaller junk brings over a replacement mast, and the crew sets to work while the distant guns continue.

Sui sprinkles garlic water over your shoulders, the charm her crew trusts against cannon shot.

"Hold that line," she says.

You hold it while she checks the men securing the mast. She comes back to test your grip, though the line hasn't slipped.

At night, eight fire-ships drift toward the fleet. You watch their flames spread across the water until boats catch the lines and tow them ashore. The crews put the fires out and break the ships up for firewood. Later, Sui cooks rice over some of it. She watches the pot closely. You can't tell what she thinks of the fuel.

On the ninth day, a way opens and the fleet sails out. Your crew gets clear with the new mast standing.

You write the damage list while your hands still shake. Sui checks the sums, moves your inkstone away from the rolling edge of the table, and signs beneath your name.

The ink is barely dry when winter leaves fewer trading boats on the coast.

## Names on a list
By December, the water has room around your junk again. Trading sails grow scarce along the coast, and the raiding boats thin with them. On calmer evenings, you bring the accounts out where Sui can see them by lamplight.

She signs her name again, slower this time. She makes you show her the marks for what comes in and what goes out, too. When you suggest she rest, she turns the page over.

"Again."

Around January 1810, a report reaches the crew. Black Banner chief Guo Podai has surrendered. The report says about eight thousand men and one hundred twenty-six vessels surrendered with him; the historian Yuan Yonglun later writes down the same numbers. Guo is a name on the page you read aloud. The number of vessels makes the table go quiet.

Half the crew want to ask for pardon. The others want another season. Sui lets each person speak, including the cook, who keeps one hand on the doorframe as he talks. When they finish, she asks you to copy the vessel lists for possible talks.

"Every name," she says. "They'll need to read them."

You work past supper. Men stop by to check how their names appear, and you make room for their corrections.

Later, Sui sits beside you while the ink dries. She studies the shore through the open hatch.

"I'm afraid to live there," she says.

You remember her awake beside the rope on Lantau. You can't tell her how to sleep on land. You set down your brush and tell her you'll help with the lists, wherever she wants her name to go.

She takes the brush from you and writes it herself.

Before you put the papers away, you take out a blank application and write two names at the top.

## The unsigned space
In early spring, Sui finds the application beneath your vessel lists. Two names sit at the top. Yours and hers. You reach for it, but she holds it out of reach.

"You haven't sent this."

"No."

She reads it by the hatch, where the light catches the ink you blotted with your sleeve. In the fleet, nobody marries without a written application. Below the names, you've asked if she'll marry you and go ashore with you. You've left room for her answer. You still aren't sure you had the right to write her name.

She points to a character you've written badly and takes your brush. Her correction is small and merciless.

"That paper can't tell me where to live," she says.

"I know."

She waits while you fold the edge of the paper flat. You tell her it can stay there. You can wait too. She looks at the empty space beneath the names, dips the brush, and signs.

By the time the fleet submits, her signature has dried. You keep the paper folded in your waxed bag, beside the brush case. Once, when the deck rolls under her feet, Sui asks if the ink has smeared.

"It hasn't."

"Good."

She goes to check the water jars. You put the paper away. There's still a crew to feed, and she still hasn't said where she'll sleep when there is no crew.

In April, boats carrying Zheng Yi Sao, women, and children set out for Canton to settle terms. You watch from your junk until their sails are difficult to tell apart. Sui stands beside you, one hand on a line. The paper stays dry in your bag while the boats disappear upriver.

## The paper she keeps
At Furongsha, the junks submit. Each vessel receives pork and wine, and the men receive paper bills. You're offered government service or shore. You choose shore. Sui reads the terms herself and chooses shore too.

She takes a ferry sampan. You carry your brush case to the Canton waterfront. For three days, she sways a little whenever she stands still. On the fourth, she takes work on a sampan and makes you pay the fare.

By 1811, you write letters for customers at a waterfront stall. She runs her boat nearby. Some evenings she comes by after your last customer and waits while you wash the ink from your hands. A sailor brings a letter to your stall with news that Zhang Baozai has joined the Qing navy and Zheng Yi Sao has moved ashore. Sui asks you to read that part again.

One evening, she sets an old cargo list on your table. The two corrected figures are still there. She could have thrown it away a hundred times. She carried it ashore instead.

"You can keep that," you say.

She unfolds the signed application beside it.

"This one too?"

"Only if you want to."

"I do."

Above the stall, she pins the application beside the cargo list herself, holding the paper steady with one hand while the other presses the pin into the wood.
