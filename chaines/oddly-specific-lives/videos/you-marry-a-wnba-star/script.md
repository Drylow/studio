# POV: You Marry a WNBA Star

The first time you touch her, it is with a plastic goniometer and both of you pretend that doesn't count. You're in a rehab room at Hospital for Special Surgery, trying not to think about rent, while rain taps the windows and antiseptic sits in the air like bad coffee. Her left knee is swollen under the ice wrap. On the wall, a muted TV shows her hitting a game winner last June, all teeth and roar and confetti. In front of you, she is staring at a heel slide like it personally insulted her. What nobody in that highlight knew was that nine months can be a short sentence and a whole career. She says, "Measure it again." You do. She doesn't flinch. You decide she is only a patient. This lasts until Tuesday.

## Tuesday lasts longer than it should
Tuesday is when she remembers your name and starts using it like a challenge. You chart range of motion, swelling, quad activation, gait work, all the unromantic nouns that stand between her and a real cut to the basket. The clinic changes when she enters. Interns stand straighter. A kid in a walking boot whispers her nickname like a prayer. An older Knicks fan asks if she can really dunk.

She looks at him. "Depends who is asking."

Rain drags thin lines down the windows. The plastic goniometer clicks in your hand. In her bag, beside the brace straps, is a size 6 ball, 28.5 inches around, worn at the seams from grip work. Her surgeon has told her nine months to a year if everything goes right. Women athletes tear ACLs several times more often than men.

"Nine months grows a person," she says. "I'd prefer a jump shot."

She isn't afraid of pain. She's afraid of being ordinary too long.

## The woman on the muted TV
Her name is Mara Voss, though everyone in the clinic seems to know that before you do. Six foot two. Guard. New York Skylarks. Late-clock threes, a mean left shoulder, and an expression that makes referees reconsider their careers. On the muted TV, she is always healthy. On your table, she counts breaths through straight-leg raises.

The injury happened last June, during a regular season running roughly May through September, with playoffs reaching October if the basketball gods feel generous. She missed the playoff push from a folding chair behind the bench, brace hidden beneath tailored pants, pretending the cameras weren't hunting her face.

You're 5'10", late twenties, still paying off school, driving a used Honda with one working speaker. Her agent texts constantly. Her mother calls at lunch. Her coach sends clips.

One morning she asks, "Will my first step come back?"

"It can," you say. "If you do the boring work."

"Boring is fine. Gone is not."

That is round one, because rehab does not care how famous your crossover used to be.

## The boring work
The boring work starts before sunrise, when Manhattan still looks rinsed and expensive and slightly guilty. Single-leg balance. Scar mobilization. Blood-flow restriction cuffs around her thigh. Stationary bike minutes that feel less like exercise and more like a private insult. Every time she nearly loses patience, she stares at the same gray floor tile until her face closes.

The goniometer becomes an enemy with numbers printed on it. Three more degrees. Five. Seven. She treats each one like a hostage negotiation.

She calls the ice machine "that little freezer demon." She tells a rookie with a sprained ankle, "Limping dramatically doesn't count as leadership."

Then one bad session empties her. She asks everyone else to leave.

"I watched old highlights at 2 a.m.," she says. "I hated her for making it look easy."

"That's allowed."

She studies you for exactly three seconds. "Don't be kind if it's fake."

"It isn't."

So she keeps working. One day she takes out the size 6 ball and practices free-throw rhythm without jumping.

"It feels smaller when I'm scared."

You notice her hands more than you should, then write down her reps like a decent professional.

## Discharge day
Nine and a half months after surgery, she is discharged from your care with clean strength testing, controlled cutting drills, and the careful optimism athletes pretend not to hear. Nothing has happened. You have been boring, chart-accurate, and painfully decent, which is not how anyone hopes to be described, but it keeps the line where the line belongs.

She signs the last form in the clinic lobby while rain streaks down the glass.

"Are you still required to call me Ms. Voss?"

"No."

"Good. Then I'm asking you to dinner."

You freeze long enough that she checks the wall clock.

She chooses Via Carota in the West Village, low lights, close tables, olive oil bright as coins. She is recognized before the bread arrives. A man asks for a photo while you're halfway through praising the ravioli. She gives him the kind face, then returns with another one.

"Fans get kind," she says. "Trolls get nothing. Family gets the real one if they survive it."

"Which one do I get?"

"We'll see."

She could stop there, with one careful dinner and a clean exit. She doesn't.

## Dinner with the public
Dating finds its rhythm in places where New York pretends not to stare. Coffee near Bryant Park before her workouts. Late dumplings in Flushing after film. Walks by the Hudson with her hoodie pulled low, because being unseen is now a luxury item.

Doorways frame her. Photographers angle upward. In every selfie, you look like someone who wandered into the wrong team photo. Then a fan account posts a blurry picture of you leaving dinner. By breakfast, strangers have identified your shoes, your clinic, and your Honda.

They call you too short, too normal, too broke, too lucky.

She takes your phone.

"Never wrestle a comment section," she says. "It likes the floor."

At family dinner, your uncle asks if she could beat LeBron one-on-one. The table goes quiet in the exact way families go quiet before becoming a legal liability.

She smiles.

"Your uncle has confidence in me. I respect that."

You apologize in the kitchen. She says men with microphones have asked worse.

What gets her is your embarrassment for her.

## The comeback game
May arrives at Barclays Center with warm rain on Flatbush Avenue and her face on a screen large enough to make privacy seem fictional. The Skylarks home opener is also her return. She is on a minutes restriction, which she hates with spiritual commitment.

On the charter, she tells you younger players may never know the old routine. Since 2024, WNBA teams have flown charter league-wide. Before that, teammates folded long bodies into commercial economy after back-to-backs, missed connections, dragged ice bags through terminals, and got recognized in food courts after losses.

The game is not a fairy tale. Her first jumper hits front rim. Her first drive is careful. Every plant makes the building hold its breath.

Then she gets a steal, pushes in transition, stops short of contact, and hits from the elbow.

Barclays exhales.

Afterward, she is furious about two missed free throws.

"You played."

"I came back," she says. "Those are different."

Later, at an empty practice court, she asks you to rebound because the ball still feels smaller when she's scared. Your hands meet once on leather.

When she says Istanbul later, you hear warning and ignore it.

## The Istanbul offer
Three months into dating, her agent calls during breakfast at your apartment. You burn eggs. Mara puts the phone on speaker, and Istanbul enters the room like weather.

For almost thirty years, many WNBA players have gone overseas every fall, to Turkey, China, and European leagues, chasing the second paycheck the American season did not provide. Sometimes they spend holidays far from family. Sometimes checks arrive late.

She has done it. She knows Turkish coffee that can wake the dead, hotel rooms where sleep won't come, and FaceTime Thanksgivings where everyone pretends the connection is enough.

Her salary before 2026 was around $100,000, solid to normal people, fragile after agent fees, taxes, training, family help, and a short career.

Miami is the other option. Unrivaled, the 3-on-3 winter league founded by WNBA stars, launched in January 2025 and averaged more than $200,000 for ten weeks.

"Istanbul is more money," she says. "Miami is less lonely."

You say, "I want you home. I don't want that to be a cage."

She looks at you like the sentence has weight.

## The March 2026 term sheet
March 2026 arrives with labor news while you kneel in her kitchen, attacking a cabinet hinge with the confidence of a man who has watched two tutorials.

Her phone lights up. The term sheet is seven years, through 2032, with a 2026 team salary cap around $7 million, maximum salaries around $1.4 million, an expected average near $583,000, and minimums roughly $270,000 to $300,000. In 2025, the maximum had been about $249,000, and the minimum about $66,000.

That's the kind of number that makes accountants cry.

Her next contract projection jumps from around $100,000 to more than a million. Your used Honda becomes funnier and somehow more honorable.

She doesn't celebrate loudly. She sits on the kitchen floor, barefoot.

"I can say no now."

No Istanbul. No late checks. No Christmas alone with a borrowed pan. Maybe no proving her worth twice every year.

She chooses home, training, and Unrivaled only if her knee and heart agree.

Because she can stay, you start carrying a question.

## The proposal before training camp
You plan it with embarrassing seriousness, because Mara's life is a calendar with shoes on.

Training camp. Preseason. Regular season. Playoffs, if the basketball gods are in a generous mood. Rehab blocks. Sleep windows. The woman has meetings for her meetings, which makes romance feel like something you should submit in PDF form.

You ask her mother first at a soul food place in Harlem, while collard greens, cornbread, and fried catfish arrive like witnesses. You sweat through your shirt.

Her mother watches you over sweet tea.

"You know what you're asking for?"

"I understand some of it."

"Good," she says. "Men who understand all of it are usually lying."

You propose at Riverbank State Park at dusk, on the outdoor court, not during a game, not on a Jumbotron. She has enough public moments.

You bring the size 6 ball from rehab. Around it, you wrap athletic tape with the question written in black marker.

She reads it. Then she shoots one free throw.

It hits back rim, bounces high, and drops.

"That was rude of you."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes. Rebound."

## Married life on charter time
The wedding is small, in Brooklyn, on an off day before the season swallows the calendar whole.

There is courthouse paperwork, her mother crying once and denying it immediately, teammates too tall for every group photo, and your uncle behaving because several aunties have explained consequences to him.

Married life begins in fragments.

You work at the clinic. She texts from charter flights, hotels in Phoenix and Seattle, buses where everyone pretends not to be exhausted. Ice bags drip in sinks. Scouting reports cover the bed. FaceTime ends because treatment starts.

By 2026, the league is bigger, with Toronto's Tempo and Portland's Fire joining, which means more cities, more cameras, more little girls in Mara Voss jerseys.

Her salary changes the apartment conversation. You still drive the Honda.

"I can replace it."

"It's fine."

Then the passenger window gets stuck during rain.

She laughs too hard and buys new wipers first, because love has priorities.

Marriage is compression socks over chairs, and Sunday morning guarded like national treasure.

## The second scare
Late August, near the end of the regular season, she plants on a drive and goes down holding the other knee.

The arena loses sound in that old, terrible way. Trauma has manners. It knocks before entering.

You are in the stands, not on the medical team, which is the worst seat in the building. You know enough to be terrified, and not enough to help.

They guide her to the tunnel. Cameras search for your face. You look at the floor.

The MRI is the next morning at HSS. Rain touches the windows, soft and steady, like the first room has found you again.

It is not an ACL tear.

Bone bruise. Mild sprain. Serious. Painful. Not the lost year you both saw waiting.

She cries in the car from East 70th Street to the FDR entrance.

"I hate that I knew the hallway."

"I know."

She misses games, returns carefully, and the Skylarks lose in the first playoff round in October.

At home, she sleeps for thirteen hours.

## The outdoor court at night
Late October puts you back at Riverbank State Park, after the season is over and before winter decides what it wants.

The lights hum above the court. The city makes its low noise below. The air is cool enough to show your breath if you wait.

There is no crowd. No camera. No trainer with a clipboard.

Just you, your wife, and the size 6 ball.

The court is damp at the edges from earlier rain, but playable. Her hands find the seams. Yours catch and send it back.

She is still 6'2" in every doorway. Still private. Still fierce. Still scared sometimes.

But now you are not measuring a swollen knee with a plastic goniometer under fluorescent lights. You are rebounding free throws under open sky.

She bends her knees once.

"Measure it again."

You look at the rim, then at her knee.

"Looks good."

"Liar."

She shoots anyway.

The ball rises through the dark, smaller than the men's ball, familiar as a heartbeat, and this time nobody is counting degrees.
