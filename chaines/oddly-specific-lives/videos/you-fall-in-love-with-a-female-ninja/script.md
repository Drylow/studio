# POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Ninja

You find a medicine seller hiding in your father's storehouse, her sandals dry despite the rain. It's late afternoon in Iga. You came for millet. Her sleeves smell of smoke. "I lost my way," she says. "Did anyone see me come in?" she asks. A man calls from the road, asking about her. His companion lifts the grain shed latch. If he finds her, he'll take your father. She goes pale. You step outside. "Haven't seen her," you say. He asks you to open it. You shout for your father. His companion drops the latch. They wait.

## The man on the road
Across the road, the two men wait for your father to come out. The one in the hat watches the shed. Inside, she holds her sandals against her chest. You hear her breathing.

"Open it," the man says.

"Ask my father."

You have no reason to sound so certain. He takes a step toward the house, and your father calls from inside that supper's ready. He's already annoyed you haven't brought the millet. His voice is getting closer. If he asks who's in the house, you don't have an answer he can live with.

The man looks toward your father's voice. "Tomorrow," he says. He and his companion walk away, slowly enough to make sure you see them.

Inside, she waits until his footsteps fade. Rain runs off the roof in a steady line across the back threshold. She takes a small pouch from her sleeve and pours rice onto the mat beside your sack.

"You don't have to," you say.

"I ate it."

She tips out a little more, counts the grains with her eyes, then puts three back. You have no idea how she can tell. She takes her sandals in one hand and slips through the rear door, leaving the medicine packet on the sill.

At supper, your father asks who you were talking to.

"He wanted the storehouse opened."

That's true enough. You look down at your bowl anyway. Your father looks at you, then at the extra rice you brought in, and lets you eat.

In the morning, the sill is empty. Your father has the packet beside his bowl. "She left this," he says. He waits for you to explain.

Three days later, at the market in Ueno, you find her weighing herbs.

## Market day in Ueno
The charcoal baskets dig into your shoulders as you cross the market in Ueno. By the time you set them down, she's already looking at you.

She's wearing a plain brown kimono and straw sandals. Her sleeves smell faintly of dried leaves. You wonder whether she recognizes you, or only recognizes the man staring at her feet to see if they're wet.

A buyer steps up to her mat before you can speak.

"You've given me one packet."

"I gave you two."

He holds up the packet in his hand. People nearby stop bargaining to watch. She points to his left sleeve.

"The other one is there."

He reaches inside, finds it, and clears his throat. She waits while he checks the contents. When he nods, she puts another handful of herbs on the scale. You pick up your baskets again, though there's nowhere you need to take them.

"Did he come back?" you ask when the buyer leaves.

"Did he come back to your farm?"

You were going to ask about the man on the road. She gets there first, and you don't like how relieved she looks when you shake your head.

"What did he want from you?"

She folds the top of a paper packet with her thumb. "I don't know what he told you."

"He asked if I'd seen you."

"And you said?"

"You heard me."

"I heard enough."

At this market, you've heard stories of shinobi from Iga and Koka. Now she's asking which path your father takes with the charcoal. You haven't decided what to make of this woman, who counts rice she doesn't owe and watches your face more closely than the road behind her.

"I'm Akane," she says.

You tell her your name. She repeats it once, quietly, while she ties the packet shut.

Then she looks at your charcoal baskets.

"Does your father still take loads past the shrine?"

## The charcoal path
"Every market day," you say. "Why?"

Akane glances past you, toward the people moving between the stalls.

"Ask him to take another path."

Two mornings later, you're leading a horse loaded with charcoal up the track above Ueno. The rain has stopped, but water still drops from the cedars onto your baskets. You've spent most of the climb wondering whether to tell your father what she said.

"You didn't ask him."

Akane is beside the path. You nearly lose your grip on the lead rope. The horse gives her a calmer welcome.

"You could say hello."

"I just did." She looks at the baskets. "How many go to the shrine?"

You tell her. She names two villages your father supplies, then a third household that takes a load before winter. She knows the route better than you do. You'd like to know how.

"A man is asking which loads go up to the shrine near the pass," she says. "Your father's name came up."

"He's carried charcoal there for years."

"That's why the man asked."

She says she works for an Iga family. She won't say more.

"Stay off the lower path today. Take the ridge."

You lead the horse uphill until the lower road comes into view. There's nobody on it. At the muddy edge, beside a stand of bamboo, you find fresh sandal marks. Someone stepped aside and waited there.

When you look back, Akane is gone. You're beginning to find that irritating.

She appears again before you reach the ridge. "Shinobi work," she says. "I entered his lodging and read the list of charcoal loads he plans to stop. Was your father angry about the rice?"

"He doesn't know."

For a moment, she stops watching the road. She looks relieved. You want to ask why she cares, but she's already walking away.

Now you have to change a route your father trusts, and explain why without bringing her name home.

## Three weeks of lessons
You tell your father there's a washout on the charcoal road. He asks where. You point toward the ridge, which is close enough to the truth to get you through supper.

Three weeks pass. After market day in Ueno, you meet Akane by a small shrine in the hills, where rainwater drips from the eaves onto a stone basin. She looks at your sandals.

"You're putting your weight down too early."

You try the patch of wet earth she points to. A twig snaps under your heel.

"Again."

The second attempt costs four twigs. She counts them aloud, which seems unnecessary.

So you take her to the paddy after the spring planting begins. You show her how far apart the rice seedlings go. She plants one row with the gaps growing wider toward the end.

"Let me show you."

"I can see it."

She finishes the row. From the bank, even she can see it. She holds out her hand for another bundle of seedlings.

"Show me again."

At supper she eats her portion, then most of yours. You ask if planting makes her hungry. She says, "Apparently."

The following market day, you watch her sell medicine near the Ueno road. Farmers ask about aches; a traveler buys a packet and keeps walking. Nobody gives her a second look. She counts the traveler's coins without losing sight of the road behind him.

Back at the shrine, you ask why she wants you to walk quietly. She sets your sandal beside a stone, clear of the fallen leaves.

"So you'll hear when someone else doesn't."

You listen. Below the hill, a cart passes on the road. Closer by, someone clears his throat. Akane recognizes the sound. Her hand closes around the empty medicine pouch.

## Her family's table
Akane brings you to her family's farmhouse near the Iga hills before the next market day. The man at the shrine was sent to tell her the clan head wants to meet you. She tells you this while you walk, giving you just enough time to wish you'd worn cleaner sandals.

Inside, a kettle hangs over the hearth. The clan head sits across from you while Akane pours tea. He asks your father's name, which villages take his charcoal, and whether you still use the road by the pass. You answer each question. Akane's cup stays untouched.

Then he asks what you told the man outside your storehouse.

"That I hadn't seen anyone."

"And had you?"

You try to explain that you'd only seen her inside, so the answer might have sounded different from the road. Akane lowers her eyes to her tea. You stop talking.

The clan head turns to her. "You went back to him."

"I chose to."

"You knew he couldn't lie."

"Yes."

She says nothing on your behalf. You find you're grateful for that, though you'd prefer to be grateful under easier circumstances.

Her family allows the meetings. You must stay clear of their work and carry no messages, even one you can't read. Akane says she understands. You say it too. The clan head looks at her when he adds that someone will know whenever she visits you. Her fingers rest against the warm cup until he finishes speaking.

Outside, the air smells of damp straw. Akane waits until the farmhouse door closes.

"I thought he'd decide I couldn't see you."

You ask if you should stop coming.

"I didn't ask you to."

She starts down the path beside you. You want to take her hand, but there are people still within sight of the house. At the next bend, she asks what your father needs from the market. You tell her, and she listens all the way to the road.

## The first touch
The next time you meet, you carry your own bundle to Ueno market. Akane carries herbs. You buy a spool of thread for your father and take the longer path home by the shrine, with nothing in either bundle that belongs to her family's work.

Summer comes. She meets you at the edge of the paddies when she can. Once, near the Ueno stalls, she tells you the man ahead is a charcoal buyer before he turns around.

"You know him?"

"I know how he walks."

You know when rain is coming by the weight of the air over the field. She asks you to teach her that. You stand together beside the seedlings, waiting for a breeze that never quite arrives, until the first drops settle the matter for you.

She keeps saying she's eaten before she sees you. She keeps finishing your rice. You begin packing enough for two and tell her your father served too much.

"He knows your appetite?"

"He worries about it."

She takes the larger portion. By autumn, she no longer waits for you to offer it.

At dusk, you follow a narrow bank between paddies toward the shrine. Mud shifts under your sandal. Akane catches your wrist before your foot reaches the water.

You find your balance. Her hand stays where it is.

For months you've kept the meetings within her family's conditions. Standing there, you wonder how long you can keep meeting and going home separately.

"What if they refuse us?" you ask.

Akane looks toward the farmhouse roofs beyond the fields. You can hear someone calling a child in for supper.

"I don't know yet."

She still has your wrist. You turn your hand and take hers. She watches your fingers settle around hers, then walks with you as far as the road.

A few mornings later, a traveler comes to your father's storehouse asking when she last visited.

## The other traveler
The traveler lays a packet of needles on your father's counter. He asks the price of charcoal, looks at the storehouse door, and asks when Akane last visited.

He gives your father plenty of time to answer. You remember the man on the road in the rain. This one has the same patient voice.

Your father looks at you. He knows she comes by, though he has learned to leave the questions alone.

"I don't discuss visitors with strangers," you say.

The traveler studies you. Your father picks up the needles and asks if they're for sale. They are. He buys one packet, and the man leaves with a polite bow.

You find Akane near the hill shrine before dusk. She's watching the lower path. When you tell her about the needles, she asks what the man carried them in. You describe the cloth packet and the knot at its mouth.

"He followed me from Ueno," she says. "He's looking for people who know my family."

You ask if he knows about you.

"He knows where your father lives."

She says she'll be gone while she settles his attention. You ask how long. She can't tell you, but she promises to send word if the farm becomes unsafe.

For two weeks, you hear every step outside the storehouse. Your father asks you to stop checking the road while you work. You try. By the third morning, he stops asking.

Akane returns at dusk while you're fixing the door. You hear her sandals on the stones and turn. Her eyes go to the new hinge, then to your face.

"Do you want me to stop coming?"

You put down the hammer.

"I want you to choose."

She looks at the road behind her. She stays until the light is gone, helping you hang the door straight. When she leaves, she still hasn't answered.

## The thin harvest
That autumn, the harvest is thin. Your father counts the rice before supper and again before he puts it away. You burn more charcoal, mend a hoe with a loose handle, and carry water when the well rope frays. Akane is gone, but every tool still breaks on time.

Near the end of winter, she comes up the farm path in a seller's plain kimono, with a basket under her arm. Your father sees her through the open door and sets down the rice scoop.

"The man with the needles?" you ask.

"He isn't following this route anymore."

She won't say what she did while she was away. She opens the basket instead. There's millet inside, enough to help your father stretch what remains of the rice.

He brings out coins. She tells him to keep them. He counts them into her hand anyway, and she accepts what he can pay.

In spring, she helps you transplant rice seedlings in a neighboring plot. She presses the first ones too deep. On the next row she leaves gaps wide enough for your father to spot from the bank.

"Closer," you tell her.

"I remember."

She moves the next seedling closer. At midday, she eats beside you with mud drying on her ankles. You go back to work before she finishes. When you glance up, your father is watching her watch you. He says nothing, though he gives her another row that afternoon.

On the walk home, you ask whether her family would allow her to marry someone outside it.

"They'd say no. They can't forbid it."

"Would you?"

She takes several steps along the paddy bank before answering.

"Yes."

You want to ask when. Her family's conditions still stand, and your father has only just begun to see what she wants. For now, you walk home with her answer, while she keeps her hand in yours until the road divides.

## Smoke beyond the ridge
In September 1581, the men bringing salt to Ueno name Nobunaga's son Nobukatsu among the commanders. Your father listens while he weighs charcoal. At home, he asks you to move the rice stores farther from the door.

The salt sellers say Nobunaga's men have entered Iga through several mountain passes. None knows which road is safe.

You see smoke beyond the western ridge before anyone at the market can tell you which village it comes from. On the forest track, families pass with bundles tied to carrying poles. A woman asks for water. You fill her gourd, and she points back the way she came.

"There are men on the road."

Later, you see samurai in lacquered armor moving between the trees below the pass. They're far enough away that you can't hear their voices. Your father shuts the storehouse and puts another rope around the rice sacks.

"We stay until we know more," he says.

By afternoon, two more families have passed the farm. Akane comes through the front gate, where your father can see her. She has a travel bundle, damp sandals, and mud on the hem of her kimono.

"I saw armed men on the road this morning," she tells him. "I crossed the upper pass yesterday. I don't know who's there now."

He looks at the sacks. There's more rice than the three of you can carry, and he has spent the whole year guarding it.

"Why should I believe you?"

"Because I've walked it."

Your father waits for her to say whether anyone followed her. She doesn't. You think of the first day in the storehouse, when her sandals were dry despite the rain. You didn't know where she'd been then, either. Now the smoke is close enough to smell when the wind shifts.

You take one sack from the stack and set it by the door. Your father watches you, then unties the rope from another.

Akane looks at the light above the ridge.

"If we're leaving, we have to leave before it reaches the trees."

## The mountain crossing
You leave before the smoke reaches the trees. Your father carries a sack of rice across his back. You take charcoal wrapped for trade, two blankets, and the tools that fit in your hands. He looks once at the storehouse door before he follows you.

The road over the pass is broad enough for a cart. Akane takes you into the trees instead.

"Stay where you can see my hand."

You remember the shrine path, the wet earth, every twig you broke while she watched without offering sympathy. This ground is steeper. Your foot slides against a root, and your father catches the back of your coat. Akane raises two fingers. You stop just before a scatter of loose stones.

Below you, voices move along the road. You wait until they pass.

Near a small shrine, you find a family standing beside the path. Their oldest boy has a bundle tied to his chest. The mother asks which way leads through to Koka. Akane looks at the child, then points them toward the lower track.

"Keep left at the split. There's water before the climb."

Your father glances at her. That's the track she means to use.

She waits until the family is moving. Then she leads you uphill, where the trees stand closer and the ground takes longer to cross. Nobody has to stop behind your slow steps now.

By dusk, your shoulders ache under the charcoal. You want to tell her you'll go wherever she goes. You can barely manage the slope beneath you, but you want to say it anyway.

Akane lifts her hand again. Somewhere ahead, a stone shifts. She listens until the sound settles, then shows you where to put your foot.

## Two days to Koka
The cave is shallow, just deep enough to hide you from the path. Akane gets everyone inside, tells your father where the trail bends, and reaches for her sleeve. She misses it the first time.

The cloth is torn near her shoulder. When she tries to lift her arm, her face tightens. She lowers it before your father can see.

"You can't keep this pace," you say.

"I know."

Your father offers to wait. Akane tells him there are people behind you who need to keep moving. She gives him directions to a stream and a crossing beyond it, making him repeat the turn before she lets him leave. Then she looks at you.

"You can go with him."

"I'll stay."

She studies the mouth of the cave for a moment. "All right."

When she can't walk, you carry her. She grips your collar with her good hand whenever the path drops, and your shoulders start to shake. At the stream, she taps your shoulder and sends you back three steps to the crossing you'd missed.

"Your load's uneven," she says later.

"It's the rice."

"You packed it badly."

"You ate most of my rice."

She rests her forehead against your shoulder. You hear one breath that might be a laugh.

The crossing into Koka takes two days. At a farmhouse, your father borrows clean cloth, and Akane finally lets you tend her arm. She watches your hands as if she expects you to ask what happened. You don't.

That night, she sits beside the cooking fire while the others sleep.

"If I don't go back to the work," she says, "I don't know what I do."

"You don't have to know tonight."

She looks at her bandaged arm. In the morning, she tries to help your father carry reeds for the roof.

## A roof in Koka
Akane manages six reeds before her arm begins to hurt. Your father takes the bundle without a word and sends her to sort the shorter pieces under the eaves. She sits there with a look you remember from the day she got a whole row of rice seedlings wrong.

You and your father help repair the farmhouse roof for the people sheltering you. After that, someone lends you a patch of ground. Akane comes out when she can, though the seedlings lean whichever way she leaves them. She pulls one up to try again.

"That one was right," you say.

"I was checking."

Rain finds the repaired roof that evening and stays outside. Akane lies awake beside the wall, listening to it. By morning, she's at the door before anyone else, asking what needs doing.

A child arrives with a notice copied onto a scrap of paper. Her mother wants to know what it says. Akane reads the lines aloud, then notices the child watching the marks rather than her face. She draws the first character in the dirt with a twig and waits while the child tries it.

The next week, two more children bring scraps. Akane clears a space under the eaves. She makes them read the same line again until they can find it without her finger. When they return the next morning, she's already set out the paper.

An older neighbor asks if she means to return to her family's work.

"I've sent word that I'm alive," she says. "That's all."

You ask later if she misses it. You know she chose that work long before she met you.

"Some of it."

She folds a child's paper carefully and puts it out of reach of the rain.

Days later, someone comes up the path carrying a letter from Iga.

## The reply from Iga
The messenger waits beneath the eaves while Akane reads. You watch her thumb hold the paper flat against the breeze. She reads it twice before she looks up.

"They know I'm safe."

Your father invites the messenger inside for rice. He accepts, which tells you the road from Iga has been long. You stay by the door while Akane folds the letter along its old creases.

"The head of my family says he won't order me back," she tells you. "He wants to hear my decision from me."

You look at the folded paper in her hand. For months, you've wondered if it would ask her to leave. Now it asks her to choose, and you have no idea what she'll say.

The messenger leaves before dark. Akane keeps the letter beside the children's papers. Each winter she reads it and puts it back. You and your father save three harvests' wages. By 1585, he buys a small paddy near Koka. She hasn't sent word to Iga. He complains about your crooked rows, though he planted half of them. Akane teaches children under the eaves nearby. At supper, she takes the largest portion and asks if anyone minds after she's finished it.

One evening, you find her reading the letter again.

"If you want to visit Iga, I'll come with you," you say.

She folds it and sets it down.

"I'll go when I'm ready."

You nod. There's a loose roof tie to fix tomorrow, and seedlings to check. You can give her time.

She watches you turn toward the tools.

"If I go," she says, "will you come back to Koka with me afterward?"

"Yes."

She looks toward the field. She has decided where she'll live, and she plans to keep teaching. You wait while she finds the words for what she wants from you. She folds the letter once more and places it in your hand as the last light leaves the rice.

## Dawn in the field
You go out before your father wakes. The seedlings stand in shallow water, their thin leaves just visible in the gray light. One row has come loose where rain washed soil away from the roots. You step into the paddy to press them back down.

Behind you, the path is quiet.

You turn before Akane reaches the bank. She stops with one foot raised, looking at you as though you have interrupted a lesson she didn't know she was giving.

"How did you hear me?"

"I was listening for you."

She lowers her foot. The water from the grasses has darkened her sandals. You remember a pair that stays dry through late-afternoon rain, and how badly you lie to the man looking for her. She looks at you now without anyone waiting on the road.

Akane comes to the edge of the paddy. For a moment, she studies the row you've repaired.

"That one will hold," she says.

"You taught me."

"I taught you where to put your feet."

She's right. You still put them in the wrong place sometimes. You step onto the bank and leave the seedlings to the morning.

"I want to stay in Koka," she says. "I want to teach. I'll visit my family when I choose."

You nod. She has told you each part before, but never with you standing this close.

"And I want to come home to you."

You look at her.

"With me?"

"With you."

You take a breath. Your father will be up soon, looking for help in the field. There will be work waiting when the sun reaches the ridge. Akane waits for your answer.

"I want that too," you say. "We'll build it together."

She steps closer and takes your hand beside the wet rice seedlings. Her sandals are damp from the path. You heard her coming anyway.
