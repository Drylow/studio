# POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Knight

A stone misses your head as she rides in, helmet bent over one eye. "The gate won't hold another strike," she says. The watchman brings the gate's split hinge. Rain blows through the forge at Fougeres castle. The man arguing about a horseshoe steps aside when she takes off the helmet. "Fix it before dusk," she says. Your thumb finds the dent beside a scrape on her skin. She looks at it, leaves you her helmet, and goes back outside. You put down the horseshoe that pays for your charcoal.

Her horse crosses the courtyard.

## The broken hinge
The watchman tells you six riders struck the outer gate, found it held, and pulled back toward the road. They'll have seen her ride out after them. She'll have seen which way they went.

You set her helmet beside the horseshoe and put the hinge in the fire.

It's 1355. You're twenty-seven, a village blacksmith hired to work at the forge by Fougeres castle. You know what a gate costs to repair, and you know how much charcoal you can buy before the supplier stops trusting you. The hinge has split where its pin takes the weight. If the other one is damaged too, you don't have enough iron for both.

Outside, voices gather in the rain. She's checking the people caught beyond the gate when the riders came. A mother carries a child past your open door. The child watches you strike the hot iron until his mother turns his head away.

By the time she returns, the hinge is cooling. Rain drips from her helmet onto the forge floor while you work the dent back into shape. You hand it over. She puts it on, turns her head, and lifts the visor herself.

"What will the hinge bear?"

"A shut gate. It needs another pin before anyone strikes it again."

She looks toward the watchman. "See that it's done."

Only then does he say, "Yes, Lady Isabeau de Kermor."

She is twenty-six. Her father is dead, and the small castle she holds beyond Fougeres has its own gate, village, and people waiting for her. She leaves you money for the hinge and orders a second pin for her gate as well.

"Come and look at it," she says.

The horseshoe is still on your anvil when you agree.

## The duke's visit
Her castle stands above a thatched village, with two stone towers and a wooden drawbridge that creaks under your cart. The old gate pin comes free in your hand. You ask for six nails to secure a plate beside it.

The steward counts them out.

"Six," he says. "I'll expect six in the gate."

He speaks to you while she stands beside him. When Isabeau tells him to bring the remaining iron from the stores, he pauses long enough for you to notice.

You stay three days. At dawn, her father's armsmaster puts her through a sword-and-shield drill in the yard. He calls the strikes; she meets them and makes him move his feet. Later she rides the road above the village and comes back with mud up one side of her horse. She's trained with him for years. The mail she wears sits under a breastplate, with plate protecting her arms. She takes it off piece by piece before supper.

A visiting duke arrives on the third day. He watches her direct a gate drill, then asks about the riders at Fougeres. She tells him where they tested the gate and how far she followed them. The watchman confirms it.

The duke asks for his sword, and even the armsmaster looks surprised. He calls Isabeau forward while the watchman and steward stand beside him. She kneels in the yard where she's trained for years, and the duke touches her shoulder with the blade and names her a knight. When she stands, the steward checks the duke's face before bowing.

You stand by the gate with your tools. She puts on the helmet you repaired, and it settles slightly crooked. She catches you looking and touches the rim.

The duke leaves before dusk. You carry your tools toward the stable, where the steward is waiting with his account book.

## Three weeks at her castle
The steward wants a price for the helmet, the hinge, and every nail. You give him one. The next morning he sends a page to your forge with a breastplate fitting wrapped in cloth.

Isabeau comes for it herself that afternoon. The strap has pulled through its fastening, and she holds the plate steady while you fit a new one.

"Will it hold?" she asks.

"You can pull on it."

She does. You have to brace your feet.

A week later, a buckle breaks as she saddles her horse. You bring a replacement to the castle, where the steward meets you at the stable door with ink on his fingers. He writes down the cost before you can take your hammer out. Isabeau pays you when the work is done, though she tells you she can't buy every piece of new iron you recommend.

On your third visit, you lie beneath the drawbridge with a lantern while she holds the chain clear of your hands. A messenger arrives from the road and waits until she climbs down to speak. Armed men have made the crossing west of the village unsafe for carts.

You climb out and see a merchant turn his cart around on the western road. Behind him, the road is empty.

That evening, a villager asks for nails to fix his roof before the next rain. Isabeau takes the new fastening for her own armor off the steward's list.

"Give him what he needs."

The steward draws a line through her name. She watches him do it, then asks you to look once more at the old gate hinge. It sits above the place those first riders could have struck. Your new pin holds when you test it. She asks how often it should be checked.

By morning, she has found another reason for you to stay.

## The riding lesson
By morning, Isabeau has decided the old hinge can wait an hour. She takes you beyond the village, where the road dries between hedges and a patient brown horse stands ready. You grip the reins like they're the gate beam, and the horse seems to take that personally.

"He's walking," she says.

"I can tell."

"You keep asking him to stop."

You look down at your hands. The reins have gone tight again. She rides beside you without laughing, which somehow makes the whole thing worse.

At the bend above the fields, she asks why you keep coming to her castle. There's a forge near Fougeres that pays sooner, she says. You watch your horse consider a patch of grass.

"The hinge still worries me."

"It worries me too."

She dismounts and checks the strap beneath your horse's belly. She tightens it herself, then leads you a few steps away from the road. From here, you can see the village roofs and the gate above them.

"I know where the guards should stand," she says. "I know when the bridge needs another man. I'm frightened I'll miss something small, and someone down there will pay for it."

You think of the villager with the leaking roof. She looks toward his house as if she can pick it out from here.

"I can check the hinge again," you say.

"I know."

She reaches up and moves your fingers along the reins, loosening your grip until the horse lowers its head. Her hand stays over yours while you both feel him settle. Then she steps back and tells you to take him around the bend.

You take him around at a slow walk. When you return, she's still waiting at the bend, her horse's reins in one hand.

On your next visit, that same hand is busy losing a fight with a needle.

## A torn cloak
Rain runs off the chapel roof when you find her at a table by the hall window. Her cloak lies across her knees. She's pulled the edges of a tear together with such determination that the cloth now gathers into a thick ridge.

"How long have you been at that?"

"Long enough."

"You have more patience with a sword."

She sets down the needle. "Come and show me yours."

The sword drill takes place under the covered passage, where the stones stay dry. She gives you a wooden practice sword, tells you which foot to move, and takes it from you before your other foot catches up. You retrieve it. The second attempt lasts nearly as long.

Back at the table, she breaks a honey cake in half. There's a little flour on her sleeve. She eats hers slowly, though you can tell she wants the one still on the plate. When a servant mentions a child in the village whose family has been short of food, Isabeau wraps that last cake in a cloth.

She picks up the cloak again. You offer to hold the torn edges, and she lets you. Outside, the rain thickens against the shutters.

She tells you a story she's heard about Tortosa, where women are said to defend the city during an attack. Afterward, she says, they're granted the right to wear a hatchet on their clothes. She studies her crooked seam. "Do you know their names?" You don't.

"What do you want them to remember here?" you ask.

"Yvon," she says.

You know him. He brought his daughter inside when the road became unsafe, then went back out to fetch his neighbor's goats.

"He came back for more than his own," she says. She folds the cloak so the crooked seam is hidden underneath. The next time you see her, she brings that same care to work no one is likely to remember.

## At the anvil
In the years that follow, Isabeau learns which tasks can wait and which ones bring her straight to your forge. One spring, it's a drawbridge pin worn thin at the shoulder. In autumn, it's a latch from Yvon's shed. She stays while you work, and one cold afternoon she takes the bellows handle from you.

"Tell me when."

You show her how the iron changes color in the coals. She watches until she can call it herself, then asks to make a small hinge pin. Her first one bends out of shape as soon as you test it. She puts it beside the anvil and asks for another piece.

Around 1360, a winter order arrives from her steward with more iron fittings on it than the castle can pay for. You spread the old pieces across the bench. Two straps can be straightened. The bridge brackets need fresh iron. She checks your marks against his list and carries it back herself.

At the next gate drill, she falls while turning with her shield. You start toward her, but she gets up before you reach her. She plants one foot and rises. Her mail settles against her, and the plates turn with her shoulders as she takes up the shield again.

Later, she brings in the helmet from the day you met. Rain falls from its rim onto the forge floor.

"Can you take the dent out completely?"

You turn it toward the light. "I can make the metal sound. You'll still find the mark if you look."

She runs a thumb over it and leaves the helmet with you.

You listen for her horse during the quiet hours at the forge. One afternoon, she arrives with the steward, who notices the space you've cleared for her at the bench.

## The steward's objection
By 1363, the steward has stopped pretending your visits are about iron. He waits until Isabeau comes to the forge, where you've cleared a place for her beside the bench.

"My lady, may I speak plainly?"

"You usually do."

He looks at the helmet in your hands. Its old dent is smaller now, though you can still find it with your thumb.

"You cannot marry your blacksmith without consequences. The households that lend us horses and men expect alliances. They'll wonder whose counsel you take, and some may withdraw their support."

Your charcoal bill is still folded in your pocket. You've never felt quite so expensive.

Isabeau sets down the buckle she came to collect. "You've given me your counsel. Leave us."

He bows to her. He doesn't look at you.

Outside, rain catches in the yard's loose stones. She waits until his steps reach the tower before she speaks.

"He isn't wrong about the support."

"I know."

"And I can't tell you what those families will do." She takes the helmet from you, turns it in her hands, and sets it back down. "If I bring people inside these walls, I may have to send you to fix the gate while they wait for you here. Do you understand what staying beside me asks of you?"

You think of the hinge at Fougeres, still warm when she rode back into the rain. "I do."

She comes around the bench and takes your hand. There's a dark line of forge soot across her palm.

"Then stay," she says.

Loving her means keeping the gate strong and trusting her to decide who comes through it.

The next morning, the iron you've ordered fails to arrive. The carter sends word that men have stopped him on the road beyond the village. Isabeau asks how much iron you have left, and you count it together.

## The raiders return
In 1364, the men on the road reach the farms below Isabeau's castle. They're a roaming free company, with no lord there to answer for them. They want food, coin, and a place to sleep. One farmer comes up the hill carrying their demands on a scrap of cloth.

Isabeau reads it beside the well. "How many families are still down there?"

The steward has a list before anyone answers. She sends the watchmen to fetch every household on it, along with two whose names he hasn't entered yet. By noon, children crowd the passage behind the gate. A woman carries her hens under one arm. The steward counts the children, then asks the woman to hold still while he counts her hens.

The final grain cart reaches the slope with a wheel dragging sideways. Its iron tire has shifted. You take the spare strap from a gate repair and work it into place while the driver holds the horse. The drawbridge stays down longer than Isabeau likes.

"Can it carry the load?" she asks.

"It can carry this one."

She walks beside the cart until its rear wheel crosses. Only then does she go out to meet the armed men. You watch from the wall. She keeps her voice low enough that you can't hear it, but you see her hand come to rest on the bridge rail while she waits for an answer.

They leave without food or payment. Inside, the steward opens his inventories and shows her which bins will empty first with so many people under the roof.

"I counted for the households we knew were coming," he says.

"Count the others now."

You spend your remaining iron on a cart wheel. That night, you eat a smaller piece of bread and sleep beside the forge in case the bridge needs work again. Isabeau finds you there at dawn.

A watchman calls down from the wall. The raiders have gone, but he has found their boot prints in the mud below the drawbridge, where someone stood long enough to study its fittings.

## The first siege
The watchman shows you the prints before the rain can wash them away. They lead close to the bridge's outer support, then back toward the road. You crouch beside the timber and find fresh scoring around a fitting.

"They're coming back," Isabeau says.

By morning, enough men fill the road to stop anyone getting through. Two carry long poles toward the drawbridge. Isabeau calls to the longbowmen on the wall, and their arrows strike the ground ahead of the men. The poles go down. The men pull back beyond bowshot and stay there.

After dark, you take a torch under the bridge. Isabeau holds the light while you feel along the fitting. One pin has worked loose, and the wood around it has split.

"How long?" she asks.

"Long enough to mend it tonight. Keep them off it tomorrow."

She takes the torch higher so you can see. Later, you forge arrowheads from iron set aside for roof fittings. The steward stands at the door with his tablets, counting charcoal, arrowheads, and the grain left in the bins. He asks you to make each piece last. You do what you can.

Near dawn, Isabeau brings you water. She sees the old horseshoe beneath a stack of iron and picks it up.

"Why have you kept this?"

"It was on my anvil when you came in with your helmet."

She turns it over. One end still bears the mark where you meant to work it. She places it back where she found it, away from the iron waiting for the fire.

A messenger slips out by a narrow path before sunrise. He knows where the duke's men were last seen, but the roads have changed since then. Isabeau gives him bread and watches until he's past the trees.

Back in the hall, the steward sets out the bowls for breakfast and begins counting everyone who needs one.

## The stores run low
By the third week, the bread comes to the hall in measured pieces. Isabeau stands beside the steward while he reads the names. You carry the basket because your hands are free, and because everyone knows you'll take the smallest piece if you choose your own.

A woman from the village holds out her hand, then looks at the bread.

"There are four of us."

The steward checks his tally. "Four portions went to your room yesterday."

"Three did."

Isabeau asks to see the basket from yesterday's round. The boy who carried it says he left one piece by the other family's door. "We thought it was ours," someone says from the back. Isabeau gives the woman today's missing portion from her own plate and asks the steward to mark the correction.

He keeps the tally close to his chest.

At the forge, the big cooking pot has split along a rivet. You use the last good light to mend it. The watchman brings iron for arrowheads, but the charcoal beside your feet will heat one batch tonight. You make fewer arrowheads. The pot goes back to the kitchen before supper.

A little girl waits there with her grandmother. Isabeau has saved a honey cake for the child, wrapped in a clean cloth. The grandmother accepts it slowly.

"Our house was empty once," she says. "When the sickness came, nobody would open the door."

Isabeau sits beside her until the child finishes eating. Then she finds the steward at his desk.

"Tomorrow, we read the portions aloud. Everyone can see what we have."

He looks at the crossed-out figure on his page. "They'll say I took it."

"You know where it went."

"That won't matter."

She leaves the ledger open between them. He shuts it after she's gone, with his finger still holding the page.

## The steward's mistake
In the winter of 1365, snow settles in the ruts outside the gate. The raiders are still there, close enough to see the smoke from their fires when the wind clears. Inside, the steward counts grain twice each morning. He has begun to read the figures aloud, but he never raises his eyes from the page.

One afternoon, Isabeau goes with him to check the stores. The copied tally that hangs by the door is gone. He says he must have moved it. She asks him to look for it while she checks the sacks.

You find the damage at dusk. Someone has lifted the iron strap beside the gate hinge and driven it back with too small a nail. Under a hard pull, the strap will give. You call for Isabeau. The steward comes with her, sees your hand on the nail, and stops.

"Who knows?" she asks.

"The men outside," he says.

He has sent them the copied tally. In the same message, he has offered a way through the gate to reach the stores, if they leave the villagers alone. He says he thought they might come before help did. Isabeau holds the nail in her palm while he speaks.

"You gave them our food."

"I wanted the families spared."

"They're inside this gate."

She takes him to the watchmen and makes him tell them which strap will fail and when the raiders expect it to fail. You replace the nail while he helps carry timber against the gate. He knows which sacks can be moved first. Isabeau puts him to work moving them.

Near midnight, the postern is opened for a runner. The steward could follow him out. Instead, he brings another beam across the yard and holds it steady while you set its iron brace.

At dawn, the watchman sees men gathering beyond the road.

## The bridge
By midmorning, they come through the rain. The watchmen have barely brought the last sheltered families across the outer yard to the drawbridge when the damaged gate takes a second blow. Its iron strap tears free. The leaf swings inward, dragging the hinge against the stone.

Isabeau sends the families across the wooden drawbridge toward the inner court. You kneel by the hinge with your tools while a watchman holds his shield above you. Rain runs off its edge and into the holes where the old pins sat.

The steward knows the bridge mechanism better than anyone who laughed at his ledgers. He sends two men for a beam of the right length, then stays to guide it past the wheel before it catches. His shaking hands hold the timber clear of the wheel.

Outside, Isabeau holds the narrow approach with the other watchmen. There is room for only one raider at a time. One steps forward with a long blade and waits for her to meet him. She doesn't move until he comes onto the wet boards. Her shield turns his first strike toward the rail. She gives ground on the next, enough to make him follow while a woman and her two boys reach the far side.

You drive the new pin through the hinge. It holds when the steward leans his weight against the gate leaf.

"Ready," you call.

Isabeau glances back to count the people crossing. She tells the watchmen to raise the bridge as soon as the last villager is clear. Then she pushes the raider's blade aside and comes across herself. You take her hand over the final gap as the bridge begins to rise.

By evening, the gate is shut. The stores will last a little longer. The hinge needs more iron than you have.

In the quiet hall, the steward counts what food remains.

## Terms at the gate
The steward is still counting when a horn sounds outside. You reach for the iron poker beside the hearth. Isabeau goes to the window.

A rider waits beyond the drawbridge with the duke's seal and four mounted men. By noon, the envoy sits across from her in the hall. Rain drips from his cloak onto the floor. He offers an escort for the next grain carts and men to patrol the road. In return, the castle must feed those men while they stay.

The steward reads out the stores. He names every damaged fitting, including the hinge you have just pinned together. His voice catches over the grain. Isabeau lets him finish.

"We can feed them for six days," she tells the envoy. "After that, they bring their own."

He agrees. His men ride the road that afternoon. The raiders see them from the far fields and withdraw. Two days later, a grain cart brings enough iron for a new hinge without a watchman beside it. You hear its wheels before you believe it.

At the gate, the steward hands Isabeau a fresh tally. She checks it, then asks him to stay and keep the next one. He nods and goes to count the nails.

You need most of them. You fit the hinge while Isabeau measures the usable timber. Down in the village, you fit new iron hooks to a roof beam. She holds the ladder steady and asks the widow beneath it where the rain comes in.

A week later, the steward finds you both at the rebuilt gate.

"The households with land will ask questions," he says. "About his place here."

"I expect they will," Isabeau says.

He leaves you to the sound of the bridge chains. She rests her hand on the sound hinge and looks at you.

"What do you want, when you can choose?"

## The dubbing
In the spring, a rider comes up the road with the duke's colours on his saddle. He brings a letter, sealed in red wax, and he won't hand it to anyone but Isabeau.

She reads it at the forge, because that's where she was when he arrived. You watch her face. It doesn't move. Then she folds the letter and puts it inside her coat.

"He's coming here," she says. "The duke. In nine days."

"Why?"

"To make me a knight."

Knights were made by dubbing: a lord tapped the sword on their shoulder, said a few words before witnesses, and from that moment they were knights for life. Squires trained for years to earn it. Isabeau has held a castle through a siege and a bridge with her own sword. Nobody can say she hasn't earned it. Plenty of people will say she shouldn't have it.

For nine days, the castle scrubs itself. The steward counts the plates three times. You work at the anvil until midnight, polishing her father's old armour, hammering out a dent in the left pauldron that has been there since before you met her.

On the morning itself, it rains. Of course it rains. The duke arrives with twelve riders and mud to his knees. He's older than you expected, grey and short, and he looks at Isabeau the way you'd look at a horse someone told you could fly.

She kneels in the chapel in her father's armour. The village crowds the door. The widow's boys stand on a bench to see.

The duke draws his sword. He hesitates. Just for a moment. Then he lays the flat of the blade on her shoulder.

"Rise," he says. "Sir Isabeau."

She stands up slowly, the way you taught her to stand in armour, weight on the back foot. She doesn't smile. She turns and looks for you in the crowd, and finds you, and only then does something move in her face.

That night, the steward gets very drunk and tells everyone he always believed in her. Nobody corrects him.

## The new helmet
You stand with her at the gate while the last cart of timber rolls into the yard. For years, you have known what to do with a broken piece of iron. She has asked for something you cannot test against an anvil.

"I want to stay beside you," you say. "Where people can see."

Isabeau looks out along the road. There are still ruts from the grain carts, and a watchman at the far turn. She calls him in before the light goes. Then she asks him to bring the villagers and landholding households to the gate at first light.

The next morning, the villagers and landholding households gather at the gate. You wear the coat with the frayed cuff you keep meaning to mend. Isabeau wears her riding coat, its silver clasp dull from the rain. Water drips from the gatehouse roof onto the packed earth. The steward stands near the door with his clean tally folded in his hands. The widow from the village comes with her two boys, who stare at your boots until their mother tells them to look up.

Before the villagers, Isabeau takes your hand. "I choose him. He stays here beside me." "I choose her," you say. Her fingers tighten. One household withdraws its horses and men. Another pledges both for the watch. Isabeau tells the steward to change the roster.

Afterward, you ask her to wait. You bring out the helmet you have been shaping while the new gate takes shape. Its rim sits level. The lining is soft enough where the old one rubbed her skin.

She turns it in her hands. Inside, just below the brow, you have cut a small horseshoe into the metal. It has the uneven ends of the one you left on your anvil when she rode in out of the rain.

"You kept that shoe," she says.

"I couldn't use it for anything else."

"I think you have."

She fits the helmet, lifts it off again, and takes your hand. Her thumb rests against the rain-cooled metal, close to the little horseshoe engraved inside.
