# POV: You Marry a Ukrainian Woman

The first time Oksana refuses to touch you, your hand hangs in a Chicago Avenue bakery doorway. Behind her, poppy-seed rolls and rye bread fog the glass case. It's February, old snow grays the sidewalk, and you're worried about being late to your second shift in Logan Square. She looks at your hand, then the threshold, like you've offered tragedy. Then her mother goes quiet behind the counter. Oksana says, "Come inside first. We don't shake across doors." You step in because embarrassment is cheaper than arguing. Later you learn this is only the first door.

## The door that will not close
You finish the door because pride is the only tool you own in unlimited supply. A plane, a shim, two screws, and the kind of focus that makes you forget Oksana is watching from beside the register. The bakery is her aunt's place, small and warm, with icons near the cash box, handwritten signs in Ukrainian and English, and a bell over the door that gives up halfway through ringing.

Later, you learn the neighborhood has been Ukrainian for longer than your family has owned power tools, and you start seeing the proof everywhere: St. Nicholas on Oakley, the Ukrainian National Museum on Superior, the blue and yellow notices taped to the bakery wall. In that moment, you just know the door hates winter.

Oksana tests it three times.

"Not because I doubt you," she says. "Doors lie."

She pays you in cash and a warm loaf.

"Thank you," you say.

"You look hungry. Take it before my aunt starts measuring you with her eyes."

You drive to Logan Square with bread warming your truck like evidence.

## Three Sundays
You come back the next Sunday because the door still sticks in cold weather, which is partly true and mostly pathetic. Oksana arrives after liturgy at the cathedral, wearing a dark coat, her hair pinned back, carrying bread like it's medical supply.

You learn her in pieces. She is 30. Born in Lviv. Moved to Chicago at 22. Works nights as a nurse in a children's hospital, which explains why she looks tired in a way sleep alone cannot fix.

You are 33, a carpenter, divorced from the idea that you're good at talking, and famously bad at cooking according to every smoke detector you've ever owned.

Her aunt calls her "Oksanochka" and says she feeds everyone because she cannot turn it off.

A teenage cousin starts to whistle indoors.

Oksana turns sharply. "Do you want the money to fly away? We have rent."

You laugh once.

She looks pleased for exactly 3 seconds, then hides it by rearranging pastries.

## The boxes by the freezer
On the fourth Sunday, you find her near the bakery freezer, sealing cardboard boxes with tape that screams every time she pulls it loose. Medicine. Thermal socks. Children's vitamins. Gloves. Handwritten labels in Ukrainian and English.

Since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, the call from Lviv has come every night, and Oksana sends medicine and warm clothes home every month. She does not give speeches about history or point to maps. She just pulls tape while the freezer hums and her phone buzzes at hours when decent phones should be ashamed of themselves.

You offer to carry one box because it's heavy and because your hands are more useful than your mouth.

"Careful," she says. "That one has children's cough syrup. It cost more than your confidence."

You carry all four to her car.

Her mother appears on video from Lviv and inspects you like a building permit.

Her grandmother says something.

Oksana translates, "She says you have honest ears."

You don't know what to do with that.

## Borscht court
Oksana invites you to Sunday dinner above the bakery, which you misunderstand as casual until you see 11 people and a table that looks structurally important. There is borscht, varenyky, sour cream, rye bread, pickled mushrooms, and three different women explaining why the other two versions of borscht are technically wrong.

In 2022, UNESCO even put Ukrainian borscht cooking on its urgent safeguarding list. Oksana's aunt says UNESCO was right, but also did not ask her mother, which seems like an oversight.

You admit you once made soup from a packet.

The table goes quiet in a way usually reserved for medical news.

Oksana saves you without looking impressed.

"He fixes doors. We cannot ask one man to do everything."

After dinner, she hands you leftovers in reused containers and tells you which ones must come back, because Ukrainians track containers like property records.

Her hand brushes yours inside the apartment.

This time, she does not pull away.

## The museum and the shirt
Oksana takes you to the Ukrainian National Museum in Ukrainian Village on a cold afternoon when the lake wind makes everyone walk like guilty penguins. She stops at embroidered clothing and explains the vyshyvanka, the Ukrainian embroidered shirt, with patterns tied to regions and family memory.

She tells you Vyshyvanka Day falls on the third Thursday of May, like it is a birthday. She shows you photos of her father in a white shirt from western Ukraine, then her grandmother in an older pattern from near Lviv.

"Do the patterns mean something?" you ask.

"Everything means something," she says. "That is the problem."

In the gift shop, she buys you a simple embroidered bookmark because a full shirt would be too much too soon.

Outside, near Chicago Avenue, she finally explains the rules. Don't shake across doorways. Don't whistle inside. Sit before long trips, because leaving should never be rushed.

You joke about needing a manual.

"You would not read it," she says.

She is correct.

Three weeks later, you try anyway.

## The long conversation
After her night shift, you sit with her in a quiet diner near Western Avenue at 6:20 in the morning. The fluorescent lights make everyone look recently forgiven.

Oksana stirs coffee she will not drink.

"I like you," she says.

It sounds less like romance and more like a weather warning.

"My family is not background. They call. They worry. They ask questions with no ending."

"I understand," you say.

"No. You are being polite. That is different."

She tells you about her mother and grandmother in Lviv, the nightly calls, the boxes, the way guilt can become a household appliance if you plug it in long enough. She does not ask you to rescue anyone. That would be insulting. She needs someone who will not resent the weight.

Her phone buzzes. A nurse friend texts that a child finally went home.

Oksana reads it and smiles so softly you almost look away.

That is when you fall.

What gets you is the brief relief on her face before she composes herself again.

Mostly, you learn her calendar, her aunt's spare keys, and which boxes go in the trunk first.

## Love as logistics
That week, you carry boxes for her, answer calls, and learn which superstition matters before a door closes.

You repair shelving at the bakery. You drive her to a warehouse store for bulk supplies. You learn that dill appears in places where Americans would surrender.

After a 12-hour job, she leaves soup on your porch and texts, "Do not eat it cold like a raccoon."

You start sitting for 10 silent seconds before long drives because she does it. One day you forget, and both of you stare at each other like the house has noticed.

You do not whistle indoors anymore. You almost do once while sanding a cabinet in her aunt's kitchen.

Three women turn around at the same time.

On the third Thursday of May, you wear a modest vyshyvanka she helped choose. She pretends not to be moved.

She touches the cuff once.

"Good," she says. "You look less unfinished."

## Lviv
A year in, you travel with Oksana to Lviv to meet her family in person. Before you leave, you sit silently by the door while your suitcase waits like it has been accused of something.

Lviv is cobbled streets after rain, coffee houses with small cups and serious waiters, the Lviv Theatre of Opera and Ballet lit in the evening, church bells folding into traffic.

Her father greets you with homemade drinks and the expression of a man evaluating a chair he may have to sit in for the rest of his life.

You say one Ukrainian phrase badly. Her mother smiles, because bad effort is still effort.

Her grandmother tests you with pysanky, Easter eggs decorated with beeswax and dyes. Later, you learn Kolomyia even has a museum shaped like a giant pysanka, which feels exactly right.

Your lines wobble. Your egg looks emotionally unstable.

Her grandmother studies it.

"She says your hand is nervous," Oksana translates, "but it listens."

That night, Oksana admits she was afraid you would treat Lviv like a sad story.

"It's beautiful," you say.

"Yes," she says. "Remember that first."

## The pumpkin problem
Back in Chicago, you research the old proposal custom because asking Oksana casually in a parking lot suddenly feels like showing up to church in flip-flops.

You find starosty in an old article about Ukrainian wedding customs, matchmakers who accompany the groom to the bride's family, then Oksana's aunt confirms the pumpkin part on the phone, sounding far too pleased.

This is not calming information.

You recruit two friends as your extremely American starosty. One asks if he needs a sash.

"No," you say, with too much panic.

You go to the bakery after closing with bread, flowers, and your friends, who look like they are there to apologize to a village council.

Oksana's aunt immediately understands and becomes dangerously happy.

Her mother and grandmother join by video call from Lviv. Her father appears holding a glass and pretending this is normal.

Oksana walks in, sees the room, and freezes.

Her aunt solemnly brings out a pumpkin first.

Your stomach drops through the floor.

Then everyone laughs except you, because your body has not received the update.

"You really learned the pumpkin?" Oksana asks.

"I feared the pumpkin."

She says yes.

Fearing produce during a marriage proposal is not dignified.

## St. Nicholas
The wedding arrives at St. Nicholas Ukrainian Catholic Cathedral in Ukrainian Village, where the gold domes and old neighborhood streets make your rented shoes feel underqualified.

You wear a suit with a vyshyvanka-inspired embroidered detail because a full traditional look would feel borrowed.

"This is the first smart thing you have done all week," Oksana says.

The rushnyk appears, an embroidered ritual towel laid for the couple. Before the ceremony, Oksana's aunt smooths the rushnyk flat. "Whoever steps on it first will lead the family," she says. "Watch your feet."

You step on it second, on purpose.

Oksana notices. Her eyes shine, but her voice stays dry.

"Good," she says. "You are trainable."

The korovai, the richly decorated round wedding bread, is shared with guests. There is dancing for hours, elderly relatives with frightening stamina, children under tables, and you discovering that carpentry did not prepare your knees for this.

Her grandmother watches on video, wrapped in a shawl, crying quietly from Lviv.

By Tuesday, there are dishes in the sink, scrubs over a chair, rye bread in the freezer, and boxes waiting by the door.

## The apartment after
Marriage begins in your Chicago apartment with boxes by the door, hospital scrubs over a chair, rye bread in the freezer, and your toolbox living under the kitchen table like a quiet relative. Oksana comes home from the children's hospital too tired to untie both shoes, so one stays on while she apologizes to the floor.

"Sorry, old man," she says.

The war is in the packages now. Medicine, thermal socks, vitamins, warm clothes. You carry the boxes because you know how to be useful when words would only make the room heavier.

At night, her mother calls from Lviv and says your Ukrainian has improved from dangerous to merely suspicious.

Then bad weather cuts the call. Oksana stands by the sink, gripping a dish towel.

You don't promise. You wash the pot and wait.

The call returns. Her grandmother is annoyed, which is a blessing.

Oksana sits hard.

"I hate being far."

"I know."

## Sviat Vechir
Your first married Christmas Eve is Sviat Vechir, December 24 into 25 now, after Oksana explains that the Orthodox Church of Ukraine and Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church changed calendars in 2023.

Oksana insists on twelve meatless dishes, beginning with kutia, because her aunt says twelve means twelve. You get mushrooms and onions, which is apparently the culinary equivalent of safety scissors.

The table fills with kutia, meatless borscht, varenyky, holubtsi with grains and mushrooms, fish, beans, sauerkraut, pampushky, uzvar, and other dishes her aunt supervises like a small ministry.

You almost whistle while chopping. Oksana points the knife at the air near you.

This feels legally important.

Their faces fill the laptop from Lviv, and everyone speaks over everyone until the room feels full.

On the laptop, Lviv crackles, the table gets loud, and Oksana's face changes.

Oksana looks at the table, then at you.

"You burned nothing important."

You accept this as a love poem.

## The same door
Year two changes when Oksana's grandmother comes to Chicago, small, sharp-eyed, and wrapped for the airport like she expects Lake Michigan to attack.

Before her flight home, everyone sits in silence for a moment by the door.

But first, she inspects the apartment, the bakery, the boxes, the door you fixed, and the pysanka you kept despite its emotional instability. She touches the folded rushnyk and says something Oksana holds for herself.

On Sunday, the bakery door sticks again because Chicago weather has a personal grudge. You kneel on Chicago Avenue with your tools. Oksana stands inside with coffee, bread cooling behind her, her grandmother by the window.

You fix the hinge. You test it three times.

You offer your hand without thinking.

She looks at the doorway, then your hand.

"Safe?"

"Inside."

She takes it. Her hand is warm in yours, and the door closes cleanly behind you.
