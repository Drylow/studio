# POV: You Fall in Love With a Geisha

A geisha's finger stops on the rib you fixed, and your rent money stops with it. Rain dents the puddles on Hanamikoji Street in Gion. "Show me where it bends," the geisha says, eyes on your hands, not your face. Her okasan, who runs the house, puts your payment away. "Come back when it's right." The young woman hands the umbrella back and goes inside. You can return to her door. Her teahouse is another matter: without an introduction, nobody gets in.

## The repair
You straighten the rib at your father's shop on Gojo Street, but the payment you need for rent is still in Gion. It's spring 1936. You're twenty-six, and the landlord calls you Shinji when he wants his money. He'll be calling soon.

Under the shop lamp, bamboo shavings catch in the folds of your sleeves. You lift the oil paper, ease the rib back into place, and press fresh glue beneath it. Your father's tools fit your hands better than his ledger does. You know exactly how long the glue needs. You check the rib twice anyway.

The next afternoon, you carry the umbrella back to Hanamikoji Street. The young woman opens it in the doorway. She's twenty-four, you learn later, and her name is Kikuha. Right now, she doesn't look at you. Her finger travels along each rib, stops at the one you fixed, and moves on.

The okasan counts out your payment. You try to keep your relief off your face.

"How long before it holds in heavy rain?" Kikuha asks.

"By tomorrow morning."

She glances toward the clear patch of sky beyond the roof. "Good."

You carry that "Good" back to Gojo Street.

The following morning, another umbrella arrives at your shop.

## A second order
The messenger says Kikuha finds a mark on the handle uneven. You turn the umbrella under the lamp, then take it to the window. You check it three times. The mark is there, but nothing is damaged, and you can't see what she wants changed.

You smooth it anyway. Your thumb leaves a faint shine in the wood.

When you bring it back, the okasan takes the umbrella from you slowly. She watches your hands as you fold it, the way your father used to watch an apprentice with his best knife. Behind her, Kikuha stands in the doorway. A white collar shows above her formal kimono, and a wide obi holds everything in place.

Inside the wooden machiya, someone practices a phrase on the shamisen. The notes start again. Voices pass down the hall, discussing the time for an evening engagement. You stand with the delivery in your hands longer than you need to.

Kikuha catches you listening. "What do you hear?"

You look at the umbrella. "The handle's quiet now."

Her mouth moves, almost a smile. "Is it?"

The okasan gives you your payment. This time, she stays at the doorway until you step back onto the street. Kikuha takes the umbrella inside.

You can fix what she sends you. You're beginning to wonder what fills the hours after that door closes.

## The door you cannot enter
One evening, an errand takes you through Gion Kobu. Outside an ochaya, guests pause to shake the rain from their sleeves. The woman at the entrance greets each of them by name. You slow down.

The woman at the entrance sees you looking. "Ichigen-san okotowari," she says. "A first-time guest needs an introduction. With kake, the bill comes weeks later. Someone has to vouch for you." You think of the landlord waiting for your rent. Nobody is likely to vouch for that ledger.

You have an umbrella repair to deliver at Kikuha's house, so you return to its doorway. Inside, the okasan sits over an account book. You can see entries for lessons, then kimono expenses. She runs the okiya. She's also the one deciding whether your work is sound and whether Kikuha has time to spare.

She closes the book. "She has work tomorrow."

"I know."

You pick up your empty tool case. Kikuha steps out with a shamisen case in one hand and takes the repaired umbrella with the other. The sky is clear. She thanks you, then walks beside you as far as the corner.

You don't ask where she's going. She doesn't explain why she brought the umbrella.

## The Shirakawa walk
A few evenings later, you find her beside the Shirakawa canal. The air is cool under the willows, and there's enough light left to see the umbrella in her hand.

"Have you finished inspecting it?" she asks.

"I thought that was your job."

"It seems to be yours too."

She starts walking. You fall into step, careful to leave room between her sleeve and yours. Water moves against the stone bank. After a while, she tells you about the lesson she has in the morning and the engagement waiting after it.

"I still have those obligations," she says. "If you're waiting for me, they won't go away."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

You watch her hands on the umbrella handle. "I can't afford an introduction to an ochaya."

"I haven't asked you to buy one."

She taps the handle twice and lets the third beat pass. "Listen."

You hear water. A cart somewhere behind you. She taps again, then explains the space a shamisen player leaves before the next note. You get it wrong twice. On the third try, she nods.

A wrapped sweet bean bun slips from her sleeve. She catches it before it falls, unwraps it, and takes a bite without losing her perfect posture.

"You planned this walk," you say.

"The bun was planned. You weren't."

She finishes it before you reach the bridge. When she turns toward home, you stay where you are. You'll see her again when she has time.

By next spring, you want to hear the pause with everyone else in the room.

## The leaking umbrella
The storm comes on a Tuesday, out of a clear sky, while you're halfway across the Shijo bridge. Kikuha is beside you with the plain umbrella she keeps sending back for repairs. She opens it. Water pours straight through a seam you never touched.

You both look up at it.

"You told me it was fine," you say.

"I told the messenger it was uneven."

"Water is running down my neck."

She tilts it so the water runs off the side instead of onto her collar. The white silk stays dry. Your shoulder does not. Under the bridge lanterns, the Kamo River turns brown and loud, and people run past holding newspapers over their heads.

"Hold it," she says, and hands you the handle.

You hold it. She reaches up with one finger and presses the paper back against the rib where the glue has let go. It doesn't help. Rain finds the same gap again.

Then Kikuha laughs. It is the first time you've heard it. It's short and surprised, as if it got out before she could check it for flaws. She covers her mouth with her sleeve and keeps walking.

You walk her to the corner of Hanamikoji, as close as you're allowed. She takes the umbrella back.

"Bring it tomorrow," she says. "Properly this time."

"It was never broken."

"It is now."

That night, you take the umbrella apart down to the last rib. There's nothing wrong with the bamboo. Somebody has carefully peeled the paper away from one seam with a fingernail.

You glue it back, slowly. You don't tell anyone what you found.

## The cheapest seats
Before the spring dance, you learn how little time she has. You walk beside the Kamo River after a delivery, drink tea before her rehearsal, and sit quietly when she's trying to hear a phrase in her head.

At the shop, you paint chrysanthemums on a new oil-paper umbrella. Your father's brushes are stiff, and you practice on scraps until the petals look like something she might recognize.

You bring it to Gion on a cold afternoon. Kikuha opens it beneath the eaves and runs a finger along each bamboo rib. Only then does she turn it toward the light.

"My name means chrysanthemum leaf," she says.

"I know."

"Then you could have painted leaves."

She's smiling when she says it. You have to look down at your shoes.

In spring 1937, you buy the cheapest seat at the Miyako Odori. The program says Gion Kobu has danced the Miyako Odori every spring since 1872, all in one style, the Inoue school. You search the stage for her. When you find Kikuha, she is keeping time with women who know precisely where she'll be.

Afterward, she meets you near the entrance, the painted umbrella in her hand.

"What did you notice?"

You tell her about the pause before a shamisen phrase. Her face changes.

"You listened."

The okasan steps up beside her. She studies the flowers, then your hands.

"Come speak to me tomorrow," she says.

You look at Kikuha. She gives you no hint what the answer will cost.

## Two years of saving
The next morning, the okasan opens her account book at a low table. She shows you what the house has paid for Kikuha's lessons and what remains owing. The kimono and obi she wears for engagements belong to the house and must come back when she leaves.

You look at the figures twice. The umbrella in your hands becomes very light.

"Can you manage it?" the okasan asks.

"Not today."

"I can see that."

She closes the book. Outside, someone begins practicing shamisen, starting the same measure again until it holds.

Kikuha finds you at the gate. You tell her what the okasan said.

"I know the account," she says. "You don't have to decide for me."

For nearly a year, you put aside money from repairs. Kikuha saves from her engagements too. At the okasan's table, she checks each entry, adds her savings to yours, and watches the okasan mark the balance settled. Sometimes she needs you to walk with her and say nothing.

In 1938, she tells her sisters she's leaving. At her modest hiki-iwai farewell, they share sweets and make her promise to visit. She returns the house's garments folded with care. The okasan straightens a corner Kikuha has already straightened.

"You'll eat properly?" she asks.

Kikuha glances at you. "We'll learn."

At your small wedding, you lift each of the three sake cups for san-san-kudo and take three sips from each. Your hand shakes around the last cup.

Kikuha leans close. "Still crooked."

The next morning, she stands beside you in the Gojo kitchen, watching a breakfast pot boil over.

## The Gojo kitchen
The rooms behind the shop smell of rice, paper glue, and whatever Kikuha has done to breakfast. She lifts the lid, looks at the scorched bottom, and sets it down again.

"Did your father cook?" she asks.

"Only when he had to."

"We should ask someone."

Later, you try her shamisen. You manage two notes. Kikuha takes it gently from your hands, and you agree it belongs on the shelf.

At the workbench, she asks for a brush. Her chrysanthemums grow across the oil paper in fine, clean strokes, each flower small enough to leave room for the rain. A customer asks for one like it. Then another does.

Her former sisters stop by for repairs. One comes to buy an umbrella and stays to drink tea while Kikuha finishes a painted leaf. You hear them laugh in the back room before you hear the door open.

Loving her is holding the oil paper flat while she paints, and not breathing on the wet petals.

You guide her blade along bamboo, keeping the rib strong. Kikuha paints a thin green stem beneath the flower and wipes her brush on a folded rag. By winter, there's a little money left after rent. You leave it in the ledger for three days before either of you believes it.

One evening, Kikuha finds a rib bending in the storage bundle. She holds it against the lamp, the way she inspected your first repair.

"Keep the straight stock apart," she says.

You move it that night. Soon, even the bent pieces are worth keeping.

## A white collar
In the spring of 1939, one of Kikuha's younger sisters from the okiya comes to the shop on Gojo Street. She's twenty now, and she walks in without stopping at the counter.

"Look," she says, and turns her head.

Her collar is white. A week ago it was red. Kikuha explained it to you once over tea: an apprentice wears red at the neck, and at the erikae, the turning of the collar, it becomes white. Her long sleeves shorten. Her hair changes. She becomes a geiko, a full geisha, and she pays her own way in the world.

Kikuha puts down her brush. She doesn't say anything at first. She walks around the girl once, straightens the edge of the collar by the width of a fingernail, and steps back.

"Good," she says.

It's the same "Good" she gave your first repair. You recognize it from across the room.

The sister has come to order an umbrella for her first spring as a geiko. She wants chrysanthemums, like Kikuha's. Kikuha says no. She asks the sister what flowers she loves. The answer is plum blossoms, because she was born in February. Kikuha nods and writes it on a scrap.

You build the frame. Kikuha paints for three evenings. On the fourth, the sister collects it, opens it in the doorway under a clear sky, and checks every rib with one finger, exactly the way Kikuha checks yours.

When she's gone, Kikuha sits on the step and looks at the street for a while.

"Do you miss it?" you ask.

"I miss the collar," she says. "Not the bills."

She goes back to the workbench. The plum blossom scraps are still on it. She folds them into the ledger, between the pages for rent and glue.

## Short supplies
By late 1939, you start setting aside every length of usable bamboo. By 1940, you patch oil paper where you once would have replaced it. At the counter, you turn away an umbrella with a split handle because you have no sound piece to give it.

The customer turns it over in her hands and glances at Kikuha's chrysanthemums drying on the rack. She knows that brushwork from the Gion stage.

"I used to see geisha everywhere near Gion," she says. "Now I see the cafe girls on my way home." She's right: Britannica puts Japan's geisha at about 80,000 in the 1920s, and there are fewer every year now.

The customer sets the umbrella on the counter. Outside, a waitress from the cafe across the street hurries past with her coat over her hair. Kikuha looks after her. Kikuha knows women in both kinds of work. When the customer leaves, she asks you whether the handle might hold with another binding.

"Maybe for a while," you say.

"Then tell her that."

You call the customer back. She waits while you wind cord around the split and test it under your thumb.

In the evenings, the neighbors' girls come for shamisen lessons. Kikuha moves from one to the next, correcting a finger, listening through a whole phrase before asking them to try again. You recognize the patient touch she put on your crooked umbrella rib in Gion.

One girl loses the beat and looks ready to put the instrument away. Kikuha taps the space before the next note.

"There. You have time."

A former sister arrives while the girls are playing. Her sleeves are wet from the rain.

"The okasan is ill," she says.

Kikuha closes the lesson early and reaches for her coat.

This time, you follow her to Gion.

## The okasan's room
The okasan's room smells of hot tea and damp bedding. Kikuha sets down a clean quilt, lifts the old one from the floor, and asks where the washbasin goes. She still knows the house well enough to find it without waiting for an answer.

You stay near the doorway with an umbrella that leaks onto the boards. Under the shop lamp that night, you replace its split bamboo rib. The next morning, you bring it back and leave it beside the okasan's pillow, close enough for her to reach.

She presses the rib with one finger.

"This one is straight."

"Yes."

"Good." She closes her eyes. "Put the tea closer."

Kikuha comes every few days. You carry bedding when it needs changing and wait downstairs while the two of them talk. Once, as you gather your tools, you hear Kikuha say she misses the stage. Her voice is so quiet you almost miss it.

The okasan answers, "I know."

At home, Kikuha starts a lesson for the neighbor's girl. She demonstrates the opening phrase, then stops with her fingers resting on the strings. She tells the girl where the next note belongs, but her hands are tired. You put the kettle on while the girl practices. Kikuha listens from her chair.

She teaches again the following week. By early 1944, the okasan can sit up to hear the girls play when they visit. Then a sister comes to your shop before noon, carrying news of an order that has reached the teahouses.

## March 1944
The teahouse door is shut when Kikuha reaches it. The government order has reached her old teahouse. The geisha houses are closing too. Two of her former sisters stand outside with bundles in their arms. One has a comb wrapped in cloth. The other keeps hold of her instrument case while the okasan checks the lock.

Kikuha takes a bundle so the okasan can walk home. On the way, she asks each sister where she's going. Neither has an answer for the following week.

The next morning, one comes to your kitchen before reporting to factory work. Kikuha wraps rice balls for her and slips in the last piece of pickled radish.

"Eat before you go in," she says.

The sister nods. Her hands stay around the warm packet.

Kikuha left the profession years ago. The women who remained now face wartime labor measures, and she writes down their addresses so she can find them after their shifts. You watch her make room at the table for anyone who comes by.

The okasan asks her to keep a shamisen safe. Kikuha carries it home beneath the chrysanthemum umbrella, holding the case high when rain runs off the edge. You've repaired those ribs again. She checks them at the shop door before she goes inside.

Customers still bring you umbrellas. Some offer a little rice. Some promise payment later. You mend the ones you can, though each repair takes bamboo you may have trouble replacing.

## Factory hours
By autumn, the shop fills with umbrellas waiting for parts. You take a sound rib from one that cannot be repaired and fit it into another. You save the rest in a box beneath the counter. There is work most days, but the money left after buying glue barely covers rice.

Kikuha's former sisters leave Gion before dawn to sew uniforms at a factory. Kikuha, already retired, watches them go. She takes work beside them because your repairs no longer earn enough.

You wrap her lunch before dawn. Some mornings she wakes before you and finds it beside the door, tied in a cloth still warm from your hands. At night, a neighbor's girl comes over with a shamisen. Kikuha shows her where to place her fingers, then makes her try the phrase again while she washes thread dust from her own.

"Leave room there," she tells her.

You hear the pause from the workbench.

The ribs you cannot use yet go into your father's old tool roll. Among them is the crooked one from your first delivery to Gion. You've kept it so long you know its bend by touch.

One afternoon, a notice appears near the end of your block. It concerns the buildings marked for wartime firebreak demolition. Across Kyoto, up to 10,000 households get the same paper, Japan Property Central reports, most with about a week's notice. You read the address twice. Then you fold your tool roll, go into the kitchen, and tell Kikuha how much time you have to empty the shop.

## The Gojo firebreak
On Gojo, a demolition mark cuts across your father's shutters and the shopfront next door. You pry the shelves off the wall. One nail holds long enough to split the wood in your hand.

You carry the bamboo stock out at night on a borrowed handcart. Kikuha takes the shamisen and the chrysanthemum umbrella. You tie your father's tools in a roll, with the saved crooked rib tucked between two chisels. On the last trip, you find a brush beneath the counter. Kikuha puts it in her sleeve.

The rooms behind the shop take less time to clear than you expect. You keep reaching for things already on the cart.

Sirens sound one night after the shop is gone. Far to the south, you see light toward Osaka. Later, you hear what happened there. Kyoto is never firebombed. On Gojo, the place where you worked is open ground now, swept flat.

In August, you listen to the radio with Kikuha and the okasan. When the broadcast ends, the okasan reaches for her cup and finds it empty. Kikuha fills it. The three of you stay seated until the tea cools.

There is little work now, and Kikuha chooses her last good kimono to sell for rice. She folds it herself. Afterward, she takes your handcart to the okasan's house, where the storeroom has space for two bedding rolls.

In the morning, you open your tool roll on the floor. Kikuha sits beside you with her brush.

## The storeroom winter
The winter of 1946 is the coldest you remember. The okasan's storeroom has one small window and no stove, so the three of you share the brazier in her front room and eat whatever the day produced. Some days that's sweet potato. Some days it's tea.

The okasan teaches you to fold a kimono, since you're living among them now. You get the sleeves wrong four times. She doesn't laugh. She just unfolds it and starts again, the way Kikuha taught the girls to start a phrase again.

"There," she says on the fifth try, and smooths the sleeve flat with her palm.

Kikuha watches from the brazier with her knees tucked under her. She has her shamisen across her lap, but she doesn't play. The strings are old and there's no money for new ones.

One night you find a way. You cut a thin length of bamboo from your old stock, shave it down under the lamp, and make a new bridge for the instrument, the small piece that holds the strings off the body. It takes three evenings. Your first attempt splits. Your second is too tall.

The third one fits. Kikuha tunes the strings and plays one phrase, very quietly, so the neighbors won't hear. Then she plays the pause after it. The okasan closes her eyes.

"Where did you get the bamboo?" Kikuha asks.

You show her the tool roll. The crooked rib from your first delivery is shorter now.

She looks at it for a long time. "You cut that one."

"It was the best piece I had."

She doesn't say anything. She plays the phrase again, a little louder.

## A table by the street
The okasan comes in while Kikuha is washing her brush. A teahouse in Gion has opened again, she says, and the younger women need someone to teach them shamisen.

"Your hands remember."

Kikuha looks at the brush in the basin. "So do yours."

She goes back three mornings a week. You hear her teaching through the open window when you bring lunch: one phrase, a pause, the same phrase again. A student misses the pause. Kikuha taps the place on the floor where the note belongs.

You build a table by the street from wood you pull out of a demolished house. One leg is shorter than the others. You fix it before Kikuha gets home.

The first umbrella on it has a rib from your old stock beneath new oil paper. Kikuha paints chrysanthemums near the edge. A woman she once worked with stops on her way past, turns it toward the light, and says, "Those are yours." She pays for it and walks on with the flowers over her shoulder.

Some weeks you sell nothing. You search for sound bamboo, stretch paper across a frame, and wait until the glue holds before you fold it. When rain comes early, you carry a finished umbrella home against your chest.

One spring afternoon, another one dries open beside the table. Kikuha arrives from Gion with a program in her hand.

"The Miyako Odori is on again," she says. "Do you remember which seats you could afford?"

You take the program. You remember exactly.

## The same row
Spring 1952 finds you beside Kikuha in the cheapest seats at the Miyako Odori. You check the row number on your tickets twice. It's the same row you chose in 1937, when even one seat took a painful bite out of the rent. This time her sleeve rests against yours. You keep both tickets folded in your palm until the lights go down.

The stage fills with color. A dancer turns, and the silk at her sleeves catches the light. Kikuha sits very still. You know she recognizes people up there, though she doesn't point them out. Her hands rest in her lap, one thumb pressed lightly against the other.

The shamisen starts a phrase. You hear where the next note should be. It doesn't come yet. Beside you, Kikuha glances over. You wait through the space with her, and when the note arrives, her thumb moves once against her hand. She turns back to the stage. Your sleeve stays against hers for the rest of the performance.

Outside, rain begins while you're still under the eaves. People gather close to the doors, opening umbrellas wherever there's room. You have to wait for a clear patch of street. Kikuha watches you reach under your arm for the one wrapped in its worn cloth cover.

"You brought it."

"I did."

You open the chrysanthemum umbrella over both of you. Kikuha places one finger against a rib and follows it from the handle toward the edge. At the first doorway in Gion, she makes you show her the bend. Here, she checks the rib once more and says nothing.

The painted chrysanthemums move down Hanamikoji through the rain.
