# Recherche et consignes — POV: You Fall in Love With a Geisha

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS about her world (use several, rounded, each inside a scene, with the source named in the sentence; never invent other statistics):
- "Geisha" means "person of the arts" (gei, art; sha, person); in Kyoto a full geisha is called a geiko and an apprentice a maiko (Britannica; Kyoto city tourism).
- Britannica and Merriam-Webster both put the number of geisha in Japan at about 80,000 in the 1920s; the University of Victoria library notes about 80,000 by 1929. Today there are only around a thousand (The Economist). Cafe waitresses began to outnumber geisha in the 1930s.
- Gion in Kyoto is one of the city's five geisha districts (hanamachi): Gion Kobu, Gion Higashi, Pontocho, Kamishichiken and Miyagawacho (Kyoto city tourism). Gion Kobu's spring dance show, the Miyako Odori, began in 1872 for the Kyoto Exposition, and Gion Kobu's geiko dance only in the Inoue school style.
- The teahouses (ochaya) follow "ichigen-san okotowari", no first-time customers: you can only enter with an introduction from a trusted patron who vouches for you and for your ability to pay, and bills come weeks later on trust ("kake", credit) (Japan travel guides on Gion etiquette). A geisha lives and is managed by an okiya (geisha house) run by a proprietress she calls "okasan", mother; retired geisha often become okami, proprietress of a teahouse or okiya (Geisha of Japan, Kyoto guides).
- The collar tells her rank: an apprentice wears a red collar; at the "erikae" (turning of the collar) ceremony she becomes a geiko and her collar turns white, her long-sleeved furisode changes to a short-sleeved kimono, and her hair changes (Geisha of Japan). OUR heroine is a geiko with a white collar in every scene.
- "San-san-kudo", three sips from each of three sake cups, binds a geiko to her older "sister" and is also the classic Japanese wedding ritual (Geisha of Japan).
- "Hiki-iwai" is a geisha's retirement celebration; marriage is one of the reasons she leaves the profession (Geisha of Japan). The famous Gion geiko Mineko Iwasaki wrote in her memoir "Geisha, a Life" that she retired at 29 and later married.
- In March 1944 the Japanese government closed teahouses and geisha houses as an emergency war measure (contemporary 1944 reports), and from the Labour Conscription Law of September 1944 geisha were sent to war work; many worked in factories (University of Maryland archive).
- Kyoto escaped both firebombing and the atomic bomb, but city officials tore down many wooden houses as firebreaks (University of Maryland archive). Japan Property Central reports that in 1944 and 1945 buildings housing up to 10,000 households were forcibly demolished in Kyoto for firebreaks, residents getting about a week's notice; Gojo, Oike and Horikawa streets were widened this way and became today's wide avenues.
- Main arts: dance, the three-string shamisen, singing, tea ceremony, conversation and drinking games; a geiko's kimono and obi are costly and belong to the okiya until she is independent.
STORY SHAPE: Kyoto, Gion, spring 1936 to spring 1952. "You" are FICTIONAL: Shinji, 26, an oil-paper umbrella maker (wagasa) in a tiny shop on Gojo Street inherited from your father; careful, funny, broke, good with bamboo and glue, hopeless with words. She is FICTIONAL: Kikuha, 24, a geiko of Gion Kobu (white collar), the best shamisen player of her okiya, dry and very funny without smiling, secretly eats sweet bean buns, terrible at cooking, perfect posture. HOOK: a rainy night; her okiya orders a repaired umbrella; you deliver it, she opens it in the doorway, finds a crooked rib in two seconds and says "Is this how you fix things?"; behind her the okasan watches you like a bill. You cannot even enter a teahouse: no introduction, no money (ichigen-san okotowari). 0-20%: your shop, your ledger, the umbrella she keeps sending back for "repairs" that are not needed; you learn the world: the okiya, the okasan, the white collar, the bill that arrives weeks later. 20-45%: courtship in secret, short and proper: walks by the Shirakawa canal and the Kamo river, she teaches you to listen to a shamisen, you make her an umbrella painted with chrysanthemums (her name means chrysanthemum leaf); the Miyako Odori in spring 1937 where you watch from the cheapest seats; her okasan opposes you (Kikuha's debt for kimonos and lessons); you save for two years. ~45%: she chooses to leave: hiki-iwai, the farewell to her sisters, she hands back her kimonos to the okiya; your small wedding with san-san-kudo, your trembling hand on the cup, she whispers "Still crooked." 45-55%: married life on Gojo Street: she can't cook, you can't play, she paints umbrellas better than you, the shop finally earns money; her old sisters come to buy umbrellas. 55-85% (at least 8 scenes): (1) 1938-40 war rationing, oil-paper and bamboo get hard to find, cafe girls outnumber geisha; (2) she teaches shamisen to neighbours' girls in the evenings; (3) her former okasan falls ill and Kikuha nurses her; (4) March 1944: the teahouses and geisha houses are closed by order; her old sisters are sent to factories, Kikuha brings them rice balls; (5) she works in a factory sewing uniforms (war work), you make umbrellas for nobody; (6) 1945: your Gojo Street block is ordered torn down as a firebreak, you carry the bamboo stock on a cart at night; (7) the sirens over other cities, Kyoto spared, the glow of Osaka on the horizon (non-graphic); (8) August 1945, the radio, silence. CRISIS ~80%: no shop, no work; Kikuha sells her last good kimono for rice; you sleep in her old okasan's storeroom. 85-100%: the teahouses slowly reopen after the war; Kikuha is asked to teach shamisen to the new geiko of Gion; you rebuild a stall; spring 1952, the Miyako Odori returns at full size (stated as the story's time, not as a statistic), you watch from the cheapest seats again, the same row; ENDING: rain on the way out; you open one umbrella for both of you; she checks the ribs and, for the first time, says nothing. Last image: a chrysanthemum umbrella in the rain on a Gion street.
