# POV: You Marry a Female Samurai

Two seconds after meeting Kaede, her wooden naginata is resting under your chin. You are on the floor of a dojo in Aizu-Wakamatsu, dust on your cheek, while young men try not to laugh. Outside, snow slides off the roof. Your uncle watches, regretting your education. You're thinking about your stipend and your ribs. Kaede looks down like she has found a dropped coin. "Are you always this bad?" she asks. Behind her, a real blade waits in the family storehouse. Later it's a wedding gift. Later, it follows her to Tsuruga Castle, as the world that trained her cracks.

## The rice storehouse
After being flattened, ledgers start looking safer than practice floors. Aizu in 1863 sits under Tsuruga Castle, all rice storehouses, sake breweries, tiled roofs, and winter snow that makes every street sound padded. You are the second son of a low-ranking samurai family, which means your inheritance is mostly manners and anxiety. Your handwriting is beautiful. Your arithmetic is better than your sword work. This makes you useful in a quiet, unromantic way. You keep accounts of rice stipends for the domain, measuring other men's honor in neat columns while your own still has dust on it. Your uncle says your wrist is weak. The storehouse supervisor says your numerals never lie. One compliment pays better. Years later, that same calligraphy will keep Kaede's school alive. For now, it is the safe skill of a man trying not to be noticed.

## What the naginata means
You first see the real blade named in a bridal inventory, written between lacquer boxes and bolts of silk. The ink looks harmless. Your uncle sends you back on another cold morning because humiliation is cheaper than private lessons. Kaede trains in kimono and hakama with daughters of samurai families, calm and exact, with humor so dry boys notice the insult several breaths late. She corrects a student's hands with two fingers. He moves backward anyway. People call women like her onna-bugeisha, woman martial artist: daughters of samurai families, trained to fight and to defend the household. You understand this better when Kaede lifts the naginata, a long pole with a curved blade, and keeps you at a distance so perfectly it feels personal. "Stay out of reach," she says. "Then decide when they come in." You try to laugh. Your ribs vote no. After training, near Nanokamachi Street, you see her buy manju and eat one behind her sleeve. "Accounts men should not stare," she says. "They should count."

## The go-between
It takes three weeks before your mother stops asking why you walk like an old man. Then a nakodo visits your house, carrying polite words that somehow make the room smaller. Your mother serves tea too quickly. Your father examines the floor as if the floor has betrayed him before. You understand the danger only when Kaede's family name is spoken. The arrangement makes sense, which is the frightening part. Your account work is respected. Her family values a steady husband. As a second son, you are not expected to inherit much beyond obligations. Your mother whispers that respected houses do not send daughters with that much presence unless they are testing someone. You ask if Kaede knows. The nakodo folds his hands. "Miss Kaede knows most things before men explain them." Being praised as steady makes you feel like someone has inspected you for leaks and approved the roof. You remember the naginata under your chin. You remember the manju in her sleeve. The miai is set near the castle town.

## The miai tea house
You are instructed to wear your best kimono and not speak too much, the first instruction all year that matches your talents. The miai happens on an afternoon when thawing snow falls from the eaves in heavy drops. The tea house smells of tatami, charcoal, and roasted tea. The nakodo sits like a referee pretending not to be one. Kaede enters in a subdued kimono, composed enough to make everyone else look badly assembled. Her mother asks about your position at the rice storehouse. You answer too honestly, explaining moisture damage, missing tallies, and why one warehouse clerk should never be trusted with subtraction. Silence follows. Kaede lowers her eyes. You think you have ruined everything. Then she says, "A man who fears bad numbers may fear bad habits too." You ask one small question. "Do you want this?" She looks at you for exactly three seconds. The charcoal cracks. "I want a house where I am not asked to become smaller." You say, "I understand." She says, "No. But you might."

Your mother notices the long wrapped shape in the dowry list before anyone else, then looks at Kaede for a long time.

## The wedding cups
The wedding arrives before you feel ready, which seems to be the traditional way of becoming a husband. In the front room of your family house in Aizu, the nakodo sits straight-backed in the cold. Family witnesses kneel along the tatami, their formal kimono making the room feel smaller. The air smells of sake, cedar, and relatives trying to look solemn while counting one another's sleeves.

You and Kaede sit before the cups.

San-san-kudo is simple, which makes it dangerous. Three cups. Three sips from each. Nine sips total, every movement watched by people who will remember if your hand fails. Your hand trembles once. Only once. Kaede's face does not change, but her sleeve shifts just enough to hide it.

That is the first kindness she gives you as your wife.

In the long Edo peace, the naginata has become a status symbol, often part of a samurai bride's dowry. In this house, it arrives with the bride's goods, with lacquer boxes, folded kimono, household goods, and the real blade wrapped in cloth.

Your mother looks at it. Then at Kaede.

"It will not sleep in the kitchen," Kaede says.

"Of course not," your mother says.

The same kind of weapon that once rested under your chin now stands inside your house as marriage property.

It stands there quietly until the day it leaves with her.

## Winter married life
Married life begins with deep Aizu winter. Snow sits on the courtyard stones. Rice steams in wooden bowls. Sake warms beside the hearth while Kaede tucks back her sleeves and trains before dawn.

You grind ink by lamplight and copy warehouse figures. She passes behind you and stops.

"Shoulders low," she says. "Spine awake. Feet honest."

"Numbers don't require battle stance."

"Bad numbers attack quietly."

This is difficult to argue with, because she is usually right.

She beats you in every practice match, even the ones she calls gentle, which is a word with no legal meaning in your house. Afterward, she eats manju from a paper packet and does not deny it, because denial would waste energy.

At night, she keeps a copied passage from The Tale of the Heike, where Tomoe Gozen is remembered as an archer and rider worth a thousand men. Kaede keeps it close because it proves women like her were remembered.

Sometimes she says she fears becoming only a weapon.

You make fresh ink when hers dries.

For a while, things actually seem fine. Snow falls. Ledgers balance. Her blade cuts the morning air.

That does not last.

## The war reaches Aizu
By 1868, talk in the rice storehouse changes. Men stop arguing about spoilage and begin arguing about roads, troops, loyalties, and rumors that arrive faster than official messages.

During the Boshin War, forces of the new imperial government press into the northeast. Aizu becomes one of the domains under attack. Tsuruga Castle, which has always sat above your life like weather, becomes the place everyone looks toward.

Then the siege begins.

People later call the siege about a month. Inside the walls, a month feels longer. You feel it as smoke beyond tiled roofs, bells at the wrong hour, hurried footsteps, gates closing, and men speaking with wounded pride.

Women tie back their sleeves.

Kaede prepares the naginata without drama. She checks the wrapping, the blade, the grip, the cord, with the calm of someone packing for bad weather. The weapon that entered your home as dowry now leaves it as discipline under pressure.

You carry rice records and supplies inside the castle, because even disaster needs someone to know what is left.

A castle under siege still requiring ledgers is the kind of thing that makes an accounts man age ten years before breakfast.

Up to this point, Kaede has controlled courtyards, lessons, and household space.

That is about to change.

## The women's unit
Before dawn, Kaede finds you in the courtyard. Frost dulls the stones. The town is quiet in the way a room is quiet before bad news.

"I am going," she says.

You know where before she explains. In 1868, during the Boshin War, a volunteer unit of about twenty to thirty women from Aizu samurai families fights with naginata. You hear Nakano Takeko's name from others with respect. Men lower their voices when they say it.

Kaede does not give a speech. She tightens the cord on her sleeve.

"The house is not only walls."

You want to ask her not to go. The words rise. You close your mouth. She ties the cord on her sleeve.

"What do you need?" you ask.

"Stand properly when you wait."

So you carry rice. You tally supplies. You copy names inside Tsuruga Castle while the fighting reaches you first as reports, then as sound beyond the walls.

News comes in fragments. Later, you hear Nakano Takeko has fallen. No one speaks loudly after that.

For a time, Kaede is absent, and every sound in the castle seems shaped like her returning or not returning.

At night, snow slides from the eaves, and each small sound makes you lift your head from the ledger, waiting for her steps in the courtyard.

## After surrender
After Aizu surrenders, Tsuruga Castle still stands above the town, and everyone understands the old rules have broken.

Kaede returns at the edge of the courtyard with the naginata wrapped in cloth. Dirt darkens her hem. Her face is older by more than days.

For one second, you are back on the dojo floor, dust on your cheek, unable to move.

She looks you over.

"You are standing badly."

The laugh comes out ugly, before you can stop it.

That night, her hand grips yours too long before letting go. She sleeps in pieces. A wooden bucket clatters, and she turns toward it before she is fully awake.

"Clean it," she says.

So you clean the blade. Not as romance. As work.

The naginata has followed her from the dojo to your doorway to the courtyard. Tonight it carries the people who survived and the ones who didn't.

Your quietness, your caution, your ledgers, your careful hands, all become useful in a ruined house. You copy names, heat water, repair mats, and sit awake when she cannot sleep.

## The sword ban
The early Meiji years arrive as paperwork. New officials. New seals. Changed names on documents. Old stipends become thinner, then words men say with lowered eyes.

Men who once wore swords avoid mirrors.

In 1876, the Meiji government's Haitorei edict bans carrying swords in public. Men keep touching the empty place at their hip where a scabbard used to sit.

Your own sword, never your best argument, becomes an object that belongs in a chest instead of at your hip.

Kaede wraps the naginata in cloth. It looks like a burial until she watches your hands.

"Do not wrap it like a fish."

"I was going to call it careful."

"You may call many things what they are not."

Kaede looks at the wrapped naginata for a long time, deciding what can still be taught.

So the naginata changes again. Kaede turns the old household weapon into something girls can practice before breakfast.

## The small school
Kaede opens the school in a clean courtyard near Nanokamachi Street, with borrowed mats, repaired wooden weapons, and patience that still makes grown men step aside.

In the Meiji era, the naginata lives on as a martial art taught to women and girls in schools. Her courtyard becomes one local version of that survival.

You keep the books. Names. Fees when families can pay. Repairs, charcoal, paper, cords.

Your calligraphy, once a safe skill, now writes the future of her work in careful columns.

The naginata has been humiliation under your chin, dowry in your house, and discipline under siege. Now Kaede turns it into practice.

Girls arrive shy and leave standing differently.

Kaede corrects them with precise mercy, and when they cannot see it, she smiles.

Your mother sends tea and pretends it is for everyone.

## Dawn posture
Years later, dawn finds you in the courtyard before the students arrive. Frost softens the ground. The town is waking by degrees, one shutter, one kettle.

You sweep dust from the practice space while Kaede checks a wooden naginata. She is older now. Still exact. The line of her back could correct a room by entering it.

You remember the first day. Cold boards under your palms. Late snow on the roof. A weapon under your chin. Kaede looking down as if judging a dropped coin.

"Your shoulders are wrong."

"Still?"

"Worse. Now you should know better."

You set the broom aside. Feet honest. Spine awake. Hands quiet.

She steps close, adjusts your sleeve, and leaves her hand there for one breath longer than necessary.

The courtyard is silent. The town wakes. This time, you do not fall.
