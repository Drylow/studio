# POV: You Fall in Love With a Spanish Woman

It's eleven at night in Seville, and the restaurant is only half full because, according to everyone around you, it's still early. You arrived at eight. You were the only person here. The waiter set your table with the pity of a man watching a tourist eat breakfast at midnight. Now the room is loud, warm and packed, and the woman at the next table has been watching you struggle with a plate of snails for ten minutes. "You're doing it wrong," she says finally, in English. "You're fighting it. Don't fight the snail." She leans over, takes your toothpick, and pulls one out in a single twist. Then she eats it herself. "Now you know," she says. And she goes back to her friends as if nothing happened.

## The wrong time for dinner
You moved to Seville three weeks ago to teach English at a language school near the river. You're twenty-nine. Back home, dinner is at six. Here, at six, people are still finishing lunch.

Nobody warned you. You learned it the hard way: the first week, you walked into restaurants at seven and found chairs stacked on tables and a cook smoking in the doorway. "Later," they'd say, waving a hand vaguely at the sky. "Much later."

In Spain, dinner is usually eaten around nine or ten at night. Some places don't get busy until eleven. Your stomach, raised on a different schedule, has been in open rebellion for three weeks.

So tonight, you waited. You arrived at eight, which you thought was late. You were wrong again. By the time the room filled up, you'd already eaten all the bread and two orders of olives.

The woman at the next table is still laughing about the snail with her friends. Then she turns back to you.

"First time in Seville?"

"Is it that obvious?"

"You arrived before the waiters," she says. "Yes. It's that obvious."

## Lucía Romero Vidal
Her name is Lucía. She tells you this while her friends pretend not to listen and listen to every word.

"Lucía Romero Vidal," she says.

"That's a lot of names."

"It's two surnames. Romero from my father, Vidal from my mother. Everyone in Spain has two."

You learn later that this is true. Spaniards traditionally carry two surnames, the father's first and the mother's second. And when a Spanish woman marries, she doesn't take her husband's name. She keeps her own. Both of them.

"So if you got married," you say, "you'd still be Lucía Romero Vidal?"

She raises an eyebrow. "Why? Are you proposing? You haven't finished your snails."

Her friends explode. One of them nearly falls off her chair. You turn the colour of the tomato on your plate.

Lucía is thirty. She restores old ceramic tiles, the painted blue-and-yellow tiles called azulejos that cover half the walls of Seville. She spends her days on scaffolding in churches and palaces, cleaning five hundred years of dirt off tiny painted birds.

"It's very slow," she says. "One tile can take a week."

"Do you like it?"

She thinks about it. "I like that the tiles don't talk."

## The tile
You don't get her number that night. She doesn't offer it, and you're too scared to ask.

But two days later, walking home through the old Santa Cruz neighbourhood, you see a scaffolding against the wall of a small chapel. And at the top, in a dusty grey work shirt, with her hair tied up and a tiny brush in her hand, is the woman who stole your snail.

"Lucía?"

She looks down. Recognises you. Sighs dramatically.

"The man who fights snails," she says. "Are you following me?"

"I live three streets away."

"That's what a man following me would say."

But she climbs down. She shows you the tile she's working on. It's the size of your hand. A painted blue bird with a broken wing, cracked straight down the middle.

"Somebody hit it with a ladder in 1960," she says. "Nobody fixed it. For sixty years, this bird has had a broken wing."

"Can you fix it?"

"I can make it whole," she says. "It will never be new. That's different."

She goes back up the scaffolding. Then, halfway up, she calls down: "There's a bar on the corner. They open at nine. Come at ten. Not before."

## Ten o'clock means ten-thirty
You arrive at ten exactly.

She arrives at ten forty.

"You said ten," you say.

"I said ten," she agrees, sitting down. "In Seville, ten means ten-thirty. Ten-forty is a little late, I admit. But it's fine."

"What does ten-thirty mean?"

"Eleven."

"And eleven?"

"Eleven means you should call to check if it's still happening."

You learn a whole new language that night, and it isn't Spanish. It's time. In Seville, time is a suggestion. Lunch is at two or three. The shops close in the afternoon and open again in the evening. The streets are empty at four in the afternoon and full at midnight. Grandmothers walk babies in strollers at eleven at night like it's perfectly normal.

Because here, it is.

You order wrong. She corrects you. You pay wrong. She corrects you again. In Seville, she explains, you don't split bills down to the cent. Someone pays this round. Someone else pays the next one. Nobody counts. Counting is rude.

"But how do you know it's even?"

"It's never even," she says. "That's the point. If it's even, the friendship is finished."

## The family lunch
Three months later, she takes you to lunch with her family.

Sunday lunch. It starts at two thirty. You're told it will be "a small thing". You arrive with a bottle of wine and a box of pastries, and you count twenty-two people in a small house in a village outside Seville.

There's her mother, Carmen, who kisses you on both cheeks and then holds your face like she's checking a melon. There's her father, Antonio, a retired bus driver who says nothing for the first hour and then tells you a forty-minute story about a goat. There are her two brothers, her grandmother, five cousins, three aunts, two uncles, a neighbour nobody invited, and a dog that belongs to no one.

The food never stops. Salmorejo, the thick cold tomato soup of Córdoba and Seville. Then fried fish. Then a rice dish. Then meat. Then more meat. Every time your plate is empty, a different aunt fills it.

At five o'clock, you lean toward Lucía. "When does it end?"

"End?" She looks at you with genuine confusion. "Why would it end?"

It ends at nine. You've been eating for six and a half hours. On the drive home, you can't feel your legs.

"They liked you," Lucía says.

"How can you tell?"

"My father told you the goat story. He only tells the goat story to people he likes."

## Living with her parents
You learn something that surprises you: Lucía still lives at home.

She's thirty. She has a good job. She restores tiles in palaces. And she sleeps in the same bedroom she had at twelve, under a poster of a band she now pretends she never liked.

"Is that normal here?" you ask carefully.

"Very normal."

It is. According to Eurostat, young people in Spain leave their parents' home at around thirty on average. Back home, people move out at eighteen, sometimes with a single suitcase and a lot of confidence.

"Rents in Seville are expensive," she says. "Salaries are not. And my mother cooks. Why would I leave?"

"For privacy?"

She laughs. "Privacy is an idea from cold countries."

You understand what she means later, when you visit her at home and her mother comes into the living room seven times in an hour. To bring olives. To ask if you're cold. To bring more olives. To ask if you want to stay for dinner. To tell you that dinner will be at ten. To bring olives again.

You're not cold. You've never been less cold in your life.

## The April Fair
In spring, Lucía takes you to the April Fair.

The Feria de Abril began in 1847 as a livestock fair, a place where farmers came to buy and sell horses and cattle. Today, it's a week-long party: a small city of striped tents called casetas, lanterns strung above sandy streets, horses with ribbons in their manes, and almost everybody dressed up.

The men wear short jackets and wide-brimmed hats. The women wear long flamenco dresses with ruffles, flowers in their hair and big earrings. Lucía's dress is red with white dots. Her grandmother made it forty years ago, and it's been altered for four different women in the family.

"You look incredible," you say.

"I know," she says. "Now, try to dance."

You can't dance. You try. You fail. A seven-year-old girl in a tiny flamenco dress takes pity on you and tries to teach you the steps. You fail again. The girl's grandmother shakes her head sadly.

At two in the morning, Lucía is still dancing. At four, the music is still going. At six, the sun is coming up over the tents, and you're sitting on a plastic chair eating fried dough dipped in hot chocolate.

"Do you do this every year?" you ask.

"Every year," she says. "Since I was born. Probably before."

## Learning the language
You decide to learn Spanish properly.

Your Spanish, until now, has been made of three phrases: "Hello," "Thank you," and "Sorry, I don't speak Spanish," which you say in such bad Spanish that people don't believe you.

Lucía refuses to teach you.

"I'm not your teacher," she says. "I'm your girlfriend. Teachers are patient."

So you take lessons. Evening classes, twice a week, with a retired professor who smokes in the classroom even though it's not allowed. You learn the verbs. You learn the genders. You learn that in Seville, people drop half their consonants and eat the end of every word, so the Spanish you learn in class sounds nothing like the Spanish you hear in the street.

Six months later, you try it on Lucía's father.

You tell him, slowly, carefully, that you enjoyed his goat story very much.

He stares at you. Then he gets up, leaves the room, and comes back with a bottle of something very old and very strong. He pours two glasses.

"Now," he says, in Spanish, "I tell you the story of the second goat."

## The proposal
You don't plan the proposal well.

You plan it for a fancy restaurant in Seville. You book a table for nine p.m. because you've learned something. You get there at nine. The restaurant is empty. You wait. She arrives at nine forty.

"Why such an early dinner?" she asks, sitting down. "Are you sick?"

It goes downhill from there. The waiter forgets your order. The ring box falls out of your jacket during dessert and rolls under the next table, where a very old man picks it up, opens it, looks at the ring, looks at you, and says loudly, "Is this for me?"

The whole restaurant turns around.

Lucía is crying with laughter. The old man hands the box to her instead of you.

"Well," she says, wiping her eyes. "Ask me, then."

So you do. In Spanish. Badly. You get one of the verbs wrong and accidentally ask her if she wants to marry your bicycle.

"Yes," she says anyway. "But I'm not changing my name."

"I know."

"And the bicycle sleeps outside."

## Planning a Spanish wedding
The wedding planning is a war, and you are not a general. You are a small country in the middle.

On one side, there's Lucía's mother, who has been planning this wedding since Lucía was born. On the other side, there's your mother, who has never been to Spain and thinks a wedding should last three hours.

"Three hours?" Carmen says, horrified, when Lucía translates. "Three hours is the appetiser."

In the end, the Spanish side wins. Of course they do. The guest list goes from forty to two hundred and twelve. Lucía's father invites the man who sells him newspapers. Her grandmother invites a priest she met at the bus stop. A cousin you've never met asks if he can bring his band.

"I don't know two hundred and twelve people," you say.

"You don't need to know them," Lucía says. "They know you. That's enough."

## The wedding
You get married in a small whitewashed church in her parents' village, under a ceiling painted blue with gold stars. The service is in Spanish. You understand about sixty percent. When the priest asks the important question, Lucía pinches your arm so you know when to answer.

The reception starts at nine at night.

There are long tables in a courtyard full of orange trees. There's ham, cut by hand by a man whose only job is to cut ham. There's paella cooked in a pan so big two men have to carry it. There's dancing, and then more dancing, and then the cousin's band, who turn out to be surprisingly good.

At one in the morning, your mother, who wanted a three-hour wedding, is dancing a sevillana with Lucía's father. Badly. Joyfully.

At three, the guests start a game where they cut your tie into small pieces and sell them to raise money for your honeymoon. It's a Spanish wedding tradition in some regions. Your tie raises four hundred euros. It was a cheap tie.

At six, you watch the sun rise over the orange trees with your wife's head on your shoulder.

"Lucía Romero Vidal," you say.

"Still," she says, and closes her eyes.

## A flat with a balcony
You move in together, finally, in a small flat in Triana, the old neighbourhood across the river from the centre of Seville. Fourth floor, no lift, one tiny balcony with a view of other people's balconies.

It's perfect.

The neighbours are a family of six on the left, a retired flamenco singer on the right, and above you, a man who plays the trumpet every afternoon at four, badly.

"We should complain," you say.

"We're in Spain," Lucía says. "Nobody complains. We just play louder music."

She puts the tiles from her old projects on your kitchen wall. Each one has a story. The broken bird from the chapel. A yellow flower from a palace. A blue fish from a tavern that closed in 1950.

At night, after dinner, which now happens at ten without you noticing, you sit on the tiny balcony and listen to the city. Televisions. Children. Dogs. Someone laughing. Someone arguing about football. Someone playing a guitar.

"Is it always this loud?" you ask.

"This is quiet," Lucía says. "Wait for the summer."

## The summer
The summer arrives like an oven door opening.

In July and August, Seville is one of the hottest cities in Europe. The thermometer on the pharmacy across the street shows forty-two degrees in the afternoon. The streets empty out. The shutters come down. The whole city disappears indoors and waits for the evening.

You finally understand the siesta. It's not laziness. It's survival.

From two to six, nobody moves. You lie on the cool tile floor of your flat, because it's the coldest place in the building. Lucía lies next to you. Neither of you speaks. The trumpet upstairs is silent. Even the dogs are silent.

At nine p.m., the city wakes up. Doors open. Children run into the streets. Old men set up plastic chairs on the pavement. The bars fill up. And somewhere around midnight, when it's finally down to thirty degrees, the whole neighbourhood eats dinner outside.

"I don't know how you live like this," you say.

"I don't know how you lived without it," she says.

## New Year's grapes
Your first New Year's Eve as a married couple, you spend at her parents' house, in front of the television, with twelve grapes each in a small bowl.

It's one of Spain's most famous traditions. At midnight, as the clock at Madrid's Puerta del Sol chimes twelve times on live television, you eat one grape for each chime. Twelve grapes, twelve chimes, twelve months of good luck.

It sounds easy. It isn't.

The chimes are fast. The grapes are big. And Lucía's family has bought grapes with seeds.

By the fourth chime, your cheeks are full. By the seventh, you're choking. By the tenth, Lucía's grandmother is laughing so hard she's crying, and you have grape juice running down your chin.

You manage nine. Lucía manages twelve, perfectly, and then kisses you with grape seeds still in her mouth.

"Nine months of good luck," her father says, patting your shoulder. "Not bad for a foreigner."

"What happens in the other three months?"

He shrugs. "We'll find out."

## The three months
You find out in March.

The language school where you teach closes. Not because of anything you did. The owner is retiring and nobody wants to buy the business. Twenty teachers lose their jobs on the same Monday.

You come home and sit on the balcony for a very long time.

Lucía finds you there. She doesn't say anything. She just brings two coffees and sits down next to you.

"I'll find something," you say.

"I know."

"It might take a while."

"I know," she says again. "In Spain, everything takes a while."

It does. Weeks go by. You send applications. You go to interviews in your careful Spanish. One school wants someone younger. Another wants someone cheaper. Another wants someone who's actually from Spain.

And then the money starts to run out.

## The family
You don't ask her family for help. You'd rather eat the balcony tiles.

But you don't have to ask.

One Sunday, after lunch, her father takes you aside. He doesn't make a speech. He just hands you an envelope. Inside, there's money and a piece of paper with a phone number.

"My friend," he says in Spanish. "He has a business. Tourists. He needs someone who speaks English and Spanish. You speak both. Badly. But both."

"I can't take the money."

"It's not money," he says. "It's family. Different thing."

You call the number the next morning. The friend runs walking tours of old Seville for tourists. He needs a guide who can explain the city in English. He offers you a job on the spot, on one condition.

"You have to learn the history," he says. "All of it. Every church. Every tile."

You know exactly who to ask.

## The tile tour
Lucía teaches you the city.

Not like a teacher. She still refuses to be a teacher. She teaches you the way she restores tiles: slowly, one piece at a time, with a lot of patience she pretends not to have.

She takes you to the Alcázar, the old royal palace, where the walls are covered in tiles so detailed your eyes hurt. She takes you to the cathedral, one of the biggest in the world. She takes you to the Plaza de España, a huge half-circle of brick built for a world fair in 1929, with a tiled alcove for every province of Spain.

"Every tile here was made by hand," she says. "Every one. And every one has been repaired at least once."

"By you?"

"Some of them."

Three weeks later, you lead your first tour. Twelve tourists. Two hours. You make mistakes. You mix up two kings. You send a group the wrong way down a one-way street.

But at the end, in front of the little chapel in Santa Cruz, you point up at a blue bird with a mended wing.

"This one," you tell them, "was broken for sixty years. My wife fixed it."

The tourists take photos. One of them cries a little. You get forty euros in tips.

## The trip to Madrid
In autumn, Lucía is offered a big job in Madrid.

A historic building near the centre is being restored, and they need someone who knows old tiles. It's six months. Good money. The kind of job that could change her career.

"Take it," you say immediately.

"It's six months."

"I know."

"In Madrid," she says, like Madrid is a foreign country. For people from Seville, it sometimes is. "They eat dinner at nine. Like animals."

You laugh. She doesn't. She's genuinely worried.

In the end, she takes it. You stay in Seville for your tours during the week and take the high-speed train to Madrid every Friday. It takes two and a half hours. You spend them preparing tours, eating sandwiches, and missing her.

In Madrid, she lives in a tiny rented room near the Plaza Mayor, with a window that looks onto a wall. She calls you every night at midnight, which is now her normal bedtime.

"The tiles are beautiful," she says. "The coffee is terrible. And nobody says hello in the elevator."

## Barcelona
For your second anniversary, you take a weekend in Barcelona.

You visit the Sagrada Família, Antoni Gaudí's enormous basilica. It's been under construction since 1882. Cranes are still standing over it as you walk around, looking up at towers that look like they're made of melted sand.

"Since 1882," you say. "That's more than a hundred and forty years."

"I know," Lucía says.

"How can anything take a hundred and forty years?"

She looks at the cranes, the towers, the workers on the scaffolding. Then she looks at you, and she smiles.

"Beautiful things take a long time," she says. "Ask any Spanish person how long dinner takes."

That night, you walk along the beach. The sea is dark. The city lights are bright. She takes off her shoes and walks in the water.

"I'm pregnant," she says.

You stop walking. She keeps going. A wave goes over her feet. She turns around.

"Are you going to say something," she says, "or are you going to fight it like a snail?"

## Two surnames
Your daughter is born in spring, in a hospital in Seville, at four in the morning.

Lucía's mother is in the waiting room. So is her father. So are both her brothers, her grandmother, three cousins, two aunts, the neighbour nobody invited, and the retired flamenco singer from next door, who brought a guitar and has been asked twice to stop playing it.

The baby has dark hair and a very loud voice. "She's Spanish," Lucía says weakly, holding her. "Listen to her. She's already complaining."

Then comes the question of the name.

"Two surnames," Lucía says. "Yours first, then mine. Or mine first, then yours. Since 2017, we can choose the order."

You look at the tiny girl. You think about it for a long time.

"Yours first," you say.

Lucía looks at you. "Are you sure?"

"Your family has been here for five hundred years," you say. "Mine has been here for three."

Her father, standing in the doorway, quietly wipes his eyes. Then he pretends it was the air conditioning.

## The grandmother
Lucía's grandmother dies the following winter, at ninety-three.

Spain has one of the highest life expectancies in the European Union, according to Eurostat. Her grandmother made full use of it. She outlived two husbands, a dictatorship, and four refrigerators. She danced at your wedding. She made Lucía's flamenco dress.

The funeral is in the village church with the painted stars. The whole village comes. People you've never seen bring food to the house for a week. Chairs appear from nowhere. Nobody is left alone for a single hour.

You sit in the courtyard with Lucía, under the orange trees.

"In your country," she asks quietly, "what do people do when someone dies?"

You think about it. "Mostly, we leave people alone. So they can be sad in private."

She looks at the courtyard full of aunts, neighbours, and pots of soup. "That sounds terrible," she says.

You look at the same courtyard. "Yeah," you say. "I think it is."

## The old dress
The following April, Lucía takes the old red dress with white dots out of a box.

It was her grandmother's. Then her mother's. Then her aunt's. Then hers. It's been altered so many times that the seams have seams.

She lays it on the bed and looks at it for a long time.

"For her," she says, nodding toward your daughter, who is busy trying to eat her own foot. "When she's older."

"It'll be a hundred years old by then."

"Then it will be a good dress," she says. "Good things last."

That week, the three of you go to the April Fair. Your daughter wears a tiny flamenco dress borrowed from a cousin's daughter. Lucía wears a new one. You wear the short jacket and the hat, which you now own.

You still can't dance. But you can carry a sleeping baby through a crowd at two in the morning while eating fried dough. In Seville, that counts for something.

## Back on the scaffolding
Lucía goes back to work when your daughter is one.

The first job is back at the little chapel in Santa Cruz. The bird with the mended wing has a neighbour now: a painted fish that cracked in the summer heat.

You bring your daughter to watch. She sits in a stroller at the bottom of the scaffolding, staring up at her mother on the platform, five metres above the street, with a tiny brush in her hand.

"Mamá," your daughter says. Her first clear word.

Lucía nearly drops the brush.

She climbs down faster than you've ever seen anyone climb a scaffolding. She picks your daughter up, kisses her, and then turns to you with an accusing look.

"Her first word is Spanish," she says.

"Of course it's Spanish."

"Good," she says. "Your Spanish is still terrible. She needed a better teacher."

"I thought you weren't a teacher."

"I'm not," she says. "I'm her mother. It's a different thing."

## Ten o'clock
Years pass in Seville, slowly, the way everything passes here.

Your tours become popular. You now explain the city in English and Spanish, with fewer mistakes and better jokes. Lucía runs a small workshop restoring tiles for churches and palaces all over Andalusia. Your daughter grows up speaking Spanish with a Seville accent, English with your accent, and complaining in both.

You eat dinner at ten. You don't notice anymore. When your mother visits and asks for dinner at six, you look at her the way the waiter looked at you on your first night.

You split bills the Spanish way. Nobody counts. It's never even.

Lucía's father has now told you eleven different goat stories. You suspect some of them are about the same goat.

## The snail
On your tenth anniversary, you go back to the restaurant where you met.

Same tables. Same loud room. Same waiter, older now, who recognises you and laughs. "The man who came at eight," he says.

You arrive at ten. Lucía arrives at ten forty. Some things never change.

And when the food comes, there's a plate of snails in the middle of the table. You pick up a toothpick. You twist. Perfectly. The snail comes out in one smooth motion.

Lucía watches. She claps slowly. Then she leans across the table, takes the toothpick right out of your hand, and eats your snail.

"Now you know," she says.

## The bird with the mended wing
It's late, after midnight, when you walk home through Santa Cruz.

The streets are warm. Somebody is playing a guitar behind a closed window. A grandmother pushes a stroller past you like it's the middle of the afternoon.

You stop in front of the little chapel. In the light of the street lamp, you can just see the blue bird with the mended wing, high on the wall.

"It doesn't look new," you say.

"It's not supposed to," Lucía says. "It's supposed to look whole."

She takes your hand. Her name is still Lucía Romero Vidal. It always will be. She never changed it.

She only changed everything else.
