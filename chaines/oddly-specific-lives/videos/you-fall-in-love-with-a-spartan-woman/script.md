# POV: You Fall in Love With a Spartan Woman

Damaris beats you to your worst pot. It slips off your cart on the road to Amyclae. She catches it, turns it over, and finds your thumbprint in the rim. You're about to apologize when she asks what it costs. She pays you now. An older woman calls, "Another farm worker collapsed." Damaris looks at her, then puts the pot back in your hands. "Bring it tomorrow," she says. You don't know why she wants that pot, or why she wants you to carry it.

## The pot full of figs
The next day, your worst pot sits on a farm table, full of figs.

Damaris lifts it with one damp hand. You've spent the walk from Amyclae preparing to explain the crooked rim, but she gets her fingers under it before you can begin.

"It's easy to grip when my hands are wet," she says.

She turns it slightly. Her thumb rests beside the mark yours left in the clay.

"You knew it was there?" you ask.

"I looked."

Outside, late-summer heat settles over the yard. It's 487 BC. You're twenty-six, newly arrived from Corinth, and your next sale has to cover a fresh load of clay. You would have sold this one cheaply. Damaris, who is twenty-three, paid your asking price.

She walks you past jars of oil and grain, pointing out which mouths need covers and which handles get used every day. She knows where each vessel belongs. When you ask about the two cracked storage jars beside the wall, she tips one toward the light and shows you a split near its base.

"Can you make these?" she asks.

"Yes."

The older woman from the road comes in carrying a basket. She tells you which turning leads back toward Sparta, then asks your price for the replacements. Before she agrees, she looks at Damaris. Damaris nods.

You wonder why everyone here waits for her. She has already moved on to the jars, measuring their width with her hands.

"Bring the new ones through the east gate," she says. "They go in the storehouse."

## A farm by the Eurotas
At sunrise, Damaris meets you at the east gate and decides where the new jars belong. You follow her past olive trees and freshly turned ground. The Eurotas catches the early light below the fields, and the Taygetus mountains stand beyond it. You look up long enough to miss a stone in the path.

"Watch your feet," she says.

She's carrying a tally of what the farm has stored, what it needs, and what the household must pay for. She stops to check a covered jar, calls to someone working beyond the trees, and changes your route because a cart needs the path. The older woman from the road brings her a question about oil. Damaris answers without opening the tally.

You try to move one of your jars into the storehouse. Its weight pulls against your wrist, and you catch it against your chest. Damaris laughs, then shows you how to get beneath it.

"Your hands are soft."

"They earn enough to keep me fed."

"Then you can use them here. I need lids made to fit these."

You agree too quickly. She sees it and looks pleased.

A woman named Thaleia comes in from the fields with the grain count Damaris has been waiting for. Dust marks the hem of her chiton. She corrects one number before Damaris writes it down. You ask whether she's paid by the day, as if this is a question about hiring help.

Thaleia looks at Damaris, then back at you.

"I live and work here," she says carefully.

Damaris calls her a helot. You hear that she is enslaved, though you still have questions you don't know how to ask. Thaleia gives Damaris the rest of the count and returns to the fields. You watch her lift a fallen sheaf out of the cart's path. Damaris checks the count, and you remember who corrects it.

## The road back
You keep bringing pots because Damaris keeps ordering them. The journey takes longer than the work should require. Sometimes you wait while she checks a lid against its jar. Sometimes she asks you to walk a field with her so you can see where a vessel will be used. You never suggest that she could have sent someone else.

One afternoon, you're heading back toward Sparta with a small jar wrapped in cloth when she passes you at a run.

"Why do you walk like you're carrying water in a cracked bowl?"

"Because you bought the last one I carried normally."

She slows, though she could leave you behind at any moment. Near the road, girls run across a cleared patch of earth. Two wrestle nearby. One lands hard enough to make you stop. She reaches up, and the other girl pulls her to her feet. Damaris is already past them. You hurry to keep her in sight.

She points to a fig tree ahead.

"Race me there."

"I have a jar."

"Keep it steady."

You run. The cloth knocks against your side, your sandals strike the hard road, and Damaris reaches the tree before you by enough time to turn and watch you finish. You bend over to breathe. She checks the knot around the jar and feels through the wrapping for damage.

"Better," she says.

"You weren't watching the race?"

"I saw that too."

She hands the jar back. For a moment, you expect her to name another vessel she needs. She looks instead at the road you just ran.

"Come tomorrow," she says. "You don't have to bring anything."

The next morning, you reach the riverbank before she does.

## The first lesson
The riverbank is hard under your sandals, and the sun hasn't reached the far side of the Eurotas. You have time to consider leaving before Damaris sees you. Then she comes down the path with her hair tied back and finds you studying the ground.

"Planning a route?"

"Looking for one that favors me."

She presses a toe against the packed earth. "Put your foot here. You'll waste less effort."

You try it. She moves your other foot.

"If I fall in, does the lesson end?"

"If you can reach the water."

She starts toward a bend in the river. You follow too quickly, because she can still speak and you would like to be able to do the same. Halfway there, your breath gives you away. Damaris laughs, slows, and points to a patch of pale stones.

"Make it that far. Save something for the way back."

You make it to the stones. On the return, you make it past them. You ask where she learned to judge a pace so well.

"By getting it wrong," she says.

That seems unlikely, though you keep the thought to yourself. She waits while you catch your breath and looks toward the road that leads to Sparta.

"Will Corinth call you home?"

"I still owe the kiln owner."

"And when you don't?"

You watch water work around a stone. You know what you'll do with the next payment and the one after it. Beyond those, your plans have always gone a little quiet.

"I don't know."

Damaris nods. She turns toward the bend again, and you expect to see her pull ahead. Instead, she stays beside you. Your sandals strike the bank almost together.

At the path, she asks how long it takes you to make one of her jars. You tell her to come see, if she has the time.

## Clay on her hands
Three weeks later, she comes to the work space you rent outside Sparta. The wheel sits near an open doorway, where the light is good and the floor can stand a little water. Damaris lifts a lump of clay as though she's checking its weight against something on the farm.

"Sit," you say.

She sits. You wet the wheel and set the clay in the center. It turns once beneath her hands before she presses down hard enough to push it sideways.

"That looked easier when you did it."

"You were watching the finished jars."

You bring her hands back to the clay. Your fingers rest over hers, and she follows the pressure you give them. When you take your hands away, the wall begins to rise. She watches it with the concentration she usually gives a store count.

"There," you say. "Less."

"I heard you."

She presses too hard again. The rim tilts. You reach for it, but she catches your wrist.

"Leave it."

"It's uneven."

"So was yours."

She works until the pot is small enough to fit between her palms. While it dries, she washes clay from her fingers in a basin and asks whether you ever send work home that you dislike.

"Sometimes."

"I hate doing that," she says. "There's enough work waiting when I get there. I don't want to bring back proof I couldn't manage this."

You look at the little pot. She puts it on a shelf herself and comes back when it's fired.

The next time you meet by the river, she brings figs in it. She offers you one before she asks why your sandals are dusty so early. You tell her you've been practicing the run on days she isn't there.

"I thought so," she says.

She eats a fig and turns the pot in her hands, careful around the crooked rim. You ask whether her family wonders where she's been going. She looks up at you.

"They've begun to ask."

## Her family's table
At the evening meal, Damaris sets bowls on the table with the same clay-marked hands you held steady at the wheel. Her brother notices the marks before he notices you. Barley bread and olives sit between you, and the last light reaches a jar beside the doorway.

"You make that one?" he asks.

"No. It was here when I arrived."

He looks at Damaris. She takes her seat and lets him look back at you.

"I hear you come from Corinth," he says. "What does a Corinthian potter bring to this farm?"

It's a fair question from a man who has spent the day working on it. You begin to answer, but Damaris names the price of the last two storage jars and what the previous seller asked for them. Her brother considers the difference. Then he waits.

You put down your bread.

"I'll pay what I owe in Corinth. Here, I'll do the work I promise."

"What work?"

"Jars. Repairs. Whatever I can learn to do properly."

He glances at your hands. "There's plenty to learn."

Damaris passes him the olives. The meal goes on. You can feel that he is watching how much of it you understand.

Afterward, you carry the bowls outside. Damaris joins you by the washing basin. She rinses one and hands it over.

"I won't ask them to choose for me," she says. "But their lives are here too."

"I know."

She gives you a look that asks how much. Before you can answer, her brother calls from the doorway.

"Does he know who harvests what he ate?"

Damaris holds the next bowl beneath the water. You think of Thaleia bringing in the farm's count, and of the care she took with her words when you mistook her for a hired worker. You hand Damaris the clean bowl.

At first light, you go back to the farm and ask Thaleia where the day's work begins.

## The short ceremony
By 485 BC, you no longer owe the kiln owner in Corinth. You tell Damaris at the farm table, where Thaleia is counting jars for the coming harvest.

"Twenty-three," you say.

"Twenty-two," Thaleia says. She points to the last mark on your tablet. You count again. She's right.

"Thank you."

"Correct the account, too."

You scrape away the mark and write the right number. Thaleia watches until you finish, then takes the tablet to Damaris. You're glad she speaks plainly to you. You also know she has to judge when it's safe to do that.

Outside, Damaris catches you by the doorway. Evening light reaches the olive trees, and there are two empty jars beside your feet.

"Do you intend to keep arriving as a guest?"

"I want a life with you."

She looks at you for a moment.

"You know my brother will ask about the jars."

"I'll answer him."

"He'll find something else."

You say, "I know."

Her brother does ask. He also helps clear a room for the family to gather. Damaris chooses her clothes herself: her hair cut short, a borrowed cloak across her shoulders, sandals that have already carried someone else through a long day. Her brother reaches to straighten the borrowed cloak. She does it herself.

There are a few people, a short meal, and her brother trying to look stern while he makes sure you both eat. Thaleia brings in bread, sees there's enough on the table, and goes back to her work. You want to call her to sit. You don't, because you know the invitation isn't yours to make freely.

When the last guest leaves, Damaris takes your hand. Her palm is warm against yours. Then she spots a jar left outside, lets go, and carries it in.

At first light, she wakes you to check the stores.

## Your first harvest
In 484 BC, breakfast begins beside a clay oil lamp. Damaris breaks bread while you turn a repaired jar toward the flame, searching for a thin line near its base.

"It'll hold," you say.

"Put water in it first."

You do. She's right to ask.

The wooden loom stands against the wall. Damaris works at it when she has time, which this morning means three passes of the shuttle before someone calls about the grain. She sets it down and goes to the storeroom. You follow with the tablet.

She measures what the household can use and what has to last through winter. You suggest opening the larger jar.

"That one's for seed."

You put the lid back. By now, you've learned to ask before you move anything heavier than a cup.

Your pots sell in Sparta and along the road to Amyclae. When the payment comes home, Damaris enters it on the tablet. She counts out what you owe for clay and oil, then ties the rest into a cloth purse. You ask for enough to repair a crack in the kiln wall. "I know about the crack," she says. "After the harvest." She puts the purse beside the grain account. You take the tablet back.

At harvest, you carry the midday meal to the edge of the field. The cut stalks scratch your calves. You and Damaris finish eating in the shade, but Thaleia and the other helot workers are still gathering grain beyond the olive trees.

"Why are they still out there?"

Damaris looks across the field. "Because we need it in before the weather turns. They can't decide to stop when we do."

You look at the bread in your hand. She takes the basket and brings the remaining food out to them. Thaleia accepts her portion, then points to a row that needs another pair of hands.

You go where she points. The work continues after sunset.

## The price of a jar
In 483 BC, a customer in Sparta turns one of your jars upside down and studies its base. The market is loud with bargaining, but he makes sure you hear him.

"Corinthian hands. Can they make anything that stays upright?"

You take the jar, set it on a flat stone, and let go. It stands.

He offers less than the price you asked. You take the jar back and wait while he looks at it again. Eventually he pays. At home, Damaris sees you rubbing the clay dust from your fingers.

"Was he difficult?"

"He wanted a cheaper jar."

"Did he get one?"

"No."

She smiles and returns to her account.

A few days later, a cracked storage vessel comes back to the farm. You hear a worker being blamed for dropping it. He's holding the two pieces together, trying to explain that he lifted it by the rim.

You know the mark across the clay. You saw it after firing and convinced yourself the vessel would hold. You step forward.

"He didn't break it. There's a flaw from my kiln."

The worker lowers the pieces. You give the farm another jar and take this one back. Damaris checks the replacement before anyone fills it, then crosses the loss out of the account.

"You did right," she says that evening.

"The worker was going to pay for it."

"Yes."

You wait, expecting more. Damaris closes the tablet.

"He needed you to speak before they'd believe him. Think about that, too."

Across the yard, Thaleia carries grain past the worker you spoke for. Both are helots, enslaved to work this farm. He goes back to lifting vessels. She fills the sound jars, one after another, until the storeroom door closes.

The next morning, Thaleia asks when you can replace a leaking vessel in the storeroom. She has already moved the grain into a sound one and needs the space back. You give her a day and keep your word. Three years later, news from the north reaches the farm at dusk. Damaris's brother trusts your repairs. He brings a loose shield strap.

## Her brother leaves
In 480 BC, Damaris's brother lays his round shield across the farm table. The leather strap underneath has pulled loose at one end. You hold it steady while he works a needle through the old holes. Outside, someone calls for a jar, and you recognize the sound of your own work being dragged across stone.

"Pull there," he says.

You pull. The leather holds.

Damaris comes in from the storeroom and tries the strap herself. Her brother waits while she turns the shield over.

"Check it again," she says.

"I just did."

"I know."

He takes it back. You go to fetch more cord.

At sunrise, you find them measuring grain together. Damaris tips each measure into a storage jar while her brother marks the count on a board. He asks how much the household can spare. She names an amount, then points to the jars set aside for sowing. He changes his mark.

You've watched them disagree over grain before. Today he listens closely enough to catch every number, and she makes him repeat the last one.

Two mornings later, he leaves on the road toward Sparta. Other hoplites are gathering there, with bronze helmets, red cloaks, and round shields that knock softly against their legs. Damaris walks out with him as far as the fig tree. She reaches beneath his shield once more and presses the repaired strap with her thumb.

"It's sound," he says.

"Keep it that way."

He smiles at her, then joins the others. They're going north to Thermopylae, where the Persian army is advancing. You stay beside Damaris until the last cloak is out of sight.

That afternoon, she counts the grain again. For the next several days, you both look up whenever feet sound on the road.

## News from Thermopylae
The news reaches the farm gate in the heat of the afternoon. A man from Sparta asks for water before he speaks. Damaris brings it herself, and he drinks while you stand close enough to hear.

The pass at Thermopylae has fallen. The man says Leonidas and 300 Spartans fought there alongside other Greeks against the Persian army. The man knows the fighting is over. He has heard names, but he won't give Damaris her brother's on the strength of what reached him along the road.

"Who told you?" she asks.

He names the person. She asks when he left Sparta, and the man answers. She thanks him for coming. After he goes, she carries the cup back inside and leaves it on the table.

At supper, her brother's place stays empty. You move a dish toward Damaris, and she passes it to Thaleia, who has come in to ask about tomorrow's grain. They settle which jar to open. Neither of them mentions the road.

Later, you find Damaris by the storeroom door.

"Do you want to tell me what he said?"

"You heard him."

You nod. She puts an account in your hands and asks you to check the amounts owed for three deliveries. You sit by the lamp and work through each one. A figure is missing. You go back to her with the correction, and she writes it in.

The next morning, she goes out to oversee the olive work. You follow with a jar that needs replacing. Thaleia tells you which one it is and where to set the new one. Damaris listens to her count, gives instructions for the next row, and takes the account back from you.

More people arrive from Sparta in the following days. None brings her brother home. At night, you see Damaris alone in the storeroom, counting the grain by lamplight.

## The long count
The lamp burns low while Damaris checks the stores. You hold it up so she can see the marks on the jars. She counts the grain once, rubs a thumb across the board, and counts it again.

"Enough for planting?" you ask.

"If the delivery comes."

You look toward the door. There's no sound from the road.

The next morning, Thaleia disputes an amount entered for the workers' share of olives. Damaris puts down her stylus and hears her out. Thaleia names the baskets carried in before dusk. You remember the last two because you moved them yourself. Damaris changes the account and asks Thaleia to check the new figure. Thaleia does.

Three days later, you bring back a delivery board from Sparta. You've marked one jar against the wrong household. The grain promised in exchange hasn't come, and Damaris has counted on it.

"I got the name wrong," you say.

She reads the board. "Can you go back today?"

You can. By the time you return, the last workers are coming in from the fields. Damaris sends you to put the grain away before dark.

Later in 480 BC, a traveler brings word to the farm of the Greek fleet's fight with the Persians at Salamis. You're far from the sea. They talk while work waits. But the news brings no word of her brother.

In 479 BC, a man back from Plataea brings word of Greek victory. He tells Damaris he saw her brother alive, helping carry the wounded after the battle. She makes him say her brother's name before she lets out a breath.

That evening, she finds you repairing a jar beside the lamp. She watches your hands for a moment.

"Will you stay because you want to?"

You set the jar down.

## A pot she can carry
"I want to," you say.

Damaris looks at the jar you've set beside the lamp. There's clay on your thumb and a smear of it on the table. She reaches for a cloth and leaves it there between you.

"Tell me again when you don't feel you have to say it tonight."

You nod. She goes to check the stores before bed, taking the lamp with her.

In 478 BC, a message comes from Corinth. The kiln owner has sold the workshop, and your old wheel is still there. The debt is paid, but you want to settle what belongs to you before someone else moves in. You tell Damaris you'll take the road north, collect it, and be back before the next firing. She asks how you'll bring it. You have an answer ready, though she improves it.

Corinth smells of hot clay when you arrive. You find the wheel under a cloth, its wooden head dry and sound. For two mornings you help the new owner clear the kiln yard. At night you sleep beside a stack of jars and miss the sound of Damaris counting grain aloud.

When you return to Laconia, she is in the yard with Thaleia, checking a delivery. Damaris sees you at the gate. She finishes the count before she comes over.

A few days later, you shape a pot small enough for one hand to carry. Around its belly, you paint a dark figure running beside a narrow line of water. The legs take more work than you'd expected. You leave your thumbprint in the rim.

You give it to her after supper. She turns it toward the lamp.

"Why am I running?"

"You always came back."

She looks at the little figure again. Her hand settles around the rim.

"Come running with me at dawn."

## The festival at Amyclae
In early summer, the whole valley walks to Amyclae for the Hyacinthia. It's the festival of the shrine near your workshop, and for three days Sparta, a city that seems to think rest is a weakness, actually rests.

Herodotus writes that in 479 BC, when Athenian envoys came begging for help against the Persians, the Spartans kept them waiting because they were busy celebrating the Hyacinthia. You understand why the moment you arrive. Nobody in Laconia hurries during it. Not even Damaris.

The first day is quiet, for mourning. The second day is loud. Boys sing. Girls in their best chitons ride in painted carts. There are races, and a long line of people carrying food to share with neighbours, guests, even the helots who work the farms.

You sell more pots in one afternoon than in the whole month before. A woman from Sparta buys four. A man from the coast buys your largest jar and asks if you can make him another with a horse on it. You say yes before you've thought about how to draw a horse.

Damaris watches you trade. She doesn't help. She doesn't need to. You've learned her way: name the price once, don't apologize for it, and don't talk while they decide.

"You didn't lower it," she says afterward.

"You'd have known."

"I would have."

That evening, you sit on the slope above the shrine while the singing goes on below. She has a fig in each hand and gives you one without looking. Somewhere down there, her brother's name is said aloud with the others the city remembers this year. She closes her eyes when she hears it. She doesn't cry. She just leans her shoulder against yours until the singing stops.

"He liked this festival," she says.

"Tell me about him."

So she does. Not much. A race he lost to her when they were ten. A cloak he tore climbing for figs. Three sentences, maybe four. From Damaris, that's a speech.

## The race at dawn
At dawn near Amyclae, Damaris opens the door with the hand that held the pot. She has it tucked under her other arm. You ask where she's taking it.

"To the fig tree."

She's already outside before you find your sandals. The air is cool, and the road still holds the night's damp in its ruts. She sets the pot beside a stone where neither of you will kick it. From there, the old fig tree stands at the end of a long, bare stretch.

You know the distance. You've lost it often enough.

Damaris starts before you can suggest a shorter run. Your sandals slap the hard ground behind hers, close enough that you hear the change in her steps when the road rises. She taught you to keep your feet under you here. You used to spend everything on the first stretch and reach the tree with barely enough breath to complain.

This morning, you wait. She stays level with you past the bend, her eyes on the road. At the shallow rise, she draws half a step ahead. You hear her breathing. You hear yours. Then you use the breath you've kept and catch her as the tree comes into view.

For several strides, her shoulder is beside yours. You reach the trunk just ahead of her and put out a hand to stop yourself. She reaches it a moment later. You both stand there breathing until you can speak.

"Did you let me win?"

"I didn't."

You look at her. She bends to brush dust from her sandal, and when she straightens, she's watching you.

"Run it again tomorrow."

"I'm staying."

"I know."

She walks back to the stone and picks up the painted pot, her hand closing around the thumbprint in its rim.
