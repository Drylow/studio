# Recherche et consignes — POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Gladiator

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS about her world (use several, rounded, each inside a scene, with the source named in the sentence; never invent other statistics):
- A marble relief from Halicarnassus (today Bodrum, Turkey), now in the British Museum and dated to the 1st-2nd century AD, shows two women fighters named Amazon and Achillia; its Greek inscription says they were released, and scholars read it as an honourable "missio", both women leaving the arena standing (British Museum; Encyclopaedia Romana, University of Chicago). They fight bareheaded with a greave, belt and loincloth, an arm guard (manica), a rectangular shield and a short blade; the historian Kathleen Coleman read the two round shapes beside them as their helmets, set down (Encyclopaedia Romana).
- Their names are stage names from myth: Amazon for the warrior women, Achillia for Achilles (British Museum relief, World History Encyclopedia).
- Tacitus wrote that in 63 AD, at Nero's games, "many ladies of distinction, and senators, disgraced themselves by appearing in the amphitheatre" (Tacitus, Annals 15.32).
- Suetonius wrote that Domitian gave gladiator shows at night by torchlight, "not only combats between men but between women as well" (Suetonius, Life of Domitian 4).
- Juvenal mocked a woman training as a fighter: everyone has seen "the wounds of the post" that she hollows out with wooden practice swords and her shield; he asks what modesty a woman in a helmet can have, and jokes that her husband is lucky if she ever auctions off her greaves (Juvenal, Satire 6, lines 246-258).
- An inscription from Ostia, Rome's port, praises a local magistrate named Hostilianus for being the first to put on women fighting with the sword (Ostia inscription, discussed by legal historian Antonio Guarino).
- Cassius Dio, a senator who lived through it, wrote that under Septimius Severus women competed "most fiercely" in a crowded athletic contest, jokes were made about other distinguished women, "therefore it was henceforth forbidden for any woman, no matter what her origin, to fight in single combat" (Cassius Dio, Roman History 76.16); historians date the ban to about 200 AD (Encyclopaedia Romana).
- A gladiator who earned his freedom received a wooden sword, the rudis: Martial describes the emperor Titus sending a wooden sword and a palm to two fighters, Priscus and Verus, who had fought to a standstill at the opening of the Colosseum in 80 AD, and both were freed (Martial, Book of Spectacles).
- Augustus forbade gladiator fights without the right to ask for mercy, and he made women watch the gladiators only from the upper seats (Suetonius, Life of Augustus 44-45).
- In Pompeii, fight announcements (edicta munerum) were painted on house walls by professional painters, some of whom signed: one notice promising twenty pairs of gladiators, a hunt and awnings against the sun is signed "Aemilius Celer wrote this alone, by the light of the moon" (Pompeii inscription CIL IV 3884; Encyclopaedia Romana). Notices named the sponsor, the occasion, the number of pairs, and extras like the awning (velarium) (Encyclopaedia Romana).
- Gladiators were nicknamed "hordearii", barley-eaters (Pliny the Elder, Natural History). A 2014 bone study of a gladiator cemetery at Ephesus (2nd-3rd century AD) by the Medical University of Vienna found they ate a mostly plant diet of barley and wheat, and high strontium levels suggest they drank a plant-ash tonic for their bones, the drink Pliny describes (PLOS ONE; Medical University of Vienna; Biblical Archaeology Society).
- Galen, the most famous doctor of the age, began as physician to the gladiators of the High Priest of Asia at Pergamon around 157 AD and learned to treat wounds and fractures there, before working in Rome (Galen's own writings; Royal Society of Biology).
- In 192 AD a fire at the Temple of Peace spread along the Via Sacra; Galen lost his books, instruments and medicines in a storeroom there and wrote that none of it distressed him; the letter was rediscovered in 2005 (Galen, On Avoiding Distress).
- Commodus fought in the arena himself as a secutor, proud of being left-handed, and took a million sesterces from the gladiators' fund for each day; Cassius Dio, sitting with the senators, chewed laurel leaves from his garland so the emperor would not see him laugh (Cassius Dio 73.19-21). Commodus was strangled in his bath by an athlete named Narcissus on the last day of 192 (Cassius Dio 73.22).
- 193, the Year of the Five Emperors: Pertinax was killed after 87 days; the Praetorian Guard then auctioned the empire and Didius Julianus won by bidding 25,000 sesterces per soldier; he lasted 66 days before the Senate named Septimius Severus emperor (Cassius Dio 74.10-17).
- A Senate decree of 177 AD on a bronze tablet found in Spain (Aes Italicense) capped gladiator prices by the size of the show: up to 15,000 sesterces for a top fighter in the richest games.
- A Senate decree of 19 AD, on a bronze tablet from Larinum, kept the families of senators and knights out of the arena and let arena performers be declared "infames", without honour (Barbara Levick, Journal of Roman Studies).
- The Ludus Magnus, the biggest gladiator school, stood next to the Colosseum, with a small practice arena ringed by cells (History Hit).
- Roman apartment blocks (insulae) were so tall that Augustus limited buildings on public streets to about 70 feet (Strabo; Britannica).
- Roman weddings: a written contract, a white tunic tied with the "knot of Hercules", an orange veil and orange shoes, a feast, a noisy evening procession to the new home, and June as the favourite month (PBS, The Roman Empire in the First Century); the bride said "Where you are Gaius, there I am Gaia", and was lifted over the threshold rather than stepping over it (Plutarch, Roman Questions 29-30).

STORY SHAPE: Rome, autumn 192 to about 202 AD. "You" (the narrator always says "you"; you have NO name in the story and there is NO other painter: you are the sign painter yourself) are 25, freeborn, a professional sign painter: you paint the fight announcements on the walls of the city at night, with a lamp, a ladder, a pot of red paint and a whitewash brush; careful, funny, proud of your letters, and you faint at the sight of blood. "Her" is FICTIONAL: Myrtale, 24, a freedwoman from Halicarnassus who signed on with a lanista for pay; her arena name is PENTHESILEA (the Amazon queen, a nod to Amazon and Achillia); dry, blunt, eats barley like it owes her money, never wastes a word. HOOK (0-15%): the emperor Commodus himself will fight at the games (the city talks of nothing else); you paint the announcement for her bout and spell her name PENTESILEA, without the H; the next morning a tall woman with a scar on her eyebrow is waiting under your wall with your own ladder: "There is an H. I fought for that H." You repaint it while she holds the ladder. COURTSHIP (15-45%): she brings you to the Ludus Magnus beside the Colosseum (cells, practice arena, the lanista with his tablets); she hits the wooden post with a wooden sword and you quote Juvenal's "wounds of the post" before realizing she has read Juvenal too; barley porridge and the ash drink in the mess; you close your eyes at every clash; Commodus dies (31 December 192, news only) and in 193 you paint one emperor's name after another on the same wall, Pertinax, the auctioned Julianus, then Severus: "the only man in Rome who profits from it". She tells you about the relief of Amazon and Achillia she saw as a child in Halicarnassus: two women who both walked out standing. That is her dream: not to win, to walk out standing. LOVE AND WEDDING (~45-50%): you ask; she wants a contract written down, "everything in writing, you of all people"; June 194; orange veil, the knot of Hercules (she ties it faster than anyone), the noisy procession through the Subura, she says "Where you are Gaius, there I am Gaia", and you try to lift her over the threshold; she lifts you instead. MARRIED LIFE AND DANGER (50-85%), mid-story scenes: (1) your third-floor room in an insula, noisy neighbours, the 70-foot rule and a building that leans; (2) the baths, then the market: she bargains like she fights; (3) you start painting her name bigger and the crowds start chanting it; her price with the lanista goes up, and you learn about the 177 AD price caps from him; (4) the sign-painting rivalry: another painter covers your notices with his; she settles it with one look; (5) a night show by torchlight, like in Domitian's day, you paint the next notice outside while you hear the crowd;(6) the big fight (~65%): a famous opponent from another school, both women end standing, missio, like the relief; she has a deep cut on the arm (never shown: bandage only) and you faint; (7) the doctor at the ludus, a Greek trained in Galen's way, sews her arm off screen, tells her about Galen's gladiators in Pergamon and how he lost his books in the fire of 192 and "was not distressed"; she uses the line on you all winter; (8) the knowledge that she is "infamis" in the eyes of the law and the neighbours' whispers, and you painting your own sign, "FELIX AND WIFE", proudly; (9) she teaches you to hold a shield and you teach her to paint letters; she has terrible handwriting. CRISIS (~80%): around 200 AD, after an athletic contest where women competed fiercely and the crowd mocked, Severus bans women from single combat, "no matter what her origin" (Cassius Dio); you have to paint the announcements of shows that no longer include her; the night you paint one, you leave the space blank. She is lost for weeks; she sits on the floor of the insula with her greave. ENDING (90-100%): the lanista frees her from the contract and, against custom, gives her a wooden sword, a rudis, "you earned it standing"; you open a small workshop together: you paint shop signs, she checks every letter, and she trains the merchants' sons to hold a stick and stand up straight. Last image: the old wall where it all began, the notice faded, with the H she made you add, painted bigger than all the other letters.

RULES: the narrator "you" (never named, never split into a second character), Myrtale, the lanista, the doctor, the opponent and all neighbours are fictional; real people (Commodus, Pertinax, Julianus, Severus, Galen, Cassius Dio, Juvenal, Tacitus, Suetonius, Martial, Aemilius Celer) only as findings, news, quotes or markers, never on screen as characters. Real facts only, with the source named in the sentence; the Antioch Olympics link to the ban is only a modern theory: do not state it as fact; the rudis given to a woman is our fiction (no source shows a woman receiving one): present it as a gesture of her lanista. NO gore: no blood close-ups, no open wounds, no stitching shown; deaths only implied, off screen (Commodus, Pertinax: news only); injuries non-graphic (a bandaged arm, a limp). Nothing sexual, no nudity: the real relief shows the women bare-chested, NEVER draw that; she always wears a tunic or subligaculum with a leather chest band, belt, manica on the sword arm, one greave, rectangular shield, helmet off so we see her face. She is an adult (24+). Correct late-2nd-century look: togas only for officials, tunics and cloaks for everyone, oil lamps, wax tablets, the Colosseum and the Ludus Magnus, insulae, no stirrups, no medieval armour, no Hollywood Spartacus costumes. Advertiser-friendly: calm, dry, funny, never mock her or the people of Rome, never glorify killing; the arena is shown as work, fear and skill.
