# POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Gladiator

You leave the H out of the name she paid for, and the woman beneath your ladder won't let you climb down. It's past midnight near the Ludus Magnus. You're painting a fight notice, thinking about your pay. The oil lamp keeps going out. She points at TALIA. "There's a letter missing." Her sponsor controls the notice. Her name above TALIA could vanish. She steadies the ladder, a wooden practice sword under her arm. She won't let go. "My mother taught me to write it," she says. You paint the H. She stays until it's dry.

## The second H
She releases your ladder, but her eyes catch on the larger name above you.

"The organizer still owes you," she says. "I'll tell him the delay was mine."

You climb down with red paint on your thumb and an apology you can't quite make. The little line says THALIA now. You had assumed it was another name for the fighter whose billing fills the wall. She tells you it's her mother's name. She paid to have it painted beneath her own.

Above it, in letters you were proud of an hour ago, stands PENTESILEA.

At dawn, she's waiting by the wall with your ladder when you come back for the lamp. She's found another missing letter.

"Again?" you ask.

"Look."

She holds the ladder while you put an H into PENTHESILEA. Her mother's name is down by your knees as you paint the one she fights under. She checks both when you finish.

It's autumn of 192. You're twenty-five, freeborn, and good enough with red letters to earn your rent. Blood makes you faint, which limits your interest in the games. Everyone else talks about Commodus appearing in the arena. Below your ladder, a man says Commodus fights left-handed as a secutor and takes money from the gladiators' fund.

Long before you, in Pompeii, a painter named Aemilius Celer signs a fight notice he paints alone by moonlight. You understand the hour. You still want your pay.

The next morning, you carry your ladder past the Ludus Magnus, the emperor's own school, to a smaller private school down the street. She calls you inside.

## Inside the school
You follow her into the private school, a street away from the Ludus Magnus and the Colosseum. A practice arena opens ahead of you, with small rooms around it. From the entrance, you can't tell which voice is hers. Wood cracks against wood. Someone laughs. You nearly walk past her.

She's twenty-four, from Halicarnassus, and busy fastening a manica along her sword arm. A leather band crosses her chest above a belted tunic. She wears one greave and carries a rectangular shield. Her helmet sits on a bench while she talks to you. Beside it lies a short wooden blade with a handle worn smooth.

A man with wax tablets stops to check a payment. He's the lanista who runs the school.

"Penthesilea," he says.

"Myrtale," she tells him. "We're indoors."

He finds her line on the tablet. She signed on for pay, he says, and she's free to go when her agreement ends. Myrtale chose her fighting name herself, after the Amazon queen. Outside, you needed two trips up a ladder to spell it.

Your notice is finished. The money is in your hand. You could leave before anyone gives you another chance to embarrass yourself.

"Can you paint a board for the practice yard?" she asks.

"Yes."

"With every letter?"

The lanista glances at the wooden blade. He says the board will need replacing often.

Myrtale takes up her shield and leads you to a post scarred from top to bottom. She puts on her helmet, raises the blade, and shows you why.

## The practice post
She strikes the post at the same height each time. The first few cuts look easy. After twenty, she has to loosen her grip between them. You paint the practice board nearby, listening to the blade land while you try to keep your lines straight.

"Juvenal jokes about a woman beating up a practice post with wooden swords and a shield," you say.

She finishes the thought before you can explain it.

"I've heard him," she says. "He doesn't say whether she hits the post."

Her next cut lands exactly where the last one did. You look down at your board. It was an easy joke to repeat when you were standing out of reach.

A shield cracks against a wooden blade behind you. Your eyes close before you can help it.

Myrtale sees. She doesn't ask you to pick up a sword. She points to the board instead.

"Hold that end."

You do. She goes back to the post, and the letters stop shaking beneath your brush.

A visitor comes through the yard and asks the lanista whether someone should read the board to her. Myrtale keeps her shield up.

"That word has too much room after the third letter," she says. "He's hiding where he scraped the paint."

You were. You turn the board, and there it is, a pale patch beneath the red. The visitor leaves you to fix it.

By the time her arm tires, you can tell which marks on the post are new. You still can't watch the blade every time it falls. She waits while you finish the word.

At the next meal, she makes room for you on the bench. You come over carrying a paint pot.

## Barley for dinner
The paint pot sits between you on the bench. Myrtale moves it away from the bread, then pushes a bowl toward you. Barley porridge. She eats hers with the concentration she gives the practice post, scraping each spoonful from the edge before she takes the next.

"You can have some."

You get halfway through saying you're fine when your stomach growls. She slides the bowl closer.

Someone down the bench calls the fighters hordearii, barley-eaters. You find this easier to understand with a warm spoon in your hand. There is bread too, and a cup she says the school makes with plant ash stirred into a drink. You taste it. You put the cup down carefully.

You scrape the last of the barley from the bowl. Myrtale tears the bread in two and hands you the bigger half.

Outside, a wooden blade cracks against a shield. You look down. She notices.

"Why do you come back if that sound bothers you?"

You turn your spoon in the bowl. "My letters look better when you check them."

"That's an expensive answer."

She gives you the last piece of bread anyway.

After dark, you walk with her toward the Subura. Notices cover the walls, fresh red letters laid across older names. At one corner, a strip of whitewash curls away, and you can read half a fighter's name underneath.

"Do you ever want yours taken down?"

Myrtale touches the edge of the paper, then keeps walking. You don't ask again.

On the last day of 192, news reaches the wall before you see her.

## Names over names
Someone says Commodus is dead, and the man beside you asks whether his name is still on the notice. A man at the wall tells you an athlete named Narcissus killed him. You look up at the red letters. They're dry. Nobody has paid you to cover them yet.

In January, you whitewash the wall and paint PERTINAX. The lime stings the cracks in your hands. By spring, someone comes to your door before breakfast with another bucket. The man with the bucket says the Praetorian Guard killed Pertinax after 87 days and sold the throne to Didius Julianus for 25,000 sesterces per soldier.

You paint DIDIUS JULIANUS over the patch where PERTINAX stood. The wall is getting thick enough to have opinions.

Myrtale finds you there with whitewash on your sleeve. She sets down the barley she's brought you and reads the new name.

"You're the only man in Rome who profits from this."

"You should see what I spend on lime."

She stays while you finish the last letter. Julianus lasts 66 days. When the next order comes, she helps you carry the bucket. You paint SEPTIMIUS SEVERUS as carefully as you painted the first two.

That summer, a sponsor orders a notice for a women's bout. You mark the space for Myrtale's arena name. He sees it and tells you to make the letters smaller. A senator has complained, he says.

Tacitus wrote that women of rank fought in the arena at Nero's games in 63. "They still bring up Nero's games," the sponsor says, looking over his shoulder, more than a century later.

You reach for the smaller brush. Myrtale takes the measure of the wall.

"Paint what he paid for," she says. "No larger yet."

You leave room around her name. Below it, three emperors lie under a coat of lime.

The next time she speaks of a fight, she begins with a stone she knew as a child.

## The stone at Halicarnassus
Above the Forum, the evening light catches the red paint beneath your nails. Myrtale notices you picking at it.

"Leave it," she says. "I want to tell you about a stone."

As a girl in Halicarnassus, she says, she used to stop before a relief of two women with shields and swords. One was called Amazon. The other was Achillia. Their names came from old stories. She remembers how deeply the letters were cut for the women who fought.

"I used to look at their feet," she says. "Both of them standing."

The inscription says both fighters are released from the bout. Myrtale traces two small shapes in the dust on the parapet with her finger.

"What did you think when you saw it?"

"That someone had to earn the stone."

Below you, a cart rattles across the Forum. She watches it pass, then looks back at you.

"I want to walk out standing," she says. "And afterward, I want my name to stay mine."

You think of her beneath your ladder at midnight, waiting for a letter in her mother's name to dry. You think of the one she makes you add to her own at dawn. Her chosen name has cost her work you can barely stand to watch.

On the way toward the Subura, you step off a curb without looking. Her hand closes around your paint-stained one. A cart rolls past close enough for you to smell the mule.

She lets go when you're safe. Your hand stays where she held it.

In spring 194, you ask her a question at your kitchen table.

## The contract and the wedding
It's a question you've worked up to through walks above the Forum, bowls shared at the school, and three more notices she checks before the paint dries.

"Will you marry me?"

She looks at your red fingers on the table. "Everything in writing. You of all people."

You think she means the answer is yes. She makes you wait while she reads the terms you bring her the next day. Your hands are still red, and hers are steady on the page.

"Yes," she says. "Now you can write that down."

June brings a small wedding and more relatives than your room can hold. She wears a white tunic, orange veil and matching shoes. She fastens the knot of Hercules at her waist so quickly that her aunt tells her to slow down. There is bread, wine, and enough noise at the feast to trouble the neighbors.

By evening, your guests escort you through the Subura, shouting well before they reach your stairwell. At your door, she looks at you and says the old words. "Where you are Gaius, there I am Gaia."

The custom says you lift the bride over the threshold. You bend toward her. She plants her feet, puts both arms around you, and lifts you instead.

She sets you down carefully. Someone in the stairwell cheers.

Later, when the guests leave and you can hear your own lamp burning, you ask, "Training tomorrow?"

"Of course."

You move her practice sword away from the door. On your first married morning, the people upstairs wake you before the practice bell does.

## The third-floor room
Your upstairs neighbor crosses his floor in sandals that announce every step. Beneath him, your third-floor room has one wall that seems to lean over the notices you leave there to dry.

The neighbor blames the building.

Each step shakes plaster dust onto your drying notices, ruining two. You move the rest beside the door, where Myrtale has to turn sideways to get in. You ask your neighbor to remove his sandals. He thinks it over, then stomps upstairs barefoot.

After the baths, you meet Myrtale at the market for a brush to repaint them. She examines barley, smells the lamp oil, and asks a brush seller why the handle on his best brush is already loose.

He names a price. You reach for your coins.

"Wait," she says.

You can face a seller without closing your eyes, which ought to count for something. It doesn't help. She gets the barley, the oil and a sound brush for less than you were prepared to pay for two of them.

At home, her greave goes under the bed. Your ladder rests against the wall beside the door, where neither of you can walk into it at night. She comes in from training, reads a fresh notice before she takes off her sandals, and taps one letter with her finger.

You correct it. Then you see her limp.

You reach for her arm. She steps back, and your hand hangs there.

"Water," she says. "And somewhere to sit."

You clear the stool and fill a cup.

"Ask me first next time."

"I will."

She drinks beside you as you paint the final letter. In the mornings she goes to the school and you take the ladder into the Subura. One afternoon, pleased with how the red paint looks across a new wall, you make PENTHESILEA larger than the sponsor ordered.

## The price of a name
You stand back to admire it. By the end of the week, people stop beneath the notice to sound out her name. A group of boys chant it on their way past the school.

The lanista calls you in and sets a wax tablet between you. Sponsors are offering more to book her now, he says. He points to the marks he has made beside each proposed appearance. One mark beside her next appearance is higher than the last. You read it twice.

He runs his nail beneath a mark. "The Senate set these limits in 177, cut in bronze. The bigger the games, the higher the ceiling. At the richest, a top fighter can cost fifteen thousand sesterces." The lanista closes his tablet before you can stare at his figures any longer.

Myrtale sees you smiling outside.

"Ask me before you change the size again," she says.

"I thought you'd like it."

"I might. Ask."

The next sponsor sends you to whitewash that same notice for his own. You tell him the first paint is barely dry. He tells you to get to work.

Myrtale comes from practice and stands beside your ladder. She says nothing until he asks why she is there.

"He loses the work," she says. "Pay him for it."

The sponsor looks at the wall, then pays. She steadies the ladder while you cover her large red letters.

That night, you paint outside an arena where torches burn over the entrance. Domitian once put women in the arena by torchlight. The flames catch on your wet paint. Myrtale is preparing inside while your brush moves across another wall.

A shout rises through the stone. You stop with her name half-painted.

Myrtale comes out with a cloth pressed above one eye. A shield rim caught her. Her next bout is booked. You paint her name again.

## Both standing
The woman from the other school arrives before Myrtale does. Her name is Sabina. She tests the sand with one foot and asks an official to move a loose board beside the arena entrance. He moves it. Your lanista checks the terms on his wax tablets twice. Sabina has made other fighters work for every step.

You find a place behind a pillar, where you can see half the arena if you lean. Myrtale comes out with her helmet on. She glances toward your pillar, or perhaps toward the wall behind it. You lean anyway.

The first strike lands on a shield. You keep your eyes open. Sabina advances behind hers, patient enough to make Myrtale circle into the light. Sand sticks to Myrtale's greave. An official calls for room. You catch a blade moving near an arm, and the pillar hides what follows.

There's a shout. You close your eyes for exactly one breath.

When you open them, both women are still moving. Myrtale's shield hangs lower. Sabina's breathing reaches you between the calls from the officials. Neither gives the other an easy opening. At last the fight is stopped, and each is granted missio. Each leaves on her feet.

You think of the stone Myrtale described in Halicarnassus, with Amazon and Achillia named beneath two standing fighters. For a moment, you can see why a child might remember it.

Then Myrtale takes off her helmet. Someone has wrapped her sword arm. She lowers her wrapped arm. Her mouth tightens before she can stop it. The pillar moves strangely, and the sand comes up to meet you.

You wake at the school infirmary. She's seated beside you.

"Did you see me standing?"

"Yes."

"Why are you lying down?"

You try to answer. The doctor speaks to her while you concentrate on the floor.

## Winter lessons
The doctor is a Greek named Demetrios. He trained in Galen's approach and speaks about rest as if Myrtale has ever listened to an order she disliked. You see clean cloth, a fresh bandage, and the arm she keeps trying to use. Galen began his work with gladiators at Pergamon around 157. Demetrios reminds her of this when she questions him.

"Galen had patients who listened," she says.

One cold evening, Demetrios tells her how Galen lost his books, instruments and medicines in the fire of 192 near the Temple of Peace. Galen wrote that the loss did not distress him.

"Not distressed," Myrtale says whenever you ask about her arm.

She says it when she can't lift the shield. She says it when the bandage comes off and you look at the floor.

By midwinter, she stops saying it.

"I'm frightened," she tells you.

"I know."

"You weren't supposed to."

Outside your room, a neighbor calls her infamis under his breath. Arena performers could be declared infames, stripped of standing in Roman law. The word reaches you halfway up the stairs. Myrtale hears it too. She grips the rail with her good hand and waits for you to come up beside her.

You paint a sign for the corner where you keep your brushes: THE PAINTER AND HIS WIFE.

"His wife?"

You look at the letters.

"I have a name," she says. "You could've asked for mine."

You wash the board and paint PAINTER AND MYRTALE. She holds it steady while it dries.

By spring, she can show you how to carry a shield without shutting your eyes. You hold it badly, but you hold it. At the table, you show her where the H belongs in PENTHESILEA. Her letters lean against one another. You tell her they're good enough. She takes the brush back.

For a few years, she still has mornings at the school. By about 200, what happens there is decided elsewhere.

## The blank space
News reaches the school after a crowded athletic contest. Women have competed fiercely, and distinguished women in attendance have been mocked. The lanista reads Severus's order from a tablet. No woman, whatever her origin, may fight in single combat. Myrtale asks him when he heard it. He tells her. She asks whether the next sponsor has been told.

He closes the tablet before he answers.

You still get orders for fight notices. One morning, you carry your ladder to a wall across from the school, and the organizer gives you a list of names. You've painted some of them before. Your brush makes the first red line, then the next. When you reach the place where Myrtale's name usually goes, you leave the whitewash bare.

By noon, the organizer comes to inspect the work.

"There's room for another name," he says.

"There was."

He looks at the list in your hand. "Finish it properly."

You paint the remaining names. By the time you carry the ladder home, the red has dried on your fingers.

For weeks, Myrtale sits on the floor of your room with her greave beside her. She oils its straps, sets it down, and picks it up again the next morning. Once, she takes her practice sword to the school and returns before the sun reaches your window.

"They'll let me use the yard," she says.

You wait for the rest. She sits beside the greave.

At the market, someone recognizes her and asks when she'll fight again. She tells him about the ban. He says at least she came out safely. She lets him go back to choosing onions. At home, she asks you to put the greave under the bed because she can't bear to trip over it.

You offer to paint PENTHESILEA on the workshop wall.

"What would it announce?"

Your brush is clean. You turn it in your hand, looking for an answer she can use.

The next morning, the lanista sends for her to settle the contract.

## The wooden sword
At the school, the lanista presses his seal into the wax tablet. Myrtale reads it before she touches the wooden sword on his desk. She asks whether there's another payment due. He shakes his head. The contract is finished. He pushes the sword toward her.

"You earned it standing."

As she reaches for it, you think of the poet Martial's Priscus and Verus, two gladiators given wooden swords and freedom after their bout ended in a draw. Myrtale's sword comes from the lanista, with no crowd to cheer. He watches her lift it, as though he knows it can't give her a place in the arena again.

For a while, it stays beside the greave under your bed. She goes to the yard when she can. One morning, she's still sitting with a cup of water when the light reaches your sandals. You suggest that people might pay to watch her train.

"I've had enough people watching."

You leave it there. She takes another week to decide what she wants to do before noon.

You rent a narrow workshop near the Subura. You paint signs for an oil seller and a man who repairs sandals. Myrtale checks your spelling while the paint is wet. When two merchants ask if she can teach their sons to hold a wooden stick properly, she asks what they can pay, then makes them bring the boys at dawn.

She teaches them where to put their feet. One mother waits outside the door each morning, watching her son through the gap. Myrtale notices and moves the lesson into the yard, where there's room for her too.

A boy finds PENTHESILEA on an old practice board.

"Does it have an H?"

Myrtale hands him the board and waits while he searches for it.

About 202, an order for a shop sign takes you both past the old wall near the Ludus Magnus. You bring the ladder because the buyer wants his lettering high enough to see from the street.

## The old wall
The buyer's shop is closed when you arrive. A neighbor says he'll be back before dark, which gives you time to set down the ladder. Across the street, the old fight notice shows through years of limewash. You recognize your red paint before you can read a word.

Myrtale crosses to it. The wall is rough beneath her fingers, worn where other notices have gone up and come down. Near the bottom, the small dedication she paid for is almost gone. You can make out THALIA if you stand close. Myrtale traces the space beside her mother's name without touching the paint.

Above it, PENTHESILEA has thinned to a few red strokes. She looks for the letters in order, quietly sounding them out. You remember the morning she brought your ladder back and made you climb it again. Your brush was nearly dry. She stood below until you got the H right.

You carry the ladder over. Myrtale sets its feet against the stones and tests the first rung with her hand. You climb far enough to see where fresh whitewash has missed a patch of the old notice. From here, the H is clear. It's larger than the letters around it. You had to reach across the ladder to paint its second upright, and she kept the ladder still.

"Do you want it repainted?" you ask.

"The H?"

"Your name."

She looks up at you. The shopkeeper calls from down the street, keys in his hand. Myrtale puts both hands on the ladder.

"Leave it. Come down first."

You climb down. She keeps her hands there until your feet reach the stones. Then you stand beside her, looking at the oversized H in her faded arena name.
