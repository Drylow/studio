# POV: You Marry a Japanese Woman

Haruka takes the cold medicine out of your hand like you're about to sign the wrong country. It's 11:40 at a convenience store beside Tennoji Station, bright enough to make being sick feel public. Your shirt sticks to your back, rent is due, and the receipt trembles. Haruka watches from the next aisle with natto and canned coffee. For now, she replaces the box and says, "Not that one." Then she walks out before you can say thank you.

## The receipt in your pocket
You buy the medicine she chose, because apparently survival now comes with customer service. Outside the store, Tennoji Station breathes late heat over the pavement. You are sweaty, feverish, and holding the receipt like evidence.

You are 31, American, teaching English at a small eikaiwa near Abeno, bad at Japanese in the particular way that keeps getting expensive. You still can't tell what any genkan in Kansai wants from you, which feels personal.

It takes 3 weeks to find her again, which is embarrassing. The whole investigation is one cold medicine box and wounded pride.

The clerk says she comes after late pharmacy shifts, buys unsweetened tea, and never blocks the aisle.

Then you see her outside the station mall, folded umbrella in one hand, checking a paper bag of prescriptions for an elderly woman who thanks her twice.

"Arigatou gozaimasu," you say, much too loud.

"Better," she says. "Quieter next time."

You nod like this was surgery.

"You were going to teach children while sleepy," she says. "Not ideal."

The receipt stays in your wallet, folded behind your train card.

## Dotonbori, after work
You meet her properly near Dotonbori on a Friday night, under the moving river light and signs shaped like crabs, octopus, and ambition. Both of you have finished late shifts. Osaka is loud in a way that makes silence feel chosen.

Her name is Haruka. She's 29, a pharmacist, precise with time, good at saying nothing, and organized enough to make your apartment look like a crime scene.

Over takoyaki near the canal, she tells you Japan's 2022 vital statistics, published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, put average first marriage at 31.1 for men and 29.7 for women.

"So," she says, "we are statistically punctual."

You eat the takoyaki too hot to look weak. She watches you suffer for exactly 3 seconds, then gives you tea.

She talks about elderly patients, careful dosages, and people nodding at instructions they didn't understand.

"My Japanese is terrible," you say.

"Yes." A beat. "But you listen."

Near Namba, she sings an old ballad perfectly, then checks the karaoke score like it owes her money. You lose badly.

"You have other qualities," she says.

Her hands stay steady as she turns your shoes the correct way. Then she gives you the next small rule, almost casually, and you don't understand why it matters until her mother is watching you.

## Rain in Nara
A rainy Sunday in Nara becomes the first real date that feels like a test you did not know you signed up for. You arrive at Nara station with the wrong umbrella, too small for two people and too cheap for weather with ambition.

In Nara Park, the deer bow for shika senbei crackers, then immediately become tiny polite criminals. Haruka demonstrates the bow with complete seriousness. You try it once and get surrounded by deer who understand weakness.

She laughs for the first time, quietly, one hand over her mouth. The deer, somehow, have negotiated better than you.

At Kasuga Taisha, along the lantern-lined paths, she shows you the purification basin. Left hand. Right hand. Mouth. Left hand again. Handle. She does not lecture. She just makes the correct thing visible.

You notice she doesn't fill silence to make you comfortable. She lets the rain speak.

In a small cafe near Naramachi, she says her parents live in Nara. Her father is traditional. Her mother hears everything. An aisatsu is not casual.

"If you meet them, you are not just visiting."

"I understand."

"No." She folds a napkin around your wet sleeve. "But you will try."

Then she adds the sentence you carry there.

"Don't perform. Listen."

## Aisatsu in Nara
You arrive at her parents' small house in Nara on a clear Saturday with a gift box from a department-store basement food floor, wrapped so beautifully you are afraid to touch it. At the genkan, you take off your shoes too early, half in and half out, achieving the body language of a confused flamingo.

Haruka's mother rescues you by turning your shoes neatly outward without comment. This is worse than correction.

You kneel on tatami. Your legs begin to die in stages.

Her father says almost nothing for an hour. He asks about your job, your family, your visa, and whether you can eat simmered vegetables. Haruka does not rescue the silence. Her family uses quiet as a room everyone sits inside together.

Dinner is miso soup, grilled fish, rice, pickles, and small dishes placed with the kind of order your life has never achieved. You try to compliment the food and sound like a textbook that became self-aware.

Eventually, her father pours you sake. The air changes.

"Take care of her," he says.

"I will."

"Learn Japanese."

Her mother asks if you are cold and brings a cushion. This is her verdict, delivered through fabric.

On the train back to Osaka, Haruka looks at the dark window and says, "I was nervous."

It is the first private weakness she gives you.

## The form on the table
Months pass in small Osaka pieces. Convenience-store dinners after late shifts. Laundry stiffening on your tiny balcony near Showacho. Missed trains at Tennoji because you trust the wrong platform, and Haruka checks the timetable, then waits for you to understand what it already told you.

Your Japanese improves from dangerous to merely suspicious.

You propose on a Tuesday night while she is sorting recycling correctly, because plastic bottles, caps, and labels apparently have separate destinies. She is kneeling by the bags, hair clipped back, checking your work with the calm of a customs officer.

You ask in rough Japanese, bad enough to make your ears burn, and she understands you.

Haruka looks at you for exactly three seconds.

"Ask again slower."

You do.

She says yes.

Then she brings out the kon'in todoke, the marriage registration form, and places it on the table like it's more sacred than the ring. In Japan, she explains, the marriage becomes legal when you submit this form at the city or ward office, signed by two adult witnesses. The ceremony itself has no legal value.

Her parents sign carefully. Your American friend from work signs after asking three times where his signature goes.

Haruka checks every box, every character, every date.

This is romance, apparently.

You notice your address is in the wrong square, and Haruka closes her eyes for one patient second.

Haruka taps the document with one finger. "The ward office makes it official," she says.

You look at the paper and understand that this is the part that makes it real, and that every box on it is suddenly more serious than you are ready for.

## Ten minutes at the ward office
The legal marriage happens at an Osaka ward office on a weekday morning, under fluorescent lights, with a clerk, a stamp, and a vending machine humming near the wall.

You wear a jacket too formal for the room. Haruka wears a simple dress and carries a folder with backup copies, because she knows you.

The clerk reviews the kon'in todoke. Haruka slides the folder across the counter and squares its corners with one finger.

Then the clerk stamps it.

The legal change happens almost invisibly.

The only sound is the copier.

Haruka looks at you.

"That was it."

"We're married?"

"Yes," she says. "We can be dramatic after lunch."

Over lunch, she explains her surname with the same care she once used on cold medicine. Because she is a Japanese citizen marrying a foreigner, Japan's shared-surname rule does not automatically apply to you both. She keeps her own surname unless she files to change it within six months.

Then she tells you about Article 750. For two Japanese nationals, Japan's Civil Code requires one shared surname. Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare figures put it at about 95% of marriages: the wife gives up hers.

"I am keeping my name," she says, and watches your face to see if you understand.

"It's yours," you say.

She studies you, then gives the smallest nod.

It feels larger than applause.

## Sumiyoshi Taisha, then Kyoto
Everyone admires the ceremony, even though the law already happened beside a copier.

At Sumiyoshi Taisha, the old bridge rises red and steep, and Haruka's mother keeps checking the sky like weather can be negotiated through concern. Later, in Kyoto, the shrine ceremony moves with a formal rhythm that makes you stand straighter without being told.

Haruka's white wedding kimono is handled by attendants with careful hands. You feel like furniture rented for historical accuracy.

During san-san-kudo, you share sake in three sips from each of three cups. Your cup trembles while Haruka keeps hers steady.

Until the final sip.

There is one tiny shake only you can see.

Guests bring goshugi, cash in decorated envelopes. Friends commonly give 30,000 yen in brand-new notes, and odd amounts are preferred because even sums can be split. You learn this after almost putting the wrong bills in an envelope.

Because of course you do.

Her friends give speeches that are funny without being cruel. One says Haruka used to correct everyone's medicine labels at university, which makes you feel chosen by destiny and over-the-counter packaging.

Her father says little again.

Then he says your Japanese has improved.

This is basically fireworks.

Haruka's mother cries only after turning away. At the reception, before dinner is even over, Haruka secretly checks the karaoke machine's song list.

## The allowance meeting
Married life starts in a small Osaka apartment with two toothbrushes, one shoehorn by the genkan, and Haruka labeling shelves in handwriting more stable than your plans.

She takes over the household budget because she is better at it, and because you once bought three kinds of soy sauce thinking one was vinegar.

In many Japanese homes, the wife manages the family budget and the husband gets a monthly allowance, okozukai. Haruka explains this like weather. SBI Shinsei Bank's yearly survey puts the average office worker allowance around 40,000 yen a month.

She gives you yours.

"Is there an appeals process?"

"Yes. Improve your evidence."

Forty thousand yen sounds like freedom until it has to cover train fares, coffee, and the pastry you buy after work for emotional support.

You stop arguing once you realize she has receipts, categories, and better math.

The genkan becomes a ritual. Shoes lined outward. Her pair neat. Yours corrected by invisible forces.

She keeps her surname past the six-month window, and nobody explodes.

You learn marriage is knowing which trash goes out on which morning, which feels less romantic until you forget and see her face.

## First New Year
Your first New Year with her family in Nara brings everything back. Shoes at the genkan. Careful hands. Labels on food you still need help reading.

At midnight, you walk with them to a neighborhood shrine for hatsumode. The cold makes everyone's breath visible. Haruka claps and bows. You copy half a second late, which is your spiritual brand.

Back at the house, lacquered osechi boxes open in layers, each compartment arranged like someone negotiated peace with the universe. You reach randomly. Her mother redirects your chopsticks before international damage occurs.

Her father gives you sake without a speech. Haruka looks softer here, half daughter, half wife, still herself.

Valentine's Day arrives later. In Japan, women commonly give chocolate to men. Haruka gives you good chocolate and says, "This is not lunch."

White Day is March 14, when men give something back.

You forget once.

The silence is polite, breathable, and lethal.

You buy a gift the next morning.

"Calendar app," she says.

You add every important date twice.

## The hospital room
The health scare comes on a gray afternoon. You take the train from Osaka to Nara with Haruka, holding the bag her mother packed: towels, documents, tea. Even fear arrives with towels, documents, and tea.

At the hospital, her family becomes quiet. Not cold. Quiet.

Your American instinct is to fill the space, ask questions, offer plans, become useful by making noise. Haruka touches your sleeve once. That is the whole instruction.

So you sit. You bow when needed. You fetch tea. You do not make the room about your discomfort.

Her father's hand looks smaller around the paper cup.

He wakes, recognizes you, and says, "Shoes?"

You look down, panicked. Your shoes are correctly placed at the room entrance.

He nods.

Later, Haruka cries in a stairwell, one hand over her mouth like the first laugh in Nara.

You stand beside her.

"Thank you for being quiet," she says.

"I learned," you say.

## The third pair
Her father complains about hospital food again. Everyone treats this as excellent news. Your Japanese stays imperfect, but now it has family words: okaasan, otousan, tadaima, okaeri.

Systems continue. Allowance. Genkan. Labels. Calendar alarms.

One late night, you're in a convenience store again, holding a medicine box you can read well enough to know it's still probably wrong.

Haruka takes it from your hand and replaces it.

"Almost," she says.

"Progress."

"Slowly."

At home, rain taps the balcony rail, and the genkan light is warm. Two pairs of shoes wait there, hers neat, yours slightly crooked.

Haruka stands beside you, one hand near a small shopping bag.

"Too early?" you ask.

"For you, yes," she says. Then, "For us, no."

Beside yours, a tiny third pair faces outward correctly.
