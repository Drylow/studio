# POV: You Marry a Female Hacker

At 6:05 a.m., she tells you your card reader is a crime scene. You're opening a bakery on Southeast Division in Portland, where rain slicks the windows. She's your quiet regular, with silver tape over her laptop camera. You're thinking about rent. Then the screen flashes approved and she doesn't move. She looks at the reader, then at the router under the counter. She says, "Who set this up for you?" You say, "Me." Her face does something small and merciful. "That's unfortunate." She leaves a napkin: Change your password. You tell yourself she's careful. You wait for her.

## The next morning, 6:05
Her knowing your password is bad would be embarrassing enough. Then you realize she can probably guess the exact one.

She returns at exactly 6:05, black coffee, cardamom bun, exact cash, the same crooked square of silver tape over her laptop camera. She sits facing the door, never with her back to the front window, even though the only threat in the room is a tray of overproofed brioche.

She looks at the tub on your shelf.

"Does your starter have a name?"

"Herbert."

She closes her eyes for exactly 2 seconds.

"Please tell me Herbert is not guarding your bank account."

You say nothing, and she takes that as your answer.

She doesn't laugh. She asks before stepping behind the counter. Then she calls the vendor, changes settings you should have changed, and labels the router with tape that says do not touch.

She still pays for her bun.

When you ask what to put on the loyalty card, she says, "Juno. No last name on paper."

## Three weeks of exact cash
For 3 weeks, Juno becomes part of the bakery's morning weather. Rain blurs the glass, and espresso steam hangs over Juno at the corner table, facing the door.

She never posts photos. She never lets anyone photograph her. When tourists lift phones near her table, she turns away with the mild patience of someone avoiding a sneeze.

She reads every terms-and-conditions page before joining your loyalty program, then refuses the loyalty program.

You tell her your flip phone can't be hacked because it barely works.

"That's not security," she says. "That's a cry for help."

You start saving her one cardamom bun.

She notices.

"That is sweet and operationally predictable."

One rainy morning, she reads ISC2's 2024 workforce report while eating. The ISC2 report says women make up about 22 percent of security teams, and about 26 percent among workers under 30.

She says it calmly. Still, you hear the tired part.

"What do you do?" you ask.

"I test systems for clients who would rather hear the bad news from me."

## The first dinner
You invite her to dinner above the bakery, in the tiny apartment that smells permanently like butter and toasted flour.

You cook badly, but sincerely. Roasted vegetables. Too much garlic. Bread you made yourself, because that is the one arena where you are not helpless.

She arrives with flowers from a shop on Hawthorne and a small flashlight on her keychain. At the door, your smart speaker chirps. She unplugs it without thinking.

"Was that necessary?"

"For my blood pressure, yes."

She sees your paper order book on the table and looks almost touched, like you've accidentally invented privacy by being stubborn.

Halfway through dinner, she sets down her fork.

"I looked you up."

You go still.

"Not deeply," she says. "Bakery license. Old local newspaper mention. Your grandmother's name."

It feels like being seen through a window you didn't know was open.

She sees that, too, almost immediately.

"I should have asked," she says. "Curiosity isn't consent."

After that, she keeps her hands folded on the table, as if touching anything else would need permission.

You forgive her too quickly. Then you make yourself say it.

"Don't do that again."

"I won't."

The relief on her face is brief, but you can see it.

## The counter at 2 a.m.
The old mixer fails during prep, so you're awake at 2 a.m., arms dusted with flour, when Juno knocks on the locked glass door.

She looks worse than you've ever seen her. No coat. Wet hair. Eyes too awake for that hour.

"Can I come in?"

The permission matters now.

You unlock the door. She sits under the yellow prep lights, hands around coffee she doesn't drink.

"White hat hackers work with permission," she says. "Black hat hackers work without it. Grey hat is the space between."

Her eyes move from the router to the rain on the glass.

"I lived there longer than I should have."

She doesn't describe tools, crimes, or clever little disasters. She says she once helped expose a crew called The Night Orchard, people who hurt ordinary people and hid behind pretty names.

"They didn't forgive me," she says. "They never had my real name. Now someone is trying to find it."

Her handle was TILDEWREN. TILDEWREN has a reputation in that world. Juno Park stays hidden behind it.

Now companies and governments pay her to test their systems before criminals find the holes. Penetration testing. Bug bounties. Permission forms thick enough to stop a door.

"The form is what makes the work legal," she says. "Without it, I'm crossing a line."

She tells you she should stop coming to the bakery.

You hear yourself say, "Don't."

And for the first time, staying seems to scare her.

## The first touch
She does not disappear after that night. That is the first answer.

She still comes for the cardamom bun, but now she sometimes stays until the breakfast rush begins and your bakery fills with wet coats and impatient shoes.

After closing, you walk through Laurelhurst Park under fir trees heavy with rain, streetlights blurred like someone rubbed them with a thumb.

"I sleep four hours on good nights," she says. "Less when work gets loud."

You tell her bread is mostly waiting.

"Security too," she says. "Mostly admitting something will fail."

She meets your grandmother at dinner. Your grandmother calls her "the computer girl." Juno accepts this with grave dignity.

Later, a phone scammer targets your grandmother. The calls stop. Your grandmother gets tea and a calm explanation.

You begin to understand when she tapes a note beside your grandmother's phone: hang up, call me, breathe.

Outside a bookstore on Burnside, a stranger's camera turns too close. Juno steps nearer. Her fingers brush your flour-cracked hand.

"Is this okay?"

"Yes."

Only then does she hold it.

## The proposal with no Wi-Fi
Three months in, she starts leaving small protections around your life. She asks first, then fixes only the things you hand her.

She changes the bakery systems only after asking. You still write orders on paper, in a coffee-stained book with one page sealed by honey. She respects this more than you expect.

She changes your password away from Herbert and looks personally betrayed that the conversation is necessary.

"Naming your sourdough starter is charming," she says. "Entrusting your entire adult identity to him is where charm files for unemployment."

She gives you a small notebook with rules in plain language. The first one says, ask before touching.

You start thinking about marriage because she already fits into the life you are building, including all its locked doors.

You propose in a rented cabin near Manzanita, where the signal dies behind wet trees and the ocean is louder than any notification. She notices the lack of internet before she notices the ring.

The box is tied into a towel with bread wire because you are nervous and not graceful.

"Did you choose this place because my phone can't breathe here?"

"Maybe."

She laughs without checking the room.

She says yes, then asks if you told anyone online.

"No."

"Good. Romantic and finally trainable."

Later, with her head against your shoulder, she says she wants girls to learn this world without having to become hard first.

You have until August before the strangest wedding guest list in Nevada becomes real.

## DEF CON week in Las Vegas
DEF CON started in Las Vegas in 1993, and it usually lands in August.

So that is when you get married.

You get the marriage license through the Clark County Clerk system, then walk into a small chapel where the air conditioning sounds like a tired jet engine.

Her guests arrive in hoodies, black T-shirts, sneakers, and social awkwardness polished into a lifestyle. They introduce themselves as MOTHNODE, AuntieRoot, Soft404, Kettle, and one man who says his name is "temporary."

The officiant looks confused but professional. Las Vegas has seen worse by lunch.

Juno wears a cream dress with pockets. In one is a folded printout of the vows. In the other is a phone she swears is off.

Someone jokes about bugs, and Juno tells you about the 1947 Harvard Mark II moth taped into the logbook, now held by the Smithsonian.

"See?" she says. "Romance."

You marry her under neon, among people who know the name she used online and only pieces of the woman beside you.

When the officiant says Juno Park aloud, a few friends go still.

She does not flinch.

Explaining to a chapel officiant that half your guests are legally employed ghosts is not ideal.

## The offline honeymoon
You drive back to Oregon and spend the honeymoon in a cabin near Cape Lookout, close enough to hear the Pacific throwing itself at the dark.

The cabin has no reliable internet, no smart speaker, no smart fridge, and one television that seems to predate several presidents.

She is the happiest you have ever seen her.

She sleeps 9 hours the first night and wakes up offended by her own biology.

You make coffee badly on a dented stove. She drinks it anyway, which you decide is either love or evidence of a serious character flaw.

You walk the beach in mist, past driftwood, gulls, and families in rain jackets pretending August on the Oregon coast is warm.

She tells you about growing up Korean-American in Beaverton, with parents who wanted safety, grades, respectable work, and a daughter who found locked doors more interesting than open ones.

"My mom understands the legal job now," she says. "She just wishes I would hack less dramatically."

You ask if she misses the old grey-hat life.

"I miss feeling certain I was the smartest person in the room," she says. "I don't miss what that certainty made me do."

On the last night, she tapes the cabin laptop camera.

You hand her the tape.

Then the signal comes back.

## Married life above the bakery
Back in Portland, married life becomes a series of negotiations that sound ridiculous until they are yours.

The smart fridge you bought on sale is banned before it crosses the apartment door.

"It has a microphone," she says.

"It keeps lettuce cold," you say.

"It can do that in exile."

The old refrigerator stays because it hums, leaks slightly, and has never asked to connect to anything. Your flip phone survives too, partly because she respects its uselessness, and partly because you have feelings now, which is inconvenient.

The bakery keeps its paper order book. Juno calls it your analog kingdom.

She still works for a bank, a hospital network, a phone maker, a state agency. She never explains tools or methods. You see results. Executives go pale on calls. Thank-you flowers arrive from people who sound afraid of gratitude. Long invoices get paid on time.

Very serious people pay your wife to ruin a meeting early, before the real criminals get a chance.

She sleeps more because breakfast becomes non-negotiable. You stop asking what she found.

You ask if she ate.

Some days she fixes your life without asking. Some days you forgive badly. You ask fewer questions about the locks, and you notice when she lets one stay open.

Then someone tries to connect Juno Park to TILDEWREN online, and suddenly every lock in the apartment has a reason.

## The week she disappears into screens
One Tuesday in November, she comes home and does not take off her coat.

Someone is trying to expose her online, and the damage is all names, addresses, and searchable links. Juno Park, the bakery, her parents in Beaverton, TILDEWREN, all tied into one searchable wound.

"Stay offline about me," she says.

You almost joke that you are barely online at all. Her face stops you.

For a week, she disappears into screens. She sleeps on the couch for 90 minutes at a time. The bakery smells like burnt coffee because you forget the pot.

Her friends with handles reappear as exhausted professionals with lawyers, boundaries, and day jobs.

She refuses to do anything illegal back.

"I don't get to become worse because I'm scared," she says.

So she goes clean, with lawyers, permissions, reports, and more patience than either of you has.

The attempt fails off screen, which feels unfairly quiet.

Afterward, she sits on the bakery floor behind the counter, back against flour sacks, hands shaking, the fear reaching her only now.

You sit beside her.

"You're here," you say.

"I stayed legal."

"I know."

## The Saturday class
After that, she starts a free Saturday class in the back of the bakery.

Six girls come first. Two parents stay. Your grandmother sits up front pretending not to supervise, which fools nobody. There are cardamom buns.

Juno teaches consent, permission, privacy, and asking who owns a system before touching it, without tricks or shortcuts.

She opens with the Harvard Mark II moth, the relay, the logbook page now at the Smithsonian.

"Bugs should feel historical and slightly ridiculous," she says.

Then she mentions ISC2's 2024 research, that women are still a minority on security teams, around 22 percent.

She gives each girl a notebook.

"Write down what you have permission to test. Start there."

At 6:05 the next morning, rain slicks Southeast Division. Juno tapes her laptop camera.

You pull out silver tape.

"Really?"

"I'm trainable."

She smiles into her coffee.

Two taped cameras. Two steaming cups. Her hand finds yours beside the register as the bakery wakes up.
