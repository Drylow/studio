# POV: You Marry a Singaporean Woman

You give away her seat before she comes back with your first-date lunch. It's noon at Maxwell Food Centre. Your posting ends in June. She's left a packet of tissues on the table while she queues for chicken rice. You lift it; a man asks for the chair. You agree. She returns with two plates. She looks at him, then at the packet in your hand. "That was our table," she says. He moves. She puts one plate in front of you. "Next time, leave it there." You put it down carefully.

## The rest of lunch
She sits down across from you. "Do you always give away a seat while its owner buys you lunch?" You look at the chicken rice, then at her. She's opening her chopsticks. Apparently, the date is still happening.

"I thought you'd left it behind."

"I left it to hold the table."

She points at the packet beside your hand. People here call it chope, she says. Tissues reserve a seat while you queue. You glance at the little plastic packet, which has done a better job of getting a table than you have. Later you'll learn that in December 2020, UNESCO added Singapore's hawker culture to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage. Right now, all you know is that you just gave a piece of it away.

"Right," you say.

"Don't worry. The noodles looked heavy."

You tell her the man was kind about moving. She says most people are, if you give them a chance. Around you, trays knock against tabletops and a fan turns above a queue that doesn't seem to get shorter. Your shirt sticks to your back. You take a bite while the rice is still warm.

She asks about your job. You tell her you're fixing software for a company near Tanjong Pagar. She already knows you're British and posted here for two years; you mentioned both when you arranged lunch. Now she wants to know whether you like the work. You give her the honest answer, including the part about your manager.

She checks her watch once. She has a hospital shift. You check yours twice, because your lunch break is ending and rent is due Friday. She catches you sliding the tissues beside your plate.

"Keeping them safe?"

"Trying to learn."

When she stands to return the trays, she points to her empty chair. "Watch that for me."

You do. She comes back for the chair, although she's already late enough to hurry. At the edge of the table, she pauses to ask when you finish work.

## That evening
After work, your phone lights up while you wait at a crossing on South Bridge Road.

Have you learned what the tissues mean?

You type that you're considering leaving a packet on your office chair. She tells you someone might take the chair anyway. You ask if she's free again. She has a night shift coming up, but she can do breakfast afterward, near Toa Payoh. You ask what time suits her. She sends you the coffee shop address.

In the morning, you get there early. The metal tables are still wet from someone washing the floor, and the paper sleeve around your cup goes damp in your hand. She arrives with a mark from her hair tie against her wrist.

She orders kopi C kosong. When you try to order the same thing, you put the words in an order the cashier has to ask her to repeat.

"Coffee with evaporated milk, no sugar," she tells you.

"I knew the coffee part."

"I could tell."

Her name is Rachel Tan Hui Min. She's thirty, a pediatric nurse at a public hospital, and she eats her toast slowly because she's been on her feet all night. A woman passing your table stops to thank her for remembering her child's favorite picture book. Rachel remembers the book. She asks how the child is doing, quietly enough that the next table can't hear.

When the woman leaves, you ask if it was a difficult shift. Rachel folds her napkin.

"That's their story."

You apologize. She shakes her head and asks about your morning instead. Your software problem sounds small when you say it aloud, but she listens until you finish.

You tell her you only have two years here. Maybe less, if your company changes its plans. She knows. You still wonder if another breakfast is fair to ask for.

"Can lah," she says.

You look up.

"We can have breakfast again. That's all I'm promising before nine."

She leaves to sleep. By afternoon, you want to send her a message, but her next shift is already on the calendar.

## The week she has no evening free
Your messages reach Rachel between shifts, her mother's grocery list and a family WhatsApp group that settles dinner before you've finished typing what you'd like to eat. She sends you a photograph of the wrong brand of soy sauce and asks you to help her remember the right one. You do. She doesn't offer an evening she hasn't got.

On Thursday, she asks her mother whether she'd be comfortable having you join them for a meal. Only after her mother agrees does Rachel ask you.

You meet at four, under a covered walkway in Toa Payoh. Rain comes down hard enough to blur the opposite block. The afternoon storm passes, but your work shirt stays damp. The air feels wet enough to wring out.

Rachel's mother has changed her plans, so Rachel takes you to a neighborhood hawker centre instead. She calls home first. At your table, an older man asks if he can share the open end. Rachel shifts her bag and makes room. He eats while the two of you decide what to order. Nobody needs an introduction.

By the time your food arrives, there are three meals and three separate conversations at your table. You have trouble following your own.

At the next table you hear several languages in the space of a minute. Rachel picks out English, Malay, Mandarin and Tamil before you can tell where one conversation ends and the next starts. You can barely keep up with Rachel's family chat in English.

Rachel takes a mouthful from her bowl. "Shiok." Her face gives you the meaning before she tells you the food's good. Near the exit, she nearly carries off your umbrella.

"Paiseh," she says, handing it back, embarrassed.

Outside, she waits while you open the umbrella. Your shoulder gets wet anyway. She has time to walk with you to the bus stop, but she has to collect those groceries afterward.

Three weeks after lunch, her message arrives: "We need to talk about your posting."

## Three weeks later
You reach the coffee shop in Toa Payoh while Rachel is finishing her shift. By the time she sits across from you, she's taken out her hair tie, but it has left a line through her hair. Your phone lies face down beside your cup. There's an unfinished reply to your manager on it, about what happens after your two-year assignment.

Rachel orders something to eat. You ask if she's hungry.

"A little."

She takes a bite before she speaks again.

"When your posting ends, will you leave?"

A spoon knocks against a glass at the next table. You've read your manager's message four times today, and you still don't have a reply for him.

"I don't know."

Rachel nods. She picks up her cup and waits while someone carries a tray between your chairs.

"That's an answer," she says. "I can work with an honest answer. I can't organize my life around a promise you haven't made."

You look down at your phone. She sees you do it.

"I'm not asking you to decide tonight."

"I know."

She tells you about her day because you've asked. A parent needs a phone call. One child wants the same picture book twice, even though Rachel has already read it badly the first time. She smiles at that, then checks the time. Her shifts change, and dinner sometimes has to change with them.

"What would staying cost you?" she asks.

You open your mouth, then close it. There's your work, of course. There's your mother in Britain, who expects to see you at Christmas. You can picture both clearly. You've never tried to fit them into the same year.

Rachel's phone rings. It's her mother, asking about something Rachel promised to pick up on the way home. Rachel says she'll get it, ends the call and puts the phone away.

You think she might need to leave. Instead, she looks back at you.

"What would it cost?" she asks again.

You have an end date. She wants to know what you're willing to do before you reach it.

## The walk to her block
You leave the coffee shop together. The pavement still holds the day's heat, and Rachel walks close to the shopfronts where the awnings give you a little shade. She has to get to the supermarket before it closes. You carry her empty cup to the bin because your hands need something to do.

"I can ask about staying," you say. "I should've answered my manager already."

Rachel waits while you cross at the lights.

"But I won't tell you the job decision is yours to make."

"Good," she says. "I wouldn't ask you to give up your family in Britain."

Your glasses start to fog as you step out from under the awning. You take them off and walk half a block looking past her shoulder. She slows so you don't have to hurry.

"Can I tell you something?" she asks.

You nod.

"Sometimes a child is uncomfortable, and I can't fix it. I do what I can on shift. Then I go home and think about what else I could've tried."

A bicycle passes. You wait for its bell to fade.

"I don't want you to tell me I've done enough," she says. "Some nights I can't hear that."

"Okay."

She looks at you. "Do you mean it?"

"I'll ask if you want me around, and you won't have to explain when you need quiet."

"You're going to have to practice."

"I know."

At her family's HDB block, she presses the lift button and reaches into her bag for her keys. She pulls out a packet of tissues instead. It's the one from your coffee shop table.

"I carried these all the way here," she says. "Nobody even needed the seat."

The lift arrives. Before she gets in, she opens the packet and hands you a tissue for your glasses. You clean the lenses and find her still waiting, holding the lift door for you. You hand it back to close the packet.

You get an invitation to dinner with her mother the following week.

## Dinner at her mother's flat
Rachel meets you downstairs and checks the time on your phone before you put it away.

"She's made soup," she says. "Come up."

At her mother's flat in Toa Payoh, you leave your shoes beside theirs. A pot sits on the stove, and a fan points straight at the dining table. Rachel's mother gives you a glass of water and asks how long you've been in Singapore. She remembers your answer when it comes up again over dinner.

The soup has lotus root and pork ribs. Her mother serves rice, then puts the ladle within your reach.

"What does your mother think of your posting?" she asks.

"She thinks the work's good. She asks when I'm coming home."

"And Rachel?"

You glance at Rachel. "I haven't told her how often we see each other."

Rachel looks up from her bowl.

"You told her we had lunch once," she says.

"That was true."

"It was also weeks ago."

Her mother takes a spoonful of soup and lets you answer. You tell her you'll call home. She asks what you build at work, and you try to explain the software without using the words you use with your manager. She asks what happens when something goes wrong. You can answer that one.

When you reach across Rachel for the serving spoon, she slides a bowl nearer to you. Her mother asks whether you've had enough rice. You say yes, then take a little more soup.

Outside the block, the evening air feels damp against your collar. Rachel walks with you toward the bus stop.

"Call your mother," she says. "Before she hears about me from somebody else."

"Who would tell her?"

"I have no idea. Call her."

You do it there, under the covered walkway. Your mother asks about work first. Then you tell her about Rachel, and about the soup, and that you should have mentioned her sooner. Your mother wants to know when she can speak to her. Rachel stands a few steps away while you say you'll ask.

The call ends. Rachel comes back and takes your hand for the walk to the bus stop.

The next invitation has more names on it.

## Chinese New Year
At Rachel's parents' flat, her auntie moves a chair before you can wonder where to stand. Rachel's mother hands you chopsticks. Her cousin gives you a little space beside a wide plate of yusheng and says, "You can reach from here."

Everyone gathers around the table. Rachel points out the vegetables and strips of fish while her auntie adds the dressing. Then the chopsticks go in. During lo hei, everybody lifts the salad and calls out wishes. You hear good health, good work, and something from Rachel's cousin about passing his exams. You lift your chopsticks higher to join in. A generous amount lands on the tablecloth.

Rachel looks at your sleeve. There is carrot on it too.

"You've made a wish for the laundry," she says.

You help pick up the pieces while the others keep tossing. Her auntie, pouring more dressing, tells you this colourful version was invented right here in Singapore, in 1964, by four chefs people called the Four Heavenly Kings. Rachel's cousin says he heard a different story. Nobody settles it. Her auntie laughs and passes you a napkin.

After dinner, that same auntie asks when you two will marry. You glance at Rachel. Her auntie has asked it lightly, but the room gets quieter around your chair.

"He needs to learn where I keep the spare towels first," Rachel says.

Her mother tells her auntie about the towels you folded after your last visit. Conversation starts again. You realize Rachel has noticed that you came back.

Later, you carry plates into the kitchen. Rachel rinses a serving spoon and asks what you'd wish for if nobody could hear you.

You think about your mother waiting for the next call. You think about your posting, which still has an end date.

"More time here," you say.

Rachel turns off the tap. "With me?"

"Yes."

Loving Rachel asks you to make time for the life she already has. She dries her hands on a towel and stands beside you, close enough that your wet sleeves touch.

## Want to BTO together?
A few weeks later, rain traps you and Rachel under a covered walkway in Toa Payoh. It's four in the afternoon. Water runs off the roof in sheets, and your work shirt sticks to your back. Rachel has finished a shift and is holding her phone where you can both see it.

There are floor plans on the screen. She enlarges one with two fingers.

"Want to BTO together?"

You look at her. A bus passes through the water at the curb. Rachel keeps the phone between you, though she has stopped looking at it.

"Are you proposing?"

"Yes. Will you marry me? We'll check eligibility together."

You take her hand. "Yes."

She smiles, then looks down at the plan. "We need to check if we can actually apply."

Rachel opens HDB's page for engaged couples. "If we're eligible, we can apply before the wedding," she says. "We just have to be legally married by the time we collect the keys." The flat could take years to build. Your posting ends sooner, and your residency paperwork is still uncertain.

"Most people I know grew up in one of these," she says, nodding at the blocks across the road. Out here, the flats rise on every side, and about seventy-seven per cent of Singapore's households live in one, by the Department of Statistics' count.

Rachel scrolls to the kitchen. She wants enough room for her mother to stand beside her at the stove. You ask where a table would fit when your family visits from Britain. She moves her thumb across the screen and shows you a spot by the window.

That night, you call your family together. Your mother asks Rachel about work. Your father asks when you can visit next. Once the call ends, you tell Rachel you don't know how often you can get home if you stay.

"Let's put visits in the plan," she says.

You open the calendar on your phone. Rachel sits beside you while you work out what to ask your manager, then check HDB's rules, ICA's residency guidance, and the Ministry of Manpower's work pass page. The rain has stopped. Your damp shirt is still hanging over the back of a chair when you write the first email.

## The application
Your manager agrees to discuss an extension, which means another meeting, another document, and a month of waiting. You answer work emails on the train while Rachel heads out for another shift. At her mother's table one Sunday, you take a call from Britain in the hallway because your father wants help with his new phone and will accept instructions only from you.

Your work extension comes through. You go through the residency paperwork separately, with Rachel nearby when you need to find an old address or take a break from the forms. More time passes. Your permanent residency application succeeds before you and Rachel apply for a flat. You read the notice once, then hand her the phone so she can read it herself.

"Now we check the rest," she says.

At the dining table, Rachel spreads out your HDB Flat Eligibility letter. You need a valid one before you can apply for a Build-To-Order flat. Rachel has written questions in the margin of a notebook. You and Rachel answer her questions, then send in the application together. Then you wait again.

On the train to work, you picture the block you hope to come home to. Rachel sends you the floor plan for your chosen unit with a circle around the kitchen. She wants to know if a small table will fit by the window. You measure the one in your rented place that evening. It might.

When news about the application arrives, Rachel reads the notice twice before she tells her mother. Her mother asks when she can visit. Rachel reminds her there are no keys yet.

At dinner, Rachel slips you a note on the back of a grocery receipt: Until we have a table, I suppose you'll have to keep choosing one. Bring tissues.

You fold it into your wallet. A week later, your mother calls to ask what she should pack for the wedding.

## The gatecrash
Your mother arrives from Britain with a dress bag she refuses to check and a question about whether Rachel has eaten. By Saturday morning, she's already asking Rachel's mother where to put the spare cups.

At her parents' flat in Toa Payoh, your brothers stand behind you in borrowed shirts. Rachel's bridesmaids block the door. They've planned a few games with her, and you have to finish them before they'll let you in. Your brothers offer advice from a safe distance.

First, you have to identify which of several handwritten notes is Rachel's. You pick the wrong one. Your mother, watching over a bridesmaid's shoulder, gets it right immediately.

The next challenge involves wasabi. You assure everyone you can keep a straight face. Rachel's bridesmaid holds up a phone, ready to show her the result. You manage almost three seconds.

From inside the flat, you hear Rachel laugh. You'd like to hear it again, though perhaps under different circumstances.

When they finally open the door, she's standing near the dining table, where her mother first asked you what yours thought of Singapore. You look at her, and the speech you've practiced disappears before you can say a word.

"I'm here," you say.

"I know."

She reaches for your hand. Your brothers go quiet, which may be the kindest thing they do all day.

Later, you and Rachel serve tea to her parents and the other elders taking part. Your family has asked about the ceremony beforehand; your mother sits close enough to see Rachel's face when her mother accepts the cup. Guests bring ang bao, red packets, for the celebration. Your brother keeps checking whether he's holding the teapot properly, while Rachel's aunt accepts her cup and presses a red packet into Rachel's hand.

Across the crowded room, Rachel's mother catches your eye and gestures toward a chair. She makes space for you as easily as she did at that first dinner.

The flat stays loud after the tea is poured. Tomorrow, there's a quieter appointment, and a promise you both have to make yourselves.

## The ROM solemnisation
The next day, the room is quiet enough to hear Rachel smooth the edge of her sleeve.

At your ROM solemnisation, you stand beside her and listen closely to the words you're asked to say. The solemniser asks Rachel if she takes you as her husband. "I do," she says. Her mother shifts in the chair behind you as the solemniser turns to you, and your mouth goes dry. "I do," you say. Now you're legally married.

You look at Rachel. She's watching you with the patient expression she uses when she knows you're about to say something difficult and wants you to take your time.

You promise to tell her when work might take you away. You promise to ask what she needs before deciding for her. The words sound small after all the planning that brought you here. You're relieved they sound like things you can do.

Rachel promises to tell you when she's tired, even when she'd rather spare you the worry. Her voice catches on the last word. She steadies it herself.

Afterward, you go for a meal with both families. Your brothers have recovered from the wasabi. Rachel's mother asks yours about the long flight, and your mother asks for the name of the dish Rachel said she'd like her to try. Across the table, Rachel nudges a bowl toward you before you realize you can't reach it.

Someone asks when you're moving into the new flat.

"It's still being built," you say.

Everyone laughs. You do too. For now, there are enough chairs at this table, and enough people offering to help when the time comes.

That evening, you go home to the place you're renting together. Her nursing shifts and your work hours take up most of the fridge door. You add the date of your next call to Britain in the corner.

In the kitchen, you open a drawer for a spoon and find Rachel's spare pens beside your measuring tape. Neither of you knows whose drawer it's supposed to be. She comes in, takes a pen, and leaves the tape where it is.

## The years before the keys
The rent comes up for renewal while the flat is still a floor plan on Rachel's phone. You sign the papers at the kitchen table. That night, you call your mother in Britain, and Rachel waves from behind you before leaving for her shift.

Her mother sends soup over the following Sunday. You return the containers washed, though she points out that you've put the lids on the wrong ones. Rachel checks her rota at breakfast and moves dinner with her mother to a night she can make.

Then Rachel has a difficult run of shifts, and her mother becomes unwell. Rachel and her family arrange the appointments and work out who can be with her. You take the groceries over when Rachel asks. You pick up a prescription on your way home. When she says she can handle a call herself, you leave her to it and put the rice on.

One evening, an update about the flat arrives. You hold out your phone as she comes through the door, still carrying her work bag.

"I can't do this tonight," she says.

You lower the phone. "Sorry."

She eats a little, calls her mother, and goes to bed. The next afternoon, she sits beside you at the table.

"Can we look now?"

You open the update. She reads it twice, then asks you to send it to her.

At the coffee shop near Toa Payoh, you order her kopi C kosong while she finds a table after another shift. When the family chat asks who can bring fruit to her mother's place, you answer before Rachel has to check her phone. You keep tissues in your bag because someone is always looking for one.

That night, Rachel writes her mother's next appointment on a receipt and sticks it to the fridge beside the date of your call to Britain.

On a Tuesday morning, an appointment to collect the keys appears on your calendar.

## Key collection
Rachel reads the appointment on your calendar twice, though the notice is on her phone too. On Tuesday, you collect the keys. She holds them in her palm for a moment before she closes her fingers around them.

You take the lift to your floor with a couple carrying a folded stroller. At your front door, Rachel tries one key, turns it the wrong way, and passes it to you without a word. You're both laughing when the lock finally gives.

The rooms are bare enough to echo. Rachel walks straight to the kitchen window. Afternoon light reaches the far wall, and she stands there checking it while you go ahead to the bedroom. When she joins you, she opens the wardrobe door and discovers there's no wardrobe yet.

At the dining area, you pull up the sketch she drew years ago. The table in it has room for six. You measure the wall with your feet and show her where the chairs would go.

"Four?" you ask.

"Four," she says. "My mother will bring extra people anyway."

You find a smaller table. On the first evening, your family in Britain gets a video call from its unassembled legs. Your mother asks to see the kitchen again. Your brother wants to know whether the bedroom has a view. Rachel carries the phone through every room, including the one full of cardboard.

Her mother arrives with soup and a container of rice. She studies the kitchen cupboards, then suggests keeping the bowls near the stove. Rachel thanks her. After she leaves, you and Rachel put the bowls near the sink, where you keep reaching for them.

The table takes an hour to assemble. You move it twice before the chairs can clear the kitchen doorway. While you unpack the last box, you set a packet of tissues on its new surface. Rachel picks it up and wipes a dusty handprint from the counter.

## A table for two
Weeks later, you leave work with a message from Rachel. Her shift is over, and she's hungry. By the time you reach Maxwell Food Centre, the lunch crowd has thinned, though there's still a queue for chicken rice and someone is looking for a table with a tray in each hand.

Rachel spots you near the drinks stall. She puts her bag on the chair beside yours and checks the queue.

"Two plates?"

"Please."

She heads off before you can offer to get them. You sit down and pull a packet of tissues from your bag. It's warm from the walk over. You place it on her side of the table, then move her bag under your chair where nobody will trip over it.

A man carrying fishball noodles stops beside you. He looks at the empty chair.

"Is anyone sitting here?"

"She's getting lunch," you say. You spot a free place two tables over and point it out. "There's one there, if that helps."

He thanks you and goes to sit down. You turn the tissue packet so it doesn't hang over the edge. A ceiling fan stirs the corners of the napkins at the next table, and somewhere behind you, a stallholder calls out an order you can't quite hear.

Your phone lights up with an email from work. You read the first line, decide it can wait, and turn the screen over. Rachel is still in the queue. When she reaches the front, she raises two fingers toward the stallholder, then catches you watching and raises an eyebrow. You hold up two fingers back.

She returns with the plates balanced carefully, sets yours in front of you, and sees the packet on her side.

"You remembered," she says.

"I kept your seat."

"I can see that."

She sits. Her hand rests beside the packet, and yours meets it on the table.
