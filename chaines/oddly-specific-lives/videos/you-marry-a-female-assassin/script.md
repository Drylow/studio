# POV: You Marry a Female Assassin

The first time you see her, your beagle is choking himself on the lead to reach a woman who has counted every exit on Hampstead Heath. Just after seven, wet grass shines under the lamps near Parliament Hill. You're thinking about council tax and whether the dog has found a stranger rich enough to sue you. Then the path behind her empties. Politely, which is worse. She looks at your dog and says, "He has sense. You should borrow some." Later, a British university study will tell you how many contract killings happened on dog walks, under polite skies. Still, you follow her.

## The second lap around the Heath
Biscuit usually saves his loyalty for crumbs, pigeons, and his own poor judgment, so when he trots after her, you notice. You circle back across Hampstead Heath, past Parliament Hill and the ponds, pretending Biscuit needs a longer walk. Really, you're tracking the woman with the paper coffee cup. She sits where she can see three paths and never puts her back to the open grass. She checks her reflection in her phone without touching it. You introduce yourself as a primary-school music teacher in north London, early thirties, with a beagle named Biscuit, anxiety, and no useful survival instincts. She says her name is Mara. She works as a freelance risk consultant, said like she's practiced making it boring. Biscuit puts wet paws on her coat. She wipes one with a napkin. "He's badly organized." You apologize too many times. She accepts none of them. Then she smiles once, very slightly, which is worse.

## Monday morning, Year 4 recorders
On Monday morning, you teach Year 4 children to play "Hot Cross Buns" on plastic recorders, which makes you question every choice that led to adulthood. Your world smells of damp coats, photocopier toner, packed lunches, and the headteacher saying "quick chat" with terrifying cheerfulness. At lunch, between marking rhythm worksheets and stopping a child from putting a recorder in a plant pot, you search for freelance risk consultants. The results are useless: corporate websites, security language, glossy photos of people pointing at laptops. Because the Heath keeps replaying in your head, you search something stupider: are dog walks dangerous in London. That is how you find David Wilson's 2014 Birmingham City University study in The Howard Journal of Criminal Justice. It examines 27 mainland contract killings, 1974 to 2013: 35 hitmen, one hitwoman. One woman in the whole sample is not much of a dating pool. This should make you feel safer. It doesn't. You do not connect Mara to it yet. You remember the path emptying, though it proves nothing except that London enjoys a little drama before breakfast.

## Borough Market, two Saturdays later
Two Saturdays later, you see Mara again at Borough Market near London Bridge, early enough that the stalls are waking up and the air smells of coffee, bread, and rain on stone. She is buying mushrooms with cash. Not a card. Notes folded small, change counted without looking.

She notices you before you can hide behind a cheese stall, which would be an undignified place to begin a romance.

"You teach music," she says.

You ask how she knows.

"You count children in groups of four."

She walks with you through the market, leaving half a step of space, choosing the side that gives her a view behind glass. A tourist raises a phone, and Mara slips behind hanging herbs so smoothly it looks accidental.

She knows Southwark Street, the turn toward London Bridge station, and one side street you miss completely.

You ask if risk consulting is dangerous.

"Only when clients are stupid."

Biscuit is not there, but you wish he were, because Biscuit would know whether this is a red flag or a woman with excellent posture.

## The restaurant facing the door
Your first proper date is at a small restaurant in Kentish Town after work, because you panic and choose somewhere you can afford without checking the wine list like a coward. She arrives exactly 4 minutes early. She lets you pick the table, then quietly asks to switch. Her seat faces the door, the kitchen entrance, and the front window.

You joke that she has strong opinions about ambience.

"Ambience is just lighting plus exits."

During dinner, she asks about your class, Biscuit, and the songs children choose when they are allowed to make noise on purpose. She remembers the child who cries during assemblies. She asks if Biscuit hates fireworks. She cuts her food into neat, patient pieces.

When the bill comes, she pays cash before you can perform the British card duel. You mention the Birmingham City University study because your flirting instincts are faulty. Professor David Wilson found the average fee was about 15,000 pounds.

She only says, "People undervalue difficult work." Then she changes the subject to your rent, which is somehow more invasive.

## Columbia Road on Sunday
Three weeks in, she invites you to Columbia Road Flower Market on a Sunday, where the street is packed, loud, and bright with tulips, eucalyptus, shouting sellers, and people carrying plants like wounded soldiers. She hates crowds, but moves through them calmly, slipping through gaps before they exist. She buys lavender for your flat because Biscuit needs "a less theatrical nervous system."

When she reaches for cash, you see three phones in her bag. One old black handset. One normal smartphone. One small spare wrapped in cloth. She sees you see them.

"Work phones?" you ask.

"Something like that."

"That's a lot of something."

"I bill accordingly."

Later, at the Northern line, she stands where she can see the platform entrance and the tunnel mouth. When the train comes, she doesn't like the crush. For exactly 3 seconds, her hand finds your sleeve.

It feels chosen.

She changes the route home at the last minute.

"Never make a habit easy to read."

You think she means London safety. Later, you understand the dog-walk irony. That night, one of those phones wakes your flat at 3 a.m.

## The kitchen table at 3 a.m.
The phone buzzes in Mara's coat at 3 a.m. She is up before you understand the sound. Biscuit sleeps under a chair. Rain taps the kitchen window.

She takes the call in the hallway and says almost nothing. When she returns, her face is calm in a way your body doesn't like.

Then you see the small grey suitcase by the front door. It was not there when you went to bed.

"Is risk consulting a lie?"

She sits at the kitchen table. That is how you know.

"Mostly."

She looks at her hands.

"I was army. After that, I did work for people who wanted problems removed."

Your flat becomes very small.

She says she once read the Birmingham City University paper by David Wilson and colleagues. Novices, dilettantes, journeymen, masters.

"If you need the label, use the last one."

The cost is not adventure. It is insomnia, lying, no pictures, and knowing too much about ordinary streets.

"One more year."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you were about to love the wrong version of me."

One year, she says. You hear the year differently after that.

## The week you try to be sensible
You do the sensible thing for 6 days, which is panic wearing a tie. You sleep badly, teach badly, and conduct the Year 2 percussion circle like a man who now fears triangles.

At lunch, you read the Birmingham study again, because fear likes homework. The average British contract killer in its sample is 38. The youngest is 15. The oldest is 63. The average victim is 36.

Fear turns into ages: 15 at bus stops, 38 in supermarkets, 63 by school gates.

The ordinary detail returns hardest. Many killings happened while victims were walking the dog or going shopping.

This lands on Biscuit. Suddenly every pavement in north London looks like a sentence with the last word missing.

You meet Mara in a cafe near Tufnell Park. She sits facing the door. You notice that you notice.

"You should leave if you need to."

"Are you leaving?"

"I'm trying."

It is not enough.

## The man in the parked car
Two months in, a silver car sits across from your flat for 3 evenings. Engine off. Driver in a cap. Too still to be waiting for a takeaway.

You notice it only because Mara has taught you to notice reflections in dark windows, which is an annoying skill once acquired.

She sees the car through the kettle's reflection while making tea. She does not stop pouring.

Biscuit growls for the first time in his life, then hides behind your legs, which is touching and professionally disappointing.

"Don't look again."

She steps outside without a coat. You stay upstairs, hearing only the kettle click off and a bus sigh at the stop.

After 4 minutes, the car leaves.

She comes back with cold hands and no explanation, then washes her hands for a long time because London door handles are filthy, which is true and not the point.

"Is it over?"

"For tonight."

She dries her hands, sits beside the tea, and listens to the street.

## The proposal without photographs
For a while, things seem fine, which is how London traps you into optimism. The relationship becomes rules you never signed but follow. You don't take surprise photos, you don't tag her, and you don't ask where the grey suitcase goes. No murder jokes before breakfast, because apparently there are standards.

Three months in, she meets your colleagues outside school and becomes popular by saying almost nothing and fixing the broken staff-room kettle with a hairpin.

Biscuit adores her, which feels like betrayal from a dog who once barked at a bin bag for 11 minutes.

At Borough Market again, she buys you coffee and holds the paper cup in both hands.

"I turned down work."

Then, softer.

"I can refuse work now. But the street still talks to me."

Later, near the book stalls under Waterloo Bridge, you stand out of the wind.

"Stay. Properly."

She checks every exit before answering.

"Yes. But no photos."

You laugh. She doesn't. Then she does, barely, and that is the yes you keep.

## Old Marylebone Town Hall
You marry on a grey Thursday at Old Marylebone Town Hall, with two witnesses, one borrowed tie, and no photographer. Mara wears dark blue and stands where she can see the doors. When the registrar asks if anyone knows a reason, she glances at the exit, and you nearly laugh at the worst possible moment. Afterwards there is a long pub lunch in Marylebone, roast beef and Yorkshire puddings, at a corner table, her back to the wall, Biscuit under your chair eating a stolen potato. Your mother asks for one photo. Mara says, "Remember it instead." Somehow, your mother obeys.

The first week as her husband is mostly paperwork, toast crumbs, and learning how quietly another person can enter a room.

Mara moves into your flat with 2 suitcases, 4 coats, and one locked metal box she places in your wardrobe.

"Don't open that."

"I won't."

She studies you the way she used to study exits.

"I know. That's why it's there."

Married life begins smaller than you expected. She labels the spare keys and buys the same tea twice because one brand "tastes too visible."

At a colleague's birthday drinks in Clerkenwell, she pretends to listen to a story about a school inspection until a parked car waits outside with its lights off. Mara takes your wrist.

"We're leaving."

You leave.

On the bus home, she watches the reflection in the window instead of the road.

Her thumb rests over your pulse until the car turns away.

## Skye without cameras
On the second morning of the honeymoon, rain moves sideways across a cottage window near Portree, under low clouds, with wet stone and offended sheep.

On the Quiraing, she walks hood up, calmer in open wind than she ever is in London.

You learn what she cannot stand.

Fireworks. Sudden bangs. Identical hotel corridors.

One night, a car backfires outside the cottage. She is awake before you are, sitting upright, breath controlled too carefully.

You don't touch her until she nods.

This matters.

"I was good at being useful," she says.

"You don't have to be useful here."

She looks at the window, where rain crosses the glass sideways.

"That's harder than it sounds."

The honeymoon is where you see how much of her calm is labor.

She lets you take one landscape photo, no people in it.

Later, it becomes the only honeymoon picture in the flat.

At breakfast, she still chooses the chair nearest the door.

## The year of almost normal
Married life becomes ridiculous: packed lunches, council tax, Biscuit's bills, and a locked drawer you both pretend is furniture.

You learn which questions not to ask, because answers become weight, and you already carry enough.

Living with Mara trains your eye: exits, pavement moods, and a phone watched too hard.

Some days, the old life shows up like a bruise.

She changes the dog-walk route every day. Hampstead Heath, Highgate, a loop that offends Biscuit's narrative.

The study said many contract killings happened on ordinary streets, while someone walked the dog or went shopping.

Your wife treats Biscuit's toilet break like a strategic conference, which Biscuit finds flattering.

At 3 a.m., a phone rings one last time. She lets it ring out, then places it in the locked drawer.

The next week, she starts pottery because an adult-learning flyer lands on the doormat.

"Clay is unlikely to call me at night."

Her first bowl is terrible.

This pleases you too much.

Months later, the dead phone stays in the drawer, beside her worst bowl.

## Six a.m., the same dog walk
At 6 a.m., Hampstead Heath is wet grass, low light, and London not fully awake.

Biscuit pulls ahead, older now, still anxious about squirrels.

Mara walks beside you, retired, married, carrying a pottery tote with one ugly bowl inside.

At the first fork, she changes the route.

You notice.

She notices.

"We never take the same path," you say.

"No."

"Still?"

"Always."

Retirement hasn't made her careless. Marriage hasn't remade her.

She untangles Biscuit's lead from a wet bench leg with those calm hands.

You think of ordinary streets, dog walks, polite skies, and questions that let someone breathe.

She takes your hand before crossing.

She takes your hand because she wants to.

Wet grass, lamps, Biscuit ahead, her hand in yours, the route changing before the city notices.
