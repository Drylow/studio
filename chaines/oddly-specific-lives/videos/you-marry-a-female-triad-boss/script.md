# POV: You Marry a Female Triad Boss

By the time she asks if you know how to pour tea, three men have decided to leave Mong Kok. You are standing under the red signs on Sai Yeung Choi Street, holding a cracked takeaway box of curry fish balls, thinking about rent and your nose bleeding onto your only clean shirt. She doesn't raise her voice. She says one quiet sentence in Cantonese, and the men become interested in another street. She hands you a tissue. "Don't tilt your head back." So why does Sunday yum cha feel more dangerous than the alley ever did anyway?

## Sunday yum cha
Sunday arrives like a court date with tea cups. She meets you at Lin Heung Tea House in Central, where shared tables fill every gap and metal trolleys push through steam like small, loud ships. Aunties move faster than lawyers. Lids clatter. Someone argues with a dumpling cart.

She wears a plain blouse, hair tied back, no jewelry except a jade ring she keeps turning with her thumb.

"Not like that," she says.

You're rinsing your bowl and chopsticks with hot tea, apparently like a man washing evidence. She corrects your hands.

Har gow arrives. Siu mai. Char siu bao. Cheung fun. A bamboo steamer lands, and she touches it before anyone reaches.

Her auntie, Yi Ma, watches your hands more than your face.

You learn to pour for others first. You learn to tap two fingers when someone pours for you. Her English name is Serena Lau. Everyone calls her Ah Sin.

Then a man sees her, lowers his eyes, leaves cash under a saucer, and goes.

The men in Mong Kok feel far away, until you notice how each careful pour lowers the room's temperature.

## Temple Street after closing
A week later, you go looking for the answer near Temple Street Night Market, after the stalls start folding tarps and fortune tellers stack their plastic stools. The neon looks tired. So do you.

You find her at a dai pai dong on Woosung Street, under fluorescent light, eating stir-fried clams with black bean sauce and beef chow fun. The stall owner calls her Miss Lau with careful warmth, the kind people use for someone who can ruin their week but usually doesn't.

She notices you before you decide whether to leave.

"Sit," she says.

You sit.

"Don't pretend this is an accident."

"I was curious."

"You followed badly."

That seems unfair. Also accurate.

"Curious men become paperwork," she says.

Around her, things keep moving. A driver leaves his phone alone. An older woman counts cash in neat stacks. A busboy gets told to finish school before his mother hears.

Someone says Jade Lantern Association in Cantonese, and the table goes quiet.

Serena peels a prawn and sets it on your plate.

Apparently, this is how your courtship begins. Shellfish and controlled panic.

Then she stops performing control for one sentence.

## The Star Ferry warning
The next time, she takes you onto the Star Ferry from Tsim Sha Tsui to Central at dusk. Victoria Harbour is gray-blue, the skyline switching on one office at a time, and the engine knocks like an old refrigerator with secrets.

She stands outside, wind flattening her blouse at the wrists, watching Hong Kong Island come closer.

"People around me are loyal," she says. "Or frightened. Or ambitious. Sometimes all three before breakfast."

You say nothing, which is becoming your strongest skill.

"If you want a normal woman, leave at Central."

The ferry pushes through dark water. Her hair comes loose near one ear.

"Do you want me to leave?"

She folds the ticket into a tiny rectangle, pressing each crease with her thumbnail.

"I want one room in my life where I don't have to be Miss Lau."

That is what does it. The tiny fold. The tired pressure of her thumbnail. The exhausted precision in her hands.

"Rules," she says. "Never ask names you don't need. Never accept gifts from men who don't smile. Never mistake silence for peace."

"I understand."

"No," she says. "But you listen. That's rarer."

Everything is in place, but you don't know what everything is building toward yet.

## Wong Tai Sin incense
Three weeks in, she brings you to Wong Tai Sin Temple on a humid morning, not as a date exactly, because calling it that would make her laugh. Worshippers hold incense, bow, and pray. The air smells like smoke, flowers, and rain trapped in stone.

She buys incense for both of you.

"Hold it straight," she says. "You look like you're signaling aircraft."

Nearby, people shake kau chim fortune sticks from bamboo cylinders, waiting for one to fall. Serena says her grandmother came here before every major decision.

She closes her eyes for exactly 3 breaths.

"My father started with a mahjong parlor," she says. "Protection, favors, debts. Then restaurants. Shipping. Cleaner on the surface."

She watches the incense smoke climb.

"He died before teaching me how to be soft."

You let the sentence sit there.

"When he died, the first call was from a lender in Macau."

"That's heavy."

"So are groceries if you carry enough of them."

You almost laugh. She does first, quietly.

The incense burns lower while she stands beside you, letting you see the part of her that doesn't give orders.

Soon, you learn her family has its own way of measuring quiet.

## Mahjong and the family's verdict
Two months in, she takes you to her mother's flat in Prince Edward, above a street where minibuses sigh at the curb and laundry hangs outside barred windows.

Mrs. Lau is small, immaculate, and terrifying in house slippers.

Four elders play mahjong at a green felt table. The tiles click like tiny bones. Someone gives you chrysanthemum tea and no instructions.

You remove your shoes. Good.

You bring oranges. Better.

You almost reach across the table, and three aunties inhale at once.

Mrs. Lau asks what your parents do, how steady your job is, and whether you can eat bitter melon. You answer badly, but honestly. Steady enough, you say, which sounds like a man negotiating with gravity. Serena doesn't rescue you.

A cousin says, "Maybe too soft."

Serena looks at him.

The joke dies young.

After dinner, Mrs. Lau says in English, "He is not clever."

Serena says, "Good. Clever men create invoices."

Mrs. Lau looks at you again. "But he watches."

The tea keeps coming. She doesn't smile.

There is a reason you remember this night later, and it isn't the mahjong.

## The proposal before dawn
Six months in, after a long night of rain, she appears at your apartment in Sham Shui Po at 5:20 a.m. with wet hair and no driver.

Your building smells like damp concrete and someone's instant noodles. She stands in your doorway with one sleeve pulled low over her wrist. Under it, you see bruising, dark and neat, quiet as a closed mouth.

She refuses coffee.

Then she drinks yours anyway.

For a while, the only sound is rain ticking against the air conditioner. Two senior men inside the Jade Lantern Association want her to step back and marry someone useful. Someone with the right surname. Someone who makes doors close.

"They think a husband is a lock they can choose."

"What do you think?"

She looks at your tiny kitchen, your chipped mug, your laundry sagging on a plastic rack.

"I think I want someone who can leave, and still chooses to stay."

She opens a small red box. No diamond. A plain gold band, sized for you.

"If you say yes, you become mine in front of everyone."

It takes you 3 seconds again.

She notices.

"Still slow."

"Still yes."

She closes your fingers around the ring and asks, very softly, if you know how to pour tea.

You have 28 days before the families sit down together, and the clock has already started.

## Door games
The wedding morning arrives loud, humid, and already negotiating.

You stand outside her family flat with your groomsmen, sweating through a new shirt and holding red packets for the bridesmaids. The hallway smells like hair spray, oranges, and elevator metal.

The bridesmaids block the door.

First, you sing a Cantopop chorus badly enough to damage property values. Then you answer questions about Serena's favorite tea. Then you do push-ups while holding a lai see envelope between your teeth, which is less romantic than advertised.

You learn money in red envelopes should be clean, new, and offered with both hands. You also learn Serena can hear you from behind the bedroom door, because her silence somehow makes your singing worse.

Downstairs, a black car stops.

A senior Jade Lantern man sends up a red envelope too thick to be friendly. The hallway stills. Drivers, aunties, neighbors. Everyone suddenly studies the floor.

Serena doesn't leave the bedroom.

"Send it back," she says. "This is my wedding."

Everyone hears it.

The envelope goes back.

The door opens. The bridesmaids laugh first, and Serena stands there with her face unguarded.

## The tea ceremony
You kneel beside Serena on red cushions before her elders, holding cups of sweet tea with red dates and lotus seeds.

By now, you know the motion. Both hands. Lower your eyes. Offer first to the right person. Wait. Don't rush the silence.

Your knees already hurt. The cup is too hot. You understand, suddenly, that the order is the point.

You serve Mrs. Lau's older brother, then aunties, uncles, elders with faces like sealed documents. Each cup returns as a blessing, a gold bracelet, a red envelope, a polite verdict folded into ceremony.

Your hands shake once.

Serena steadies the saucer with one finger, so small nobody else sees it.

Mrs. Lau takes the cup last. She lets you hold it there long enough for your knees to start negotiating.

"You pour for the living," she says. "You remember the dead. You protect the quiet."

"I will."

Serena looks down, her jaw tight over the empty saucer.

Near the shoe cabinet, the uninvited senior man stands while the young cousins make a careful space around him.

He receives no tea.

No auntie reaches for a cup on his behalf.

And at the banquet, every table will understand it.

## The nine-course banquet
The banquet is in a Cantonese restaurant in Tsim Sha Tsui, all chandeliers, lazy Susans, and red cloth over tables that have been placed with military sadness.

You learn why the Jade Lantern senior sits near the door, why Serena's mother has the head table, and who notices.

Suckling pig arrives first, lacquered and shining. Then steamed fish, prawns, roast chicken, abalone with mushrooms, fried rice, longevity noodles, and sweet dessert soup that tastes like relief pretending to be sugar.

Serena changes into a red qipao for the toasts.

You follow her table to table, holding a small glass, learning to smile without promising anything.

A police acquaintance congratulates you carefully. A lawyer does it neatly. A shipping man does it like weather reports. Three aunties do it with their eyes.

Then the senior Jade Lantern man raises his glass.

His toast sounds friendly until it doesn't.

Serena lifts hers.

"To family," she says. "To lawful business. To clean hands."

Nobody claps too soon.

Under the table, she squeezes your hand once.

You stay quiet beside her while she answers the toast herself.

## Lunar New Year as husband and wife
Married life begins with supermarket bags, bills, late dinners, and Serena falling asleep with one hand still close to her phone.

Married life isn't dramatic, which somehow makes it harder.

At Lunar New Year, you hang red fai chun in the flat and place oranges where she tells you, because luck in her house has strong opinions about corners.

You give lai see to children, doormen, and unmarried cousins, who take the envelopes with both hands and sudden good posture.

Serena keeps a careful list of every amount, because Mrs. Lau will know by lunch if one envelope is light.

Yum cha returns. You pour tea before anyone looks at the pot. When someone pours for you, two fingers tap the table by instinct.

Mahjong returns too.

Mrs. Lau says your strategy is improving, which means she only insults it for 10 minutes.

Loving Serena is midnight congee, spare flats in the car, remembered birthdays, and egg waffles from the same Mong Kok stall.

Then she disappears for two days.

When she comes back, her phone goes face down.

"Do you hate this yet?"

"I hate the waiting."

"Fair."

You keep waiting on the sofa, even though you hate every minute of it.

## Victoria Harbour before sunrise
One night, the call comes during rain.

Serena looks at the screen, closes her eyes for one breath, and changes into a black blouse without asking you to wait up.

You wait up.

Of course you do.

She returns before dawn with no blood, no story, and no victory in her face. Only exhaustion, wet hair, and a gray smear of incense ash on one cuff from Wong Tai Sin, where she stopped alone before coming home.

After that, the senior man is gone.

He stops appearing at dinners, toasts, and those meetings where everyone leaves one chair politely empty. Nobody explains why.

Serena does not celebrate.

She takes you to the Star Ferry pier before the city wakes.

The ferry is nearly empty. Victoria Harbour lies pale and gray-blue under the morning, and the engine knocks like it did the first time she warned you to leave at Central.

She stands beside you, hands around a small thermos.

"You should have married someone easier."

"I tried," you say. "She refused the envelope."

She almost smiles.

Then she pours tea into a paper cup and gives it to you first.

For a second, you don't move.

She has broken her own order in private, and the paper cup feels heavier in your hands.

"Do you know how to pour tea now?"

You take the thermos with both hands.

Carefully, you pour for her as the ferry crosses the harbor.

She taps two fingers on the bench.

The bench is cold, the tea is warm, and for once her hand stays where you can reach it.

Rain softens the city beyond the windows. In your pocket, the old tissue from that first night is still folded thin.

Her hand finds yours in the gray light.

This time, nobody is leaving.
