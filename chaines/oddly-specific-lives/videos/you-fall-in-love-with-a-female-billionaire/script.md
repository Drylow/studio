# POV: You Fall in Love With a Female Billionaire

The woman holding the broken bicycle is worth more than the whole street outside your shop, and right now she can't pay you eleven dollars. Her assistant slides a black corporate card across your counter. Your card reader beeps, thinks about it, and refuses. The assistant tries again. Same beep. "Cash?" you ask. The woman in the grey coat looks at you like you've asked her for a kidney. "I don't carry cash," she says. "Nobody carries cash." You look at the bicycle. It's old, green, Italian, beautiful, and the chain is wrapped around the back wheel like spaghetti. "Then it's free," you say. She doesn't thank you. She just watches your hands while you work.

## The shop on Valencia Street
Your shop is the size of a parking space, wedged between a laundromat and a taco place in San Francisco. You're twenty-nine. You rent it month to month, and every month is a small war. You fix bicycles. You fix them well. You fix them for students, delivery riders, old men who've ridden the same frame since the seventies, and people who cry a little when you tell them their bike can be saved.

You do not fix them for people who arrive in black cars.

But the black car is parked outside, and the man behind the wheel hasn't turned off the engine. The assistant, a tall young man with two phones, keeps checking one of them.

"We have a call in nine minutes," he says.

"She can take it here," you say. "Chain's almost back on."

The woman finally speaks again. "It was my father's," she says, nodding at the bike. "He rode it to work for twenty years. I've never ridden it once."

"Why not?"

"I can't ride a bicycle."

You stop turning the pedal. She says it like a confession, then looks at her watch like the confession was scheduled.

## The thank-you note
Three days later, an envelope arrives at the shop. Thick paper. Your name written by hand. Inside, a card that says only: "Thank you for the chain. V."

And a cheque.

You read the number four times. Then you put it in the drawer under the register and close the drawer and lean on it, as if it might climb out.

It's more than you make in a year.

You don't cash it. You can't explain why. Maybe because cashing it would make the whole thing a transaction, and it didn't feel like one. Maybe because you're scared of what the bank teller's face would do.

Instead, you look her up. It takes four seconds. Victoria Hale, thirty-three, founder of a logistics software company that tells trucks, ships and warehouses where everything is. You've used it without knowing. Everyone has. There's a photo of her on a stage, in the same grey coat, not smiling.

And there's a word under her name that you've only ever seen in magazines at the dentist: billionaire.

According to Forbes, its 2024 list of the world's billionaires counted a record two thousand seven hundred and eighty-one people. Only three hundred and sixty-nine of them were women, about thirteen percent. And Forbes notes that most of those women inherited their money.

She didn't. She built a company from a laptop. And she can't ride a bike.

## The second visit
She comes back a week later, alone this time. Well, not alone. There's a man in a dark suit standing outside the laundromat pretending to read a menu from the taco place. He's been reading it for twenty minutes.

"You didn't cash the cheque," she says.

"How do you know?"

"I have people who know things." She says it flatly, like a weather report. "Why didn't you cash it?"

"Because it was eleven dollars of work."

"It was a thank-you."

"A thank-you is a card. You sent a card. The cheque was a lot of extra."

She tilts her head. Nobody, you realise, has refused her money in a very long time. She doesn't know what to do with her hands. She puts them in her coat pockets.

"Teach me," she says.

"Teach you what?"

"To ride it. My father's bike. I want to ride it."

"I fix bikes. I don't teach people to ride them."

"I'll pay you."

"See, that's the problem."

She almost smiles. Almost. The man outside turns a page of the taco menu that doesn't have pages.

## Fifteen-minute blocks
You agree to one lesson. Her assistant calls you to schedule it, and that's when you learn how her life works.

Her calendar is cut into fifteen-minute blocks. Every block has a name. Board prep. Investor call. Lunch, standing. Legal review. Car to airport. Your lesson appears in her calendar as "V. — Bike — 45 min", squeezed between a call with Singapore and a dinner with people whose names the assistant won't say.

"Six a.m.," he tells you. "Saturday. She'll be there at five fifty-nine."

"Why five fifty-nine?"

"Because six is late."

You learn about the rest of it slowly, over weeks, in pieces. The security team that changes every eight hours. The driver who knows four routes to every place she goes. The lawyers. The board of directors that can, in theory, fire her from the company she founded.

And the family office. That one takes a while to understand. It's a private company with its own staff whose only job is to manage one family's money, houses, taxes, travel and problems. Ernst & Young has estimated there are at least ten thousand single-family offices in the world. Ten thousand little companies, each one existing for one rich family.

Hers has eleven employees. You have one employee. It's you, and he's underpaid.

## The empty parking lot
Saturday, five fifty-nine. An empty supermarket parking lot near the bay, still wet from the night fog. She arrives in running shoes, expensive leggings, and the expression of someone about to sign a contract she hasn't read.

You lower the seat of her father's bike. "Feet flat on the ground," you say. "You're just going to walk with it between your legs. Like a very slow horse."

"That's undignified."

"That's step one."

She walks the bike across the parking lot. Back again. Her security man stands by the black car, arms folded, trying not to watch. On the third lap, you tell her to lift her feet for one second. She does. The bike tips. She catches it. She looks at you, furious.

"That's normal," you say.

"I don't like normal."

"Everybody falls, Victoria."

It's the first time you've said her name. She notices. She lifts her feet again. Two seconds this time. Then three.

At six forty-four, her phone buzzes. Singapore. She hands you the bike, takes the call standing in the middle of the empty lot, and talks about shipping containers for exactly fifteen minutes. Then she hangs up, takes the bike back, and says, "Again."

## Dates that look like meetings
You start seeing each other, if you can call it that. She schedules you.

"Dinner — 90 min — Thursday." That's a date. "Walk — 30 min — after board." That's also a date. Once, her calendar just says "Him", and the assistant gives you a look that suggests he's been asked not to have opinions.

She's funnier than anyone expects. Not with jokes. With precision. When a waiter recommends the wine "for a special occasion", she asks him to define special. When her investors call a quarter "challenging", she translates it for you: "It means we lost money and they want to sound brave."

She has secrets, too. She eats instant noodles. Not ironically. She keeps three packs in her desk at the office and one in her coat. "They cost forty cents," she tells you, as if defending a family member. "They taste the same every time. Nothing else in my life does that."

And she never carries cash. Never. She once tried to buy you a coffee from a street cart, opened her wallet, found nothing inside but cards, and stood there looking betrayed by her own wallet.

You paid. Three dollars. She looked at the coffee like it was a gift. Which, you realise later, it was. People give her things all the time. They almost never pay for her.

## The jet
She has to fly to New York for two days. "Come," she says. "I'll have you back Sunday."

You've flown four times in your life. Each time, your knees were in your chest and someone's elbow was in your ribs.

This time, the plane is waiting at a small private terminal where nobody checks your shoes. There's no line. There's no gate. There's just a staircase and a woman at the top who already knows your name.

Inside, there are leather seats that swivel, a table made of real wood, and a sofa. An actual sofa. On a plane.

"Where's the seatbelt for the sofa?" you ask.

The flight attendant shows you. It's hidden in the cushions. Victoria laughs for the first time since you've known her. Not a polite laugh. A real one, short and surprised, like a sneeze.

A new large-cabin business jet in this class has been listed at around sixty-five million dollars, according to the aviation press. You do the maths at thirty thousand feet. It's roughly two hundred thousand years of your shop's rent.

"Do you ever get used to this?" you ask.

She looks out the window at the clouds. "You get used to everything," she says. "That's the dangerous part."

## The dinner you pay for
Back in San Francisco, you insist on taking her to dinner. A real one. Your treat.

She protests. You don't listen. You pick a nice place. Not her kind of nice, with fourteen courses and a chef who comes out to explain the foam. Your kind of nice, with candles and a menu that fits on one page.

It still costs a week of your income.

You don't tell her. You smile through dessert. You leave a good tip. You walk her to the black car like a gentleman. Then you go home and eat cereal for dinner for six days.

She finds out anyway. She always finds out. She doesn't mention the money. She just shows up at the shop the following Monday with two packs of instant noodles and a kettle, and you eat lunch together on overturned crates while a delivery rider waits for his tyre.

"Even," she says.

"That's not even."

"It's the most even thing I've ever done."

## The photographers
It takes the press three months to notice you.

A photographer catches the two of you outside your shop: her in the grey coat, you in a greasy apron, both laughing at something the taco guy said. By the next morning, the picture is everywhere.

The headlines are not kind. "Tech billionaire's mystery man." "Who is the bike guy?" One website puts a red circle around your hands, as if your fingernails were evidence.

Customers start coming into the shop who don't have bikes. They pretend to look at inner tubes. They take photos when you turn around. One woman asks how much you charge to "fix" a bike she doesn't own.

Victoria calls that night. "I'm sorry," she says. "I should have warned you."

"Warned me about what?"

"That everyone will decide what you are before you get to decide it."

You look at the photo on your phone. You look happy in it. That's the part nobody wrote about.

## Riding without hands on the seat
Saturday mornings in the parking lot continue, fog or no fog. Her security man now brings coffee for three.

By the second month, she can ride in a straight line while you hold the back of the seat. By the third month, she can turn. Badly. Wide, slow turns, like a container ship leaving port.

"You're overthinking it," you tell her.

"Thinking is my job."

"Not here."

One morning, you let go of the seat without telling her. She rides fifteen metres alone. Then she realises. She looks back at you, wobbles, panics, and rides straight into a shopping trolley.

She sits on the wet asphalt next to her father's bike, laughing so hard she can't get up. The security man takes one step forward, sees her face, and takes one step back.

"You let go," she says.

"You didn't need me."

She looks at you for a long time. "That's not the same thing," she says.

## The question
You don't propose with a ring that matches her bank account. You couldn't if you tried.

You propose in the shop, after closing, with the shutter half down and the smell of chain grease in the air. You've made her a ring yourself out of a small steel bearing from her father's old bike, polished for three nights until it shone.

"It's a ball bearing," you say. "From the back wheel. I replaced it. The bike is fine."

She holds it under the shop lamp.

"It's worth about two dollars," you add, in case she needs the information.

She puts it on. It's slightly too big. She doesn't care. "Yes," she says, and then, because she's Victoria: "I'll have someone resize it."

"No you won't."

"No," she agrees. "I won't."

## The prenup
A week later, her lawyers come to your apartment.

There are three of them. They bring a document thicker than a phone book. They're polite in the way people are polite when they're paid by the hour and the hours are very expensive.

Prenuptial agreements used to be rare. A Harris Poll for Axios in 2022 found that fifteen percent of married Americans had signed one, up from three percent in a 2010 survey. In Victoria's world, it isn't a question. It's furniture.

The lead lawyer walks you through it. If you divorce, you get this. If she dies, you get that. Her shares, her company, her houses, her plane: none of it is yours, not now, not ever.

You nod through all of it. You don't argue a single page.

Then, on the last page, before you sign, you take a pen and add one handwritten line: "She rides a bicycle at least once a week."

The lawyers look at each other. One of them calls Victoria. You hear her voice through the phone, short and certain.

"Leave it in," she says.

## The wedding nobody expected
The magazines expect a gala. A castle. Four hundred guests. A dress that costs more than a house.

You get married on a Tuesday afternoon, in a small garden in Sausalito, with twenty-two people.

Her mother cries from the first minute. Your mother cries from the second. Her assistant is the best man, sort of, and he's holding both his phones and the rings at the same time. The taco guy caters. Victoria wears a simple white dress and the steel ring. You wear a suit you bought, not rented, which is a first.

When it's time for the vows, she reads hers off a small card, the way she'd read a quarterly report. "I promise to be on time," she says. "Early, actually. I promise to tell you the truth even when it's expensive. And I promise to keep falling off your bicycle until I stop falling off your bicycle."

Twenty-two people laugh. You don't. You can't. Your throat has decided to be closed.

## Two apartments, one fridge
Married life starts strangely. She has a penthouse with a view of the bay. You have a one-bedroom above a dry cleaner's. For the first month, you live in both, like a divorced couple who happen to still be married.

Then she moves into yours.

Nobody believes it, including her security team, who now has to stand outside a dry cleaner's. But she says the penthouse is too quiet, and that she likes falling asleep to the sound of the cleaner's machines.

She still can't cook. She burns water. Not metaphorically. She once forgot a pan of water on the stove until it disappeared. You come home to find her staring at the empty pan like it lied to her.

You cook. She does the dishes with the same focus she uses for acquisitions.

Her mother, a retired school nurse from Ohio, adores you. She calls you every Sunday to ask if Victoria is eating real food. You say yes. You both know you're lying.

## The board
Her board of directors does not adore you.

There are nine of them. Most own large pieces of the company. They meet four times a year and call her in between, constantly. To them, you are a risk. A man with no money married to a woman with a lot of it is, in their language, "a governance concern".

At a company party, one of them, a silver-haired man with very white teeth, shakes your hand for a long time.

"So," he says, "you fix bicycles."

"I do."

"Fascinating." He says it the way people say "interesting" about a rash.

Victoria hears it from across the room. She doesn't react. She just walks over, takes your arm, and tells the silver-haired man that his last investment lost thirty percent. Then she asks you to show her where the noodles are.

There are no noodles at the party. You find some anyway. You eat them on the fire escape, her in a cocktail dress, you in your good suit, watching the city lights.

## The yacht
In the spring, she buys a yacht.

She doesn't announce it. She just mentions it on a Tuesday, the way you'd mention buying new socks. "I bought a boat."

"How big is the boat?"

"It has a crew."

It's beautiful. White, long, with a deck where you can watch the sunset over the bay with a glass of something that costs more than your bike tools. There's a captain, a chef, four deckhands, and a stewardess who folds towels into the shape of swans.

On the third evening, you do the maths on a napkin.

The superyacht industry has a rule of thumb: a yacht costs roughly ten percent of its purchase price every single year, just to run. Crew, fuel, insurance, docking. You divide. You divide again. You show her the napkin.

"We used it six days this year," you say. "Each day on this boat costs more than my shop makes in ten years."

She takes the napkin. Studies it. Folds it neatly.

"Your maths is right," she says.

"I know."

She keeps the napkin in her wallet. Next to the cards. Where the cash should be.

## The day the money fell
In June, her company's stock falls.

It isn't her fault, exactly. A rival announces something. Analysts get nervous. Investors sell. By the closing bell, the shares are down nineteen percent.

Most of a billionaire's fortune isn't cash, Forbes explains in its methodology. It's shares of their company, valued at the stock price. When the price falls, the fortune falls. On paper, Victoria loses several billion dollars between breakfast and dinner.

That same day, you lose your floor pump. Someone takes it from outside the shop.

You come home in a bad mood about the pump. She's sitting on the kitchen floor with her laptop, eating noodles straight from the pot.

"How was your day?" you ask.

"I lost a few billion dollars."

"Someone stole my pump."

She looks at you. You look at her. And then you both start laughing, the kind of laughter that comes from very tired people. You sit down next to her on the floor. She shares the noodles.

"Which one's worse?" she asks.

"The pump," you say. "I actually used the pump."

## The gold digger
The tabloids move on from "bike guy" to something uglier.

A gossip website runs a long article calling you a gold digger. It has a cartoon of you climbing a mountain of money with a bicycle pump. It quotes "a source close to the couple". It suggests that you've been "playing the long game".

You read it in the shop, alone, after closing. You read it three times. Then you close the laptop and sit in the dark.

What hurts isn't the cartoon. It's that you can't prove anything. How do you prove you'd love her if she were broke? You can't. You can only keep being the same person in the same apron in the same shop.

Victoria finds you there at midnight. She doesn't say anything comforting. She doesn't say it'll blow over. She just sits on a crate next to you.

"You never cashed the first cheque," she says.

"No."

"I still have it. It's in a frame in my office. My lawyers wanted to know why. I told them it was the best investment I ever made."

## Your friends
Your friends change, a little.

Not all at once. Not on purpose. But there's a pause, now, before they invite you somewhere. Will she come? Will her security come? Is this place good enough?

Your oldest friend, a mechanic named Rudy, stops letting you buy rounds. "Your money's no good here," he says, and he means it as a joke, but it lands wrong. Because it's not your money. It's never been your money. You still fix bicycles. You still pay your shop's rent, every month, from your own account, with the prenup on file to prove it.

One evening you say this, out loud, at the bar.

Rudy's quiet for a while. Then he slides his beer toward you. "Okay," he says. "Your round, then."

You pay. It costs fourteen dollars. It's the best fourteen dollars you've spent all year.

## The anniversary
Your first anniversary falls on the night before her quarterly results.

She forgets. Not completely. She remembers at eleven at night, from the office, and calls you, and says, "I'm so sorry," three times in a row, and you can hear in her voice that she's been awake for twenty hours.

You don't argue. You hang up, boil water, make two packs of instant noodles, pour them into a thermos, and ride your bike across the city in the dark.

The security guard in her building lobby knows you by now. He lets you up.

She's alone on the thirty-first floor, surrounded by spreadsheets, shoes off, hair tied up with a pencil. When she sees the thermos, she puts her face in her hands.

You eat on the floor of her office at two in the morning, looking at the lights of the bay.

"Happy anniversary," you say.

"This is the best dinner I've ever had," she says. "Don't tell the chef on the boat."

## The friend who sold the story
Not everyone around you is a friend.

A man you've known since school, someone who used to borrow tools and never return them, starts calling more often. He wants to have dinner. He wants to meet Victoria. He asks a lot of questions about your apartment, her schedule, her security.

You don't notice anything wrong. Victoria's head of security does.

He comes to the shop one morning with a printed email. Your old friend has been offering a tabloid "an exclusive inside story of the billionaire's marriage". Photos. Private details. A price.

You read the email twice. Your hands are shaking.

Victoria takes it from you. "This happens," she says quietly. "It's happened to me since I was twenty-six. It's not your fault."

"It feels like my fault."

"That's how they make it feel," she says. "That's the business model."

She doesn't sue him. She doesn't need to. The story never runs. You never speak to him again.

## The vote
In the autumn, the board makes its move.

The stock hasn't recovered. The silver-haired man has gathered enough votes. They want a new chief executive. Someone "more experienced". Someone, in Victoria's words, "taller and named Brad".

She still owns her shares. They can't take those. But they can take the title. The office. The company she built from a laptop in a rented room.

The vote is set for Thursday.

On Wednesday night, she doesn't come home. She doesn't answer her phone. Her assistant doesn't know where she is. Her security team does, but they won't say.

You find her at four in the morning, in the only place that makes sense.

The empty parking lot by the bay. Her father's green bike next to her on the ground. She's sitting on the curb, still in her work clothes.

"I tried to ride it," she says. "I couldn't. I kept thinking about the vote."

"You're overthinking it."

"Thinking is my job."

"Not here," you say. And you hold out your hand.

## The morning after
She loses the vote. Six to three.

She walks out of the boardroom at ten fourteen in the morning. Not ten fifteen. Ten fourteen. She's early even to her own defeat.

You're waiting in the lobby in your apron, because you came straight from the shop. People stare. You don't care. She walks past the reporters, past the security gates, past the silver-haired man, and straight into your arms.

"I'm still the biggest shareholder," she says into your shoulder.

"I know."

"I'm still very, very rich."

"I know."

"I don't have anything scheduled," she says. "For the first time in eleven years, I have nothing in my calendar."

You take her to the shop. She sits on a crate all afternoon in jeans, watching you true a wheel. She asks questions. How the spokes pull against each other. How a small adjustment on one side fixes a wobble on the other.

"It's all tension," she says. "Balanced tension."

"That's a bicycle wheel."

"That's a company," she says. "I just never had the time to look at one being built."

## Selling the yacht
The first thing she does with her empty calendar is sell the yacht.

She doesn't make a speech about it. She just calls a broker on a Monday, and by the end of the month it belongs to someone else. The crew all get new jobs and a very large bonus. The stewardess sends you a towel folded into a swan, as a goodbye present.

You keep the swan on the shelf above the register. It slowly unfolds over the following weeks until it's just a towel again. You keep it anyway.

"Do you miss it?" you ask her.

"The boat?"

"Yeah."

She thinks about it with the seriousness she gives to everything. "I miss the sunsets," she says. "We can watch those from the parking lot."

## Something small
For a while, she does nothing. She sleeps eight hours. She visits her mother in Ohio. She learns to boil water without losing it.

Then she gets restless. Of course she does. She's Victoria.

She doesn't start another giant company. She starts something small. A tiny software tool that helps small repair shops keep track of their parts, their customers and their bills. She builds the first version on her laptop at your counter, between customers, eating noodles.

You're the first user. It works. It tells you, for the first time in your life, exactly how much money your shop makes. It's a bit depressing.

"You need to raise your prices," she says.

"I don't want to raise my prices."

"You undercharge for everything."

"I charged you eleven dollars."

"You charged me zero," she says. "I'm still trying to pay you back."

## The second shop
Two years after the vote, you open a second shop together, in a neighbourhood where there isn't one.

It has her name on the lease and yours on the sign. It has her software on the computer and your tools on the wall. And every Saturday morning, it has a free repair day for kids.

They come with flat tyres, broken brakes, chains wrapped around wheels like spaghetti. You fix them. Victoria writes each repair down on a whiteboard, because she likes the numbers. By the end of the first year, the whiteboard says one thousand two hundred and six bikes.

Some of the kids don't know how to ride yet. Victoria teaches those ones.

"Feet flat on the ground," she tells them. "Like a very slow horse."

"That's undignified," one little girl says.

"That's step one," says Victoria.

## Once a week
The prenup clause is never removed. You check, once, as a joke. It's still there on the last page, in your handwriting. "She rides a bicycle at least once a week."

She does. Every week. Rain, fog, quarterly results, her mother's birthday. Saturday at five fifty-nine, in the empty parking lot by the bay.

Sometimes she rides for an hour. Sometimes for five minutes in a business suit between two calls. Once, with a fever, because "a contract is a contract".

Her old board has had three chief executives since she left. None of them has been taller than Brad. The stock has recovered, then fallen, then recovered again. She checks it less now. Some days, she doesn't check it at all.

She still doesn't carry cash. But she keeps a three-dollar bill in her wallet now, folded next to the yacht napkin, in case she ever needs to buy you a coffee.

## The parking lot at dawn
It's a Saturday in spring. Five fifty-nine. Fog rolling off the bay.

She takes her father's green bike out of the back of a very ordinary car. She doesn't need your help with the seat anymore. She doesn't need you to hold the handlebars. She swings a leg over, pushes off, and rides a long, perfect circle around the empty parking lot. No wobble. No shopping trolley. Just a woman on her father's bicycle in the morning light.

She comes back to where you're standing. Stops. Puts one foot down.

"I don't need you to hold the seat," she says.

"I know."

"Hold it anyway."

So you do. You walk beside her with one hand on the back of the seat, the way you did the very first morning, while she pedals slowly and the fog lifts off the water.

She's worth more than the whole street your shop is on. She has never once been able to pay you back. And outside the little shop on Valencia Street, two bicycles lean against each other in the sun, waiting for Monday.
