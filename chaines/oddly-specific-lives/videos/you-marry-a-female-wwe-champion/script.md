# POV: You Marry a Female WWE Champion

The first time you see her, she's pressing an ice pack to her ribs while thirty strangers practice how to hate her. You're in a hallway at WWE's training center in Orlando with a dead laptop, a screwdriver, and rent waiting at home like a dog with teeth. Then the crash pads in the next room go quiet. She looks at the laptop under your arm and says, "If that dies, my villain dies with it." You laugh because you think she's joking. Nobody else does. You decide to fix the computer. By midnight, you're still there.

## The phone that isn't broken
She follows you to the front desk with the ice pack still trapped under one arm, like pain is just another appointment she is late for.

"Can you fix my phone too?" she says.

You take it because you are 31, an Orlando IT contractor, and you still believe every small yes might become a full-time job with dental. The screen is black. Your parking validation is already dying in your pocket.

You press one button. Then another.

The brightness is turned all the way down.

She knows.

Around you, people with taped wrists carry protein shakers past ring ropes and crash pads, calmly throwing themselves onto mats before sunrise. At WWE's Orlando Performance Center, wrestlers rehearse promos on camera, review the footage, and learn that the match starts before anyone touches the mat.

"I'm Jess," she says. "28. Former gymnast. Current problem."

Her shoulder is bruised yellow at the edge.

"You really don't know who I am."

"Should I?"

She smiles like you just gave her oxygen.

## Three mornings later
Three mornings later, another ticket brings you back before 7 a.m., when the building looks like a clinic with wrestling rings under fluorescent light and choreography taped to the walls.

Jess is alone in an empty practice ring under flat fluorescent light, repeating one fall again and again. Back to mat. Breathe out. Stand up. Do it again.

You know WWE plans the finish ahead of time. Then her back hits the mat, and the word fake leaves your mouth before you say it. Her chin tucks, her breath goes, and the mat cracks under her like a door slammed by someone angry.

She sees you watching.

"Don't make that face."

"What face?"

"The civilian face."

She climbs through the ropes and shows you the ice pack, the taped ribs, the shoulder she keeps rolling like a stuck door hinge. You start noticing them after that. Ice packs. White tape. The soft slap of crash pads.

You find them in her bag, on the passenger seat, sweating on your kitchen counter.

Now you notice the work in everything she does.

## The diner on Colonial Drive
After midnight, she texts that the front-desk printer is jammed.

It isn't.

By 12:40, you're sitting across from her at a late-night diner on East Colonial Drive, where the coffee tastes burned and the pancakes arrive like an apology nobody asked for.

"I'm a heel," she says.

You wait.

"The villain. The person people are supposed to boo. Wrestling has words for everything."

"My job is to make people want me to lose."

"That sounds lonely."

She looks down at her coffee.

"Only if you're good at it."

She talks without bragging. College gymnastics. The tryout. Developmental years. Camera angles. How to land without showing fear. She tells you WWE was called WWF until 2002, when it lost a legal fight over the initials with the World Wildlife Fund.

"The tape is real," she says. "So is knowing which camera can't see you wince."

Then her hands shake when she reaches for the syrup.

She gives up and cuts her pancakes into tiny squares.

That does it.

"One day," she says, "they're going to hate me so loud I'll know I did it right."

## The airport rule
Three weeks in, you pick her up at Orlando International Airport after a TV trip, near baggage claim where everyone looks half-lost and overcharged.

She comes out in a hoodie, sunglasses, and an exhausted smile meant only for you.

Fans recognize her before you do.

"Coward," one man calls.

Another asks for a selfie while insulting her like she personally ruined his childhood. She doesn't correct him. She lifts her chin and gives him the cold little smirk he paid to hate.

In the parking garage, beside a rental car with a dented bumper, she finally breathes.

"You can't hold my hand in public."

You look at her fingers. They are inches from yours.

She explains kayfabe, the old wrestling tradition of treating storylines and characters as real, sometimes outside the ring. Her story says she is single, cruel, untouchable.

A soft boyfriend in airport parking ruins the lie.

"I understand," you say.

You mostly don't.

At the hotel, she grips your hand with both of hers. In the airport, her fingers hover two inches from yours all the way to the elevator.

## Months out
At 2:13 a.m., Jess sits on your bathroom floor, staring at her right arm like it belongs to someone else. She tries to lift it, gets halfway, and stops.

"Sorry."

That is the part you can't stand.

She breathes through her teeth on the cold tile and apologizes for needing help.

For months, Jess is off television and in your apartment, sore, bored, and limping to the kitchen in your old socks.

You learn ice rotation, heating pads, and physical therapy near Lake Nona. The medicine cabinet fills with tape, ibuprofen, heating pads, and ice packs. So yeah, not great.

At 3 a.m., she's awake with ice melting through a towel, counting breaths until her shoulder stops burning.

"If I can't be hated, I don't know what I am."

"You're Jess."

She studies you. She almost believes it.

You drive, listen, refill ice packs, and learn not to flinch when she hurts. Her trainer sees the fear first.

"If you're staying, learn the ice schedule, drive steady, and don't make a face in waiting rooms."

You wonder what remains when her body is taken from her.

## The proposal on the living-room mattress
During rehab, your apartment becomes a training room. The couch moves aside, and a cheap mattress takes its place. Ice packs sit in the freezer beside frozen peas.

She teaches you the safest version of a back bump because you keep saying you understand, and she knows you don't.

"Relax your neck."

You land badly, make a sound like a folding chair, and she laughs for the first time in weeks.

One supervised flop onto a mattress gives you a new respect for everyone thrown through anything on purpose.

She returns to light training. The crash pads thud through the apartment in a steady rhythm, familiar enough that you stop looking up.

One afternoon, after taping your wrists as a joke, she says, "Your stance is terrible."

"You like the stance."

You haven't proposed yet. The ring is hidden in a sock drawer because panic has made you practical.

"Are you asking me because I'm broken?"

"I'm asking because you're here."

She says yes. Then she critiques your foot placement.

You ask on the mattress between ice packs and wrist tape, with the crash pads still stacked against the wall. But the next problem is public. She can wear the ring at home, in the car, and under a hoodie at the grocery store, but not where a fan can zoom in.

## The beach wedding near Cocoa
By the time you marry, the ceremony happens on a quiet stretch near Cocoa Beach, close enough to Orlando that nobody has to make it a production.

There is no arena, no branding, and no camera beyond family phones held mostly low. Anyone who takes a picture waits until her mother checks the background before it leaves the group chat.

Her trainer gives the speech beneath a white canopy, saying Jess learned to fall before she learned to trust anyone to catch her.

Someone mentions WrestleMania 32 in 2016, when WWE retired the Divas Championship and introduced the Women's Championship. Jess remembers watching that change and seeing a door open.

Before she walks across the sand, she looks down at her simple dress and asks, "Do I look ridiculous?"

She's barefoot, slightly sunburned from rehearsal, and trying not to shake.

"No."

"That was too fast."

After the vows, she performs the promised backflip in her dress. She lands cleanly in the sand, and her trainer yells like a proud uncle.

At the reception, he raises his glass.

"Next time I see you cry, I want gold on the table."

On the ride home, she slips the ring onto a chain and tucks it under her shirt before the gas station.

## Married to the villain
Married life begins with protein tubs beside cereal, athletic tape in the junk drawer, and an ice pack wrapped in a dish towel during breakfast.

She travels most weeks. Your texts become time zones, hotel gyms, rental cars, airport coffee, and photos of her boots beside unfamiliar beds.

At a televised event, you sit in the crowd like an ordinary fan. You wear nothing that connects you to her.

Twenty thousand people boo when she walks out. The sound hits you physically, like weather inside a building.

She is brilliant at it. Slow smile. Cruel posture. One look that makes strangers furious.

Later, she reminds you that women first main-evented WrestleMania in April 2019, at WrestleMania 35 in MetLife Stadium. She wants her fictional main event to feel worthy of that history.

After the match, you text, "You okay?"

"Ask me at home."

At home, she cries into your sweatshirt for ninety seconds. Then she pulls back.

"Did the boos sound big enough?"

You tell her they did.

She nods.

By morning, strangers online have circled her ring finger in a blurry fan photo. She moves the ring back to the chain beneath her shirt, where the marriage is still real and much harder for strangers to touch.

## The biggest show of her year
The championship program begins with a phone call she takes in the hallway, barefoot, one hand over the speaker like the refrigerator might leak secrets.

She gives you none of the names, finishes, or logos, and dinner becomes careful because the secret is in the room.

She doesn't tell you the finish.

Kayfabe has infected the chicken.

For a while, things seem normal enough. You meal prep rice in plastic containers. She practices promos in the laundry room, glaring at a stack of towels like they betrayed her family. Online, the gold belt is only a rumor moving through message boards at 2 a.m.

Then her sleep gets thin.

She knows the milestones. She says the date of the show like a prayer and a debt.

One night, she puts both empty hands on the kitchen table.

"I think I win."

"You think?"

"I know. I'm scared to say it in the room."

Before the arena, she gives you one rule.

"Don't sit where I can see you."

"Why?"

"Because if I see your face, I might soften."

Then she looks at the table.

"If I bring it home, don't make a big speech."

## The gold belt
You sit where she told you to sit, high enough that the ring looks small and bright, like a dangerous toy under hospital lights.

The arena shows no real branding from where you are. WWE is a name in the air. The belt waiting near the timekeeper is generic gold, heavy and blank, catching every flash like it has been expecting her.

When Jess walks out, the boos rise so hard they turn into weather.

For one second, it is almost beautiful.

She lifts her chin, smiles that cruel little smile, and you remember the diner on Colonial Drive, her hands shaking over pancakes.

One day they're going to hate me so loud I'll know I did it right.

She knows the finish, and she still has to protect bodies on every fall. She takes the bumps clean. She protects her opponent's neck. She sells pain without begging for pity. She times the last fall like a locked door opening.

Then it's over.

Her hands close around the gold.

They shake.

Backstage, she finds you behind a curtain in a concrete hallway, still in her gear and boots, mascara wrecked just enough to tell the truth.

"Don't make a big speech."

"I won't."

You cry first.

She laughs once, then cries harder, folding into you like she's been waiting all night to stop standing.

## The kitchen table
After midnight in Orlando, the apartment is quiet except for the refrigerator hum and the soft scrape of her boots on tile.

The generic gold belt sits on the kitchen table.

The belt her trainer once wished onto this table shines under the cheap ceiling light beside two unopened bottles of water.

An ice pack rests against her ribs again.

That first ice pack belongs to the hallway now. Here in the kitchen, this one is wrapped in a dish towel from your own drawer, held in place by the arm you know not to touch too quickly.

Thousands hated her tonight.

She did it right.

Now she is asleep on your shoulder in the kitchen chair, still in her boots, one hand loose on the belt like it might disappear if she trusts the room too much.

After a while, she wakes halfway.

"Did they hate me?"

"So much."

"Good."

Her eyes close again.

"Does it hurt?"

"Yeah."

A breath.

"Stay."

You stay.

Under the table, away from cameras, away from kayfabe, her hand finds yours.

Nobody makes either of you let go.
