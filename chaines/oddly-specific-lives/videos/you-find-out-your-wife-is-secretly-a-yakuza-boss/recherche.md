# Recherche et consignes — POV: You Find Out Your Wife Is Secretly a Yakuza Boss

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS about her world (use several, rounded, each inside a scene, with the source named in the sentence; never invent other statistics):
- Japan's National Police Agency counted about 18,800 members and associates of designated crime groups at the end of 2024, the twentieth straight year of decline and a record low; formal members fell below 10,000 for the first time.
- Police data cited by Japanese media point to stricter laws (the Anti-Boryokudan Act of 1992 and its amendments) and ageing members as the main causes.
- By 2011 every one of Japan's 47 prefectures had passed an organized-crime exclusion ordinance: businesses must not deal with gang members, who struggle to open bank accounts, rent apartments, buy insurance or sign phone contracts in their own name.
- Local rules treat someone as a gang member for about five years after they quit (the "five-year rule"); former members describe being unable to open a bank account or buy a car during that time.
- Women are not formal members. The boss's wife, called "ane-san" (big sister), is shown the same respect as the boss; a criminologist who studied yakuza women described their behind-closed-doors work managing money and keeping the peace as the "glue" of the group.
- A Japan Society lecture described Mako Nishimura as the only woman known to have gone through the sakazuki, the sake-cup ritual that binds a member to a boss.
- The sakazuki echoes the san-san-kudo of a Shinto wedding, where bride and groom each take three sips from three sake cups.
- Many onsen and public baths still refuse entry to people with tattoos because of the association with gangs.

She takes off her cardigan and shows you the irezumi across her back (a carp climbing a waterfall), the reason for the onsen. She gives you one chance to leave: your visa, your school, your life, untouched. You stay, and you ask one question only: "Is the bento real?" "The bento is real." She reads the newspaper numbers out loud: the families are dying, fewer members every year, the old men have nowhere to go. Her plan, slowly: close the family, find jobs for the old men (a noodle stand, a delivery company, a small construction crew), pay the debts, give the office back to its owner. The price: some of the old men call her a traitor; one of them, the scarred man from the supermarket, stays loyal and becomes the cook of the noodle stand. CRISIS (~75%): the last meeting where she ends it; you wait in the car outside for four hours. ENDING (90-100%): five years later, the five-year rule has run out; a bank in Namba on a rainy morning; she fills in the form herself, very slowly, and the teller bows to her the normal, small, polite fifteen degrees: the first ordinary bow of her life; outside, she hands you your bento.
