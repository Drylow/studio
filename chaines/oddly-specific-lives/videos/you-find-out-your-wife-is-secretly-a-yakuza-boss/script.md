# POV: You Find Out Your Wife Is Secretly a Yakuza Boss

A man with a scar through one eyebrow bows to your wife at Kuromon Ichiba, and he won't straighten while she's checking leeks for bruises. You hold the tofu and worry about the rent increase. Ice from the fish stall melts into the drain. Rin turns a leek in her hand. She says, "Go home." He straightens and leaves without buying anything. You ask who he is. "Someone from work," she says, and picks another leek. So why did she ask you to put the apartment lease, the electricity, even her phone plan in your name?

## Three years earlier
Three years earlier, you meet Rin in Namba, at the door of your conversation school. She has come to book lessons for her nephew. The school is closed.

At 31, you've come from Canada to teach, and your visa renewal worries you more than your job. Rin notices the Japanese on the sign you've taped to the door. You mean to say the school is closed for the afternoon. You've written something closer to the afternoon is closed.

"Can I?" she asks.

She fixes it with your pen, then checks that you can read what she's written. She's born in Osaka, she says, and does the books for her family's real-estate company.

A week later, she comes back to check the sign is still right, and stays for karaoke. She laughs so hard she misses her turn. Her singing is worse. She fixes the sign again the next morning. Later, when you learn why her name stays off your bills, you remember how carefully she checked that sign.

## The wedding
For a year, she brings food after your late shifts. When her father gets sick, you wait with her outside the hospital. You propose. She says yes. At Sumiyoshi Taisha in Osaka, you kneel beside Rin for san-san-kudo. You take sake from three cups while the shrine's wooden floor holds the warmth of the afternoon. Rin steadies the smallest cup with both hands. 

Her side of the room is full of quiet men in dark suits.  Several call her "ojo," young miss, when they greet her. You take it for a family nickname, though one man waits for her to sit before he does.

On the phone from Canada, your mother asks when she will meet Rin's family.

"Soon," Rin says, and changes the subject.

On the train home, Rin notices you've stuffed your dress shirt into your bag. She takes it out and folds it in thirds across her lap, careful of the collar.

That shirt is folded the same way on your bed now. You can still picture the man waiting to sit.

## The names on the bills
Back in your apartment, Rin puts the folded shirt away while you sort the mail. Every envelope carries your name and none carries hers.

"It's easier," she says.

She pays the household bills in cash, and for a long time you find the arrangement convenient. One set of forms. Fewer questions when you move in. You don't ask easier for whom, because your landlord keeps the rent low. Today, his increase notice arrives.

You remember New Year: two of Rin's "uncles" arrive with a box of mochi that looks expensive before you open it. They bow at your door. You remember Shinsekai, near Tsutenkaku, where a ramen-shop owner waves your money away. Rin thanks him like she expected to pay.

Every morning she makes your bento while you look for clean socks. The morning after Kuromon Ichiba, you watch her press the lid shut with the same hands that checked each leek. You wonder how many small comforts she's arranged without telling you.

Her phone rings before she can put the bento in your bag.

## Three in the morning
Rin answers before the second ring. Your bento sits on the counter. You hear her Osaka dialect turn sharp in the kitchen, though the call lasts less than a minute.

"Someone at work has died," she tells you.

It takes until dinner for her to say it's her father. At his funeral, hundreds of men in black suits line the street. Officers photograph the gathering from a van. You recognize one of the men who bowed at your wedding. He bows again when Rin passes, lower this time.

That night, you sit across from her at the kitchen table. Your untouched bento is still in the fridge. Rin keeps her hands around a cup of tea.

"My father led the Hibari Lane family," she says. "There's no son."

You wait. She tells you it's a yakuza group. An old lieutenant leads it on paper. The decisions pass through her.

You think of the lease, the men at the shrine, the man beside the leeks. You can't tell which part of your marriage she needs you for.

Rin looks at the cup, then answers the question you haven't asked.

## The kitchen table
"Women generally aren't formal members," Rin says. "My father's men started calling me ane-san before I married you."

You know the word. Ane-san is a way to address a boss's wife. Rin says her father's men use it because they bring their decisions to her. Rin says it gives her no rank on paper.

She stands and takes off her cardigan. Underneath, she wears a slip. A carp climbs a waterfall across her back, its scales dark against her skin.

"That's why I keep refusing the onsen."

You remember every excuse. The crowded baths. The long drive. Later, you check onsen rules on your phone. Some baths still turn away guests with tattoos.

Rin puts the cardigan over a chair. "You can leave. Keep your school, your visa, your ordinary mornings. I'll make sure of it."

That offer hurts more than the tattoo. She has thought about losing you.

"Is the bento real?" you ask.

"The bento is real."

You stay at the table. Only when she reaches for her cardigan do her hands shake.

The next morning, she packs your usual lunch. Before you take it, she gives you a rule.

## Rules for an ordinary day
"Don't photograph family gatherings," Rin says. "Don't ask about late calls. Don't discuss my family at school."

You carry the bento to the conversation school in Namba. Two weeks later, Rin changes your route home halfway through a walk. A police car waits at the next crossing. She sees it before you do. You walk beside her anyway, less certain of where you stand.

On Friday, a detective from the Osaka prefectural police organized-crime unit waits outside your school.

"Do you know who you married?"

"Yes."

He asks another question. You give him nothing about Rin or the group. Inside, your students want to practice talking about weekend plans. You keep seeing the detective's face above their worksheets.

That night, Rin asks, "Was he polite?"

"Yes."

"Are you frightened?"

"Yes."

She leaves your answer alone.

You think of the cups at your wedding. You learn what sakazuki means. A boss and a member exchange sake cups to seal their bond. At your wedding, the cups you shared with Rin marked your marriage. Still, you wish you'd understood what those men were calling Rin.

A new apartment application arrives the following week.

## A receipt in your name
Rin writes her name at the top of the apartment application. At the viewing, the landlord asks about her family. She answers him directly. The next day, he declines. He gives no other reason.

She sets the rejected application beside the old lease. She runs her thumb over the date, then leaves it there. She says, "I thought we could do this one differently."

On Saturday, she chooses white chrysanthemums for her father's grave. She pays with your card. At Sumiyoshi's cemetery, the receipt falls from her coat pocket, and she picks it up before you can. Your name sits above the price of flowers she chose herself.

You read the Osaka Prefectural Police ordinance that night. It bars businesses from knowingly giving benefits that support gang operations. The National Police Agency tells companies to cut gang ties. Japan's land ministry's model lease excludes gang members. Rin has never been a formal member. That landlord cites no rule. He makes his own decision about her ties.

You want to ask why she keeps leading the family. Before you find a way, police raid its office.

## Tea at dawn
Rin doesn't come home after the raid. You wait through the night at the kitchen table, your visa renewal form unfinished beside a cold cup. You don't call anyone. By five, you know the sound each train makes beyond the window.

At dawn, the door opens. Rin is uninjured. She sits on the floor of the genkan without taking off her shoes.

"Can you make tea?"

You bring two cups. She has a newspaper folded under her arm. Rin taps a small box on the inside page. "The National Police Agency," she says. "About 18,800 of us at the end of 2024. Fewer every year, twenty years in a row." She folds the corner down. "We are getting old," she says. "And there is nowhere for the old men to go." She runs a finger down a photograph of the office door. Its brass nameplate is gone. Beside it, a man she's known since she was young hides his face from the camera. "He has two kids," she says. The paper shakes against her knee, and you remember she gave him a key to that office.

Rin folds the paper. "I don't think this family should continue."

You sit on the step beside her.

"Some of them don't know where else to go."

The tea cools while she tells you how long some of the older men have depended on her. Then she gets up, finds a pen, and lays a list beside your visa form.

## The list
Rin writes "Close Hibari Lane" at the top of the page. Under it come the ordinary debts the family still owes, the rented office keys, and three names beside possible jobs. A noodle stand in Shinsekai. A delivery company. A small construction crew. She calls each place herself and asks what work is available. Nobody promises her anything.

The man with the scar from Kuromon Market comes by on Thursday. He and Rin meet over coffee, but he spends most of the visit turning the leeks she's bought for dinner, checking the pale stems for bruises.

"If the stand opens, I'll cook," he says.

Rin nods and writes his name down. By evening, two older men have called her a traitor. You make dinner when she misses it and leave her plate covered on the stove. When she gets home, you listen.

On Sunday, you visit an apartment near Tennoji. Rin likes the light in the kitchen. At the door, she says, "Next time, my name goes first. Even if they say no."

That night, a date appears on her calendar. The last meeting.

## Four hours outside
You wait in the parked car for four hours. Streetlight slides across the windshield whenever a truck passes. The bento Rin packed that morning sits on the passenger seat, its rice cold now. You keep your phone face up. She doesn't call.

At last, the building door opens. Rin comes out alone and gets into the car. Her hands rest on her knees while you wait for her to speak.

"The family is finished," she says.

You look at the dark windows above you. "Are you okay?"

"Some of them won't speak to me." She glances at the lunchbox. "He stayed. He'll cook."

A week later, she returns the office keys to its owner. When the noodle stand opens in Shinsekai, the man with the scar serves you both steaming bowls. Rin puts cash on the counter, and he gives her the change.

On the walk home, she says, "Leaving doesn't mean people welcome you back."

Rin reaches for a pen above the apartment application, then sets it down. In the morning, she signs. The landlord says no.

## The years after
On laundry nights, Rin still folds your shirts in thirds. Your mother finally flies to Osaka, eats with you both, and asks Rin to write down how she makes the miso soup. Your visa renewal comes through, so you stay with Rin. When she leaves first, your bento waits by the door.

The man from the delivery company tells you he can't open an account or buy a car after leaving the group. Local rules can treat a man as a gang member for about five years after he quits. Rin reads the bank forms on the floor beside the laundry basket. She knows her ties may still matter to anyone reviewing her name.

The bank's forms ask about her ties, and nobody can tell her how they will read her answers.

Five years after the office closes, she decides to apply. The night before, she checks every document herself.

"Leave your card at home," she says.

By morning, rain is falling outside the bank in Namba.

## A rainy morning in Namba
Inside the bank, Rin fills in the application slowly. She writes her own name, checks it, and turns to the next box. You sit beside her with your hands in your lap. She keeps the pen until every page is done.

The teller looks through the documents and asks her to come back in a week.

A week later, the teller slides a new passbook across the counter with Rin's name printed on the cover, and gives her a small, routine bow. It's the first bow you've seen her receive that asks her for nothing.

Outside, rain runs along the curb toward a drain. Rin holds out your bento.

"Did you remember the leeks?" you ask.

"Look."

You open the lid. The leeks are packed beside the rice. Rin lets go of the lunchbox as your hand closes around it.
