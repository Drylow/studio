The billionaire adds another folder to the pile in your arms and tells you to get the agreement finished before dinner. He's holding a coffee. You haven't finished lunch. But the clause holding up his deal is one he personally requested, and explaining that may cost you the client who pays your firm to be available.

Imagine advising a fictional wealthy entrepreneur called Marcus Bell. You're an experienced lawyer, and your firm has assigned you to the matter. You aren't his accountant, publicist, or personal assistant, although people will occasionally send you questions intended for all three. The engagement documents define the work you're doing. They become more important when everyone starts assuming who your client must be.

The first meeting happens in a luxurious room beside Marcus's pool. He invites you to sit, then takes a call while you open the papers. His assistant passes through carrying another guest's bag. He says you can talk freely because everyone here works for him. You suggest moving the legal discussion somewhere appropriate rather than testing confidentiality against the people walking through the room.

Marcus finds that unnecessarily formal. He hired a lawyer because the deal matters, and he doesn't want the meeting to feel like a lecture. You move anyway. A comfortable setting doesn't settle who can hear a conversation, and his reassurance doesn't tell you what every person nearby has been authorized to receive.

The American Bar Association's model rules treat confidentiality as a professional obligation with specific conditions and exceptions. They're model rules, not a promise that the law is identical everywhere. The useful point for this meeting is simpler. You need to handle the information appropriately before a problem exists, not explain afterward that the house was full of friendly people.

Inside the office, Marcus opens the papers for a company acquisition. Your firm's engagement is with the buying company. Marcus can give its instructions within the agreed authority; he isn't your separate client on every personal matter. You put the draft beside the engagement letter. He looks at the pile and asks whether all that paper is why the deal isn't finished yet.

He points to the clause allowing his company to keep using the seller's warehouse after completion. Marcus requested the access. Now he wants the end date removed, because his next site isn't ready. The seller needs the space back. You ask how long the company really needs and who has checked the move. He stops tapping the paragraph and calls the colleague managing it.

The ABA's model rule on the allocation of authority distinguishes a client's objectives from the lawyer's professional role in pursuing them. Marcus can decide what he's trying to achieve within the engagement. That doesn't require you to pretend every method he suggests is sensible, permitted, or likely to be accepted. You explain the choices rather than turning a marked-up page into a personal contest.

The assistant sends a file described as the latest version. The other party's adviser refers to a different set of changes. You compare the documents through the firm's process before giving Marcus an answer about what either side has accepted. A filename containing final isn't proof that the contents reflect the current instructions.

The inconsistency is ordinary, not evidence of a plot. Someone circulated a revision while another person was still commenting on the previous one. You identify the version being discussed and confirm the changes that remain open. Marcus wants to know whether this means starting again. You explain the specific work needed rather than presenting version control as an endless legal mystery.

A colleague flags a tax assumption in the proposed structure. You bring in the firm's tax specialist before telling Marcus the clause can solve it. He asks why he is paying several lawyers to answer one question. The specialist explains which question is theirs. Marcus turns back to you and asks whether the two answers can be put on one page. That request, you can support.

The specialist's answer affects the business choice without making it for Marcus. You explain it in plain language and check that he understands the consequence, rather than burying it in a forwarded message. He asks a practical question you hadn't anticipated. You take it back for clarification. That takes time and prevents a mistaken impression from reaching the next instruction.

For a while, the discussion improves. Marcus identifies the business concern behind the clause. You propose an approach for the team to assess. The specialist lawyer asks for a missing document, and his assistant finds it. You can see why people enjoy working with someone who makes decisions quickly when the relevant information is actually in front of him.

Then his business partner joins by telephone. The partner dislikes the revised approach and wants a different protection. Marcus says it's effectively the same thing. It isn't. The promises would apply to different circumstances, and the other party would evaluate them differently. You need a decision from the appropriate person before you can send instructions that the team can rely on.

The conversation exposes the client-identity problem you checked at the start. The firm is representing the party defined in the engagement, not automatically every person who knows Marcus or shares a business interest with him. You clarify the position and consult the appropriate colleague about what the new request requires. Nobody gets personal advice simply because they're on speakerphone with someone paying a substantial bill.

That pause makes you unpopular. Marcus says the lawyers are making an easy deal difficult. You describe the concrete disagreement rather than defending your profession in general. He and the partner want different wording because they are worried about different consequences. A faster email won't make the disagreement disappear. They need to resolve the business instruction or choose not to proceed on those terms.

He asks what you would do. You explain your professional view, including the disadvantages of the available options. This is where the work becomes less pleasant than the impressive office suggests. You have to give advice the client may dislike while remaining clear about which decisions are ultimately theirs to make.

The ABA's rule on advisers calls for independent judgment and candid advice. The word candid does real work in a room where everyone would prefer reassurance. You don't dramatize your concern to force a decision. You also don't soften it until the client hears something you cannot honestly support.

Marcus chooses a negotiating position. You confirm it, send it through the appropriate team, and wait for the other side's response. His assistant brings food, which you eat beside a stack of papers without feeling like the luxury has quite reached your part of the room. Marcus asks whether you could review another matter while you're waiting.

The spare folder concerns Marcus's personal property purchase. It belongs to neither the company acquisition nor the advice your firm has agreed to give today. You send a short description to the colleague handling new engagements and leave the folder closed. Marcus says you could at least read it while waiting. Your lunch is still beside your laptop, also waiting for you to find spare time.

The response from the other side arrives while you're discussing that. They will consider an alternative clause, but not the version Marcus expected them to accept. He wants you to tell them the deal is off unless they agree. You explain what that message would mean and ask whether he is prepared to follow through. He says they'll know he's serious. You ask the question again.

Marcus suggests sending an excerpt of the other party's correspondence to someone who might help persuade them. You ask who would receive it and for what purpose. You need to consider the actual duties, authority, and circumstances before information is shared. You don't treat the word helpful as sufficient permission for a new recipient to join a confidential matter.

The friend owns another business and likes giving Marcus opinions about deals. You ask whether the acquisition team has authorized this disclosure and why the friend needs the other side's email. Marcus admits he mainly wants someone to agree that the seller is being difficult. You can explain the problem without forwarding confidential correspondence to recruit an ally. His assistant takes their finger off the send button.

His assistant looks grateful when you make the decision clear. They were already preparing to forward the message because Marcus sounded certain. You help them identify which communication is authorized and what remains restricted. You don't give the assistant personal legal advice about unrelated questions that the exchange happens to remind them of.

For the next call, Marcus removes the friend and adds the finance colleague who can explain the company's cash position. The colleague answers a question the lawyers couldn't settle from the draft alone. Marcus sees why the seller wants protection and proposes a narrower one. You send that for consideration. Having the right person in the conversation has changed the offer more than another confident opinion would have.

You aren't trying to make him timid. The instruction has a consequence. If the other side walks away, the people doing this deal need to have understood that was an outcome he accepted. After a pause, he authorizes a narrower response that communicates his position without pretending the discussion has already ended.

An awkward timing issue follows. Marcus asks whether a document can be dated as though it was completed earlier. You ask what he means. There may be a legitimate way to document an agreed effective date, depending on the actual facts and applicable law, but you won't make a false statement about when something was signed or approved. The team needs to handle the distinction correctly.

He says he doesn't want a lecture about fraud. You haven't accused him of it. You have identified a request that needs a clear, lawful form. The ABA model rule prohibits assisting conduct a lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent. You keep the conversation at that level and refer the specific drafting issue to the appropriate expertise rather than offering him a clever way to hide the facts.

Marcus becomes quiet. This is the moment you worried about when he handed you the extra folder. Being valued for solving problems can make it feel dangerous to identify one you won't solve in the requested way. You explain the legitimate options the team can evaluate and give him room to choose among them.

He chooses to proceed with accurate documentation. That doesn't make the afternoon easy again. The change affects timing, and the other side still needs to agree the outstanding terms. You update the team without announcing a victory over the client. They need a usable instruction, not a story about how bravely you delivered it.

Dinner is approaching. Marcus asks whether everything will be finished beforehand. You give the current position rather than the answer he'd like to take into the dining room. A response is outstanding. The team can continue the agreed work, but you cannot guarantee when a separate party will decide. He leaves for dinner irritated at a delay he no longer describes as your refusal to try.

While he eats, you speak with the other advisers. The remaining disagreement is smaller now that both sides understand the business issue. It still matters. You compare the revised wording with the actual instruction and ask the specialist to confirm their part. Nobody signs off simply because everybody has been looking at the matter for a long time.

The tax specialist returns with the consequence Marcus had hoped was already covered. It isn't. You place the explanation beside the draft and show which option would change it. Marcus asks why nobody mentioned this before. You point to the question left open in the earlier note, then stop defending the note. He needs to choose with the answer now available, even if both choices irritate him.

You clarify the position before asking for the final decision. Marcus dislikes the consequence but understands the available alternatives. He chooses to proceed on the revised basis after the appropriate advice. You record the instruction and tell the team which point has been confirmed. The file now contains a decision, not a collection of reassuring statements everyone might interpret differently.

The workable version arrives later. You read it against the agreed changes rather than assuming it is correct because it looks familiar. A small inconsistency remains. You send it back, explain the exact point, and wait for the corrected version. The task is unglamorous enough that it is easy to see why a client might think you're being slow. It is also the task you were hired to do.

A junior colleague sends you a question about the new wording. You could answer quickly from memory, but you open the current draft and check the point. They have noticed something worth clarifying. You explain the decision and show which version records it, rather than making them feel that raising a question is an admission they shouldn't be working on the matter.

You remember how easy it is for the client's urgency to become pressure on everyone below you in the team. You're responsible for handling that appropriately too. Having spent the day resisting an unsupported promise to Marcus doesn't justify demanding unsupported certainty from a colleague because you'd like to leave for dinner.

You tell them what is confirmed and what still requires review. A further specialist answer arrives and is incorporated through the proper process. You check the revised result, then confirm the position with the relevant people. The work advances because somebody asked a precise question, not because everyone performed absolute confidence until the signing began.

Marcus returns and asks whether he can sign. You explain what has been agreed, the issue that remains relevant to him, and the consequences he is accepting. He asks a better question than the ones he asked at the pool. What happens if the other party doesn't perform? You take him through the relevant position without promising enforcement will be effortless or that the paperwork can remove every risk.

He decides to proceed with the properly completed agreement. The appropriate parties follow the signing process, and the team confirms the completed documents. There's no dramatic courtroom moment. Nobody delivers a confession. A negotiation has reached terms the parties actually agreed to, recorded in a form your team can support.

As you're packing, Marcus says he appreciates that you didn't let the process get away from him. You remember several points when he wanted you to move faster than the process allowed. You accept the thanks without rewriting the day into an easy relationship. Your firm's next discussion with him will still need clear scope, fees, responsibility, and availability.

He reaches for the spare folder he gave you earlier. You tell him which colleague is reviewing the new engagement and what information they need. He starts to hand it to you, thinks about that answer, and places it on his own desk. You leave carrying the completed file you were actually asked to handle.
