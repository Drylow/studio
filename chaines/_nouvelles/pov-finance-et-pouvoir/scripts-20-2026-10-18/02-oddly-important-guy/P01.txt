Your new boss hands you his phone while you're carrying his paperwork, his coffee, and the problem he gave you before breakfast. He's still in a dressing gown. He wants to know why you're looking stressed. But the difficult part of working for a billionaire is going to be deciding which of his instructions you're allowed to ignore.

You put the coffee on the nearest table before taking Adrian Vale's phone. He has a message open about another appointment. You ask which meeting it replaces. He says it doesn't replace anything. In the calendar, both are now occupying the same hour. Somewhere behind you, the coffee starts leaving a ring on a table you haven't yet learned whether you're allowed to use.

The phone contains a message from someone Adrian wants to see today. You open his calendar. Today already contains a meeting, a lunch, a flight, and a meeting he accepted while someone else was arranging the lunch. You ask which appointment should move. He says you know what's important. You've worked here since Monday. You know where the printer paper lives.

The salary looked excellent when the recruiter described it. You heard travel, access, responsibility. You thought about leaving your cramped old office and working somewhere decisions happened quickly. They do happen quickly here. Unfortunately, different decisions happen quickly in different rooms, and nobody has told the calendar.

Maya, the senior assistant, sees you staring at the screen. She asks what he actually approved. You show her the message. She asks again. Apparently, mentioning somebody over breakfast and confirming a meeting with them are different things. You have treated the first as the second, and their office is already arranging a car.

Your first rescue involves a phone call you hoped you wouldn't have to make. You say the appointment is still being confirmed. The other assistant pauses, then thanks you for checking before their principal leaves. You expected a confrontation. Instead, you've discovered another person doing your exact job at the other end of an expensive misunderstanding.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes executive support in terms of calendars, documents, research, and coordination. That sounds manageable when you read it as a list. Try doing the calendar while somebody changes the documents, then coordinating people who believe they've each received the final instruction. You begin keeping a record of decisions, not just requests.

By lunchtime, Adrian has forgotten the extra meeting. He asks whether you found the cufflinks he mentioned in the car. You haven't been in the car. He told the driver, who told the house manager, who thought you were handling it. The house manager is now searching a guest room while a plumber waits outside a locked service gate.

You could join the search. It would make you look helpful, and it would leave the plumber standing there while the repair appointment disappears. You ask the driver where the cufflinks were last seen, give that information to the house manager, and call the employee who controls access. Three people can finish their own jobs if you stop trying to perform all of them.

That works until Adrian decides he needs the cufflinks for tonight. There was no tonight on the calendar. There is now a dinner at the house, and somebody has already invited guests. You ask how many. He names people rather than a number. One of those people travels with an assistant, another might bring their partner, and the chef has started preparing a meal for a different group.

You write the names down and ask the chef what must be confirmed before preparation continues. You don't promise that everyone will eat the same thing. The chef asks about dietary requirements, seating, and timing. You're relieved to receive questions with answers that can actually be recorded. Then Adrian calls from the pool and asks why the florist hasn't arrived.

The florist knows nothing about a dinner. You have the email confirming flowers for tomorrow's meeting. Adrian remembers asking for flowers, so to him this sounds like the same request. You could tell the florist that they made a mistake. It would save you an awkward conversation now and make the next conversation with them much worse.

You tell Adrian the florist can supply the smaller arrangement today. 'I asked for the large one,' he says. You agree. He looks at you, waiting for that to make another option appear. You explain the collection time and hold out the photograph of what is available. Maya watches from the doorway. You keep the photograph still until he points at it and says that one will do.

He chooses the smaller one. The pause before that decision felt longer than the entire phone call. You had assumed good service meant never presenting a limit. Maya says he can't choose between options nobody tells him about. Then she gives you the next limit. The driver is already taking a guest to an appointment and cannot collect flowers at the same time.

You arrange a separate collection through the house manager, who confirms the cost before anyone leaves. It's an ordinary decision, but now you understand why those confirmations matter. Access to a wealthy person's suppliers doesn't automatically give you permission to commit their money, change their staff's schedule, or move a guest's appointment.

The florist's invoice arrives with a charge for collecting hired vases from last week's event. The office thinks the house approved it. The house thinks the office did. On the phone, the florist reads back the message asking them to return. You can hear a delivery van running behind them. They have already done the work while your employer's staff are still deciding whose question it is.

You find Adrian's message asking for the vases to be collected, attached to a different conversation about the dinner. You send it to the office employee who approves the invoice and tell the florist who is handling the answer. They stop asking you to chase someone. They ask you to email the job number. It is the first request all afternoon you can finish without approaching a pool.

The house manager opens your notebook and cannot tell which dinner the vase collection belongs to. You can, because you remember the conversation. She asks what happens if you're ill tomorrow. You put the date, event, and approval together in the shared record. The next message concerns cufflinks, and this time it goes under Adrian's morning departure instead of existing as a mysterious word beside a flower order.

That discovery changes your next conversation with Adrian. When he asks for a change, you confirm the decision against the actual arrangement and explain the consequence if there is one. You keep it brief. You aren't requesting a formal ceremony every time he changes his mind about lunch. You're making the difference between a passing thought and an instruction visible to the people expected to act.

Late afternoon brings a document Adrian wants signed before dinner. He tells you to send it to the lawyer and get it finished. You can send it. You can't tell the lawyer what their review will find, or tell the other party to accept terms they haven't seen. You record the deadline as a request rather than a promise.

He asks whether you called. You did. He asks whether they know it's urgent. They do. You want to explain that urgency doesn't create an answer, but the useful response is more specific. The lawyer is waiting for a missing attachment. You find the employee who has it, get permission to forward it, and send the complete file. The review can finally start.

There is something satisfying about that part. You solve a real blockage instead of performing concern in front of it. The lawyer replies with a question. Adrian is having a conversation he doesn't want interrupted. You have to decide whether the question can wait. This is the responsibility you wanted in the interview, and it has arrived without looking particularly glamorous.

You check what the question affects. If it waits, the other party may leave before responding. If you interrupt, Adrian will be annoyed for a moment. You approach, explain that single consequence, and ask for the decision. He answers. You leave before he can hand you an unrelated request halfway through the reply.

Maya smiles at that. You aren't getting paid to absorb every unfinished thought within arm's reach. You're helping work reach a decision. Sometimes that means listening patiently. Sometimes it means returning with a question that feels irritating because it requires a choice. The people around you need that choice more than they need another reassuring message.

The guests begin arriving early. One gives security a different name from the one on your list. They know Adrian, and they're offended by the delay. You're tempted to wave them through because you recognize their face. Instead, you contact the appropriate member of the household team and verify the invitation through the agreed process.

The guard doesn't need you inventing an exception at the gate. The guest doesn't need an audience for their irritation. You move the conversation aside, get the confirmation, and pass it to the team. They handle access. You handle the invitation information. Nobody turns the moment into a story about how important the guest is.

Inside, a seat is missing. The guest who might bring a partner has brought one. The kitchen can adjust the meal, but the dining room needs to know before service begins. You pass the change directly to the person running the room, then check that the kitchen received the same version. You're learning to ask for confirmation without making every interaction sound like an interrogation.

Adrian notices the smaller flowers. He asks why they aren't the ones he wanted. You tell him which choice he made and why, without reciting the whole difficult afternoon. He looks at them, decides they're fine, and goes back to his guests. All that anxiety over a decision he has already stopped thinking about.

Dinner nearly starts on time. You stand in the service corridor with a cold sandwich and hear the people inside compliment how relaxed the evening feels. The chef is checking the next course. The house manager is finding a coat. The driver is confirming the return journey. You have seen the work behind that relaxed room, and none of it belongs to you alone.

Your phone lights up. Adrian wants to change tomorrow's departure. You open the schedule and find the appointment that will be affected. This time, you don't immediately move it. You send him the two arrangements that are possible, along with the consequence of each. He picks one. You confirm the change with the relevant people before touching anything else.

For a moment, you think you've cracked the job. Then a guest asks whether you can arrange a weekend for their family. It sounds flattering. They trust you. It also sounds like a second employer arriving in the middle of your first employer's dinner. You say you'll check the scope with the house manager rather than casually accepting another person's entire holiday.

An invitation arrives for an event Adrian would enjoy. The sender asks whether he will attend and mentions that they could include you as well. It sounds like one of the benefits you imagined when you took the job. You show Maya, expecting advice about what to wear. She asks whether you're being invited as a guest or needed as his working assistant.

Maya asks what you think you are going there to do. You describe the guests you might meet. She asks who will manage Adrian's schedule during the evening. You stop describing the guests. She has attended events that looked like privileges in photographs and kept her standing in a corridor through dinner. You ask the sender what the invitation includes before looking for something suitable to wear.

You clarify it. Adrian wants you there to manage his evening, not enjoy an independently planned night out. You can discuss the assignment, but you stop counting it as leisure when you explain your week to Rowan at home. Rowan asks whether that changes the plans you already made together. You say you'll check the working arrangement before answering for both of you.

The event can be covered by an agreed rota without using the evening you'd already protected. You are relieved and slightly embarrassed by how ready you were to trade it away for a glimpse of a room you could later describe to friends. You're still curious about the work. You also want your own plans to remain plans after somebody wealthier develops a new one.

After the guests leave, Maya asks when you're going home. You say when everything is done. She looks at the phone. It will still contain requests when you wake up. She helps you separate the items that require a decision tonight from the ones that can be handed over or scheduled. There's an actual late-duty arrangement. You should have asked about it during induction.

The next morning, Adrian remembers the dinner as effortless. He compliments the evening, then asks why a request from last night hasn't been completed. You show him where it sits behind the task he chose to prioritize. You expect him to object. He considers the list and changes the order. The change creates work, but at least everyone is working from the same order now.

Your bank sends an ordinary notification about one of your own bills. You open it while waiting for a supplier to answer about something costing far more than anything in your personal budget. The contrast is briefly absurd. You have spent the day discussing decisions involving another person's wealth, and your own finances remain exactly the ones you brought through the staff entrance.

A friend asks whether working here means you're getting rich too. You tell them you receive the salary and terms you agreed, not a share of every impressive thing you've helped arrange. The job may offer experience and future opportunities, but those need evaluating as real possibilities rather than silently adding the employer's lifestyle to your compensation.

You complete the personal payment from your own account, check the supplier's answer, and return to the work. The small interruption helps keep the difference clear. You can be good at managing someone else's priorities without allowing every description of their success to become an assumption about what the role is doing for you.

By the end of the week, you're less impressed by the driveway and more impressed by Maya's ability to end a conversation with a clear next action. You still enjoy parts of the job. The responsibility is real, the problems vary, and sometimes your work genuinely saves somebody a miserable afternoon. You also understand why the role needs more than gratitude and a generous salary.

You ask for a discussion about availability, cover, and the requests you're authorized to approve. The conversation isn't comfortable. Adrian liked being able to reach whoever answered fastest. You explain the mistakes that happen when everyone treats everything as immediate. Maya brings examples, and the house manager describes what the team can actually support.

You don't leave with every Friday free or a promise that the phone will never ring late. You leave with a rota, an escalation contact, and agreement on which decisions need Adrian's approval. Those arrangements will have to survive a busy week before you trust them. Still, they're more useful than being told you're part of the family when you need somebody to cover your evening.

On your way out, Adrian calls you back toward the pool. He's holding his phone again. You take out your notebook and ask what he'd like to move. He starts describing a new appointment, stops, and asks what it would replace. You turn the calendar toward him. For once, the next person carrying this decision is him.
