You wanted to be a shield-maiden. A painted shield, a sword, your braids flying as you charge into battle. Instead, it is barely light, your fingers are numb, and you are watching your husband's ship row out of the fjord without you.

Somebody has to stay behind. Somebody always does. This summer, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Aslaug, the wife of Eirik from our last two chapters, on a farm on the western coast of Norway in the tenth century. Like Eirik, you are an imagined person, built from what archaeology, laws and written sources tell us, not a real woman from history.

Eirik is rowing for Bjorn on a raid across the sea. You have heard the songs about men like him. You have also noticed who stays home with the cows.

Popular culture loves the Viking woman with a sword. And to be fair, Norse women had some rights that were unusual for their time. We will get to those. But your day does not start with a battle. It starts with a list.

Milk the cows. Feed the household. Check the grain. Spin. Weave. Watch the sky. Spin again. Somewhere on that list there is supposed to be time for sword practice. There is not.

And at the end of this story, we will open a real grave in Sweden that made headlines around the world, and ask what scholars actually concluded about warrior women.

The moment the sail disappears, the farm is yours. The National Museum of Denmark puts it simply: when a man was called on an expedition, responsibility for the farm was handed over to the woman.

Not the glory. The responsibility. The animals, the stores, the fields, the repairs. If the roof leaks, it is your roof now. To be honest, it has been your roof for a while.

Old poetry gives you a symbol for this. A poem called Rígsþula describes a farmer's bride arriving at her new home with keys hanging from her. For a long time, keys were read as the badge of the Viking housewife.

The graves complicate that. Keys turn up in only about one woman's grave in twenty, according to the National Museum of Denmark. And in Vestfold, in southern Norway, the archaeologist Marianne Moen found keys in almost as many men's graves as women's.

Some, like the Swedish History Museum, still see the key as a sign of authority over the household. Others suspect it meant something else entirely. The archaeologist Pernille Pantmann has suggested that keys marked a special role held by only a few, perhaps a wise woman, someone with knowledge others lacked. Either way, your authority does not come from a key. It comes from the fact that if you stop working, everyone eats less.

Scholars broadly agree on this much: a woman's power was strongest inside the house. Out in public, men ran things.

You find that out when your neighbor returns to his favorite subject: the boundary. With Eirik away, he suggests that the marker stone may have, somehow, wandered onto your land.

Can you take him to the assembly yourself? Probably not. In Iceland's law book, written down later, a woman could not bring a case unless a man prosecuted it for her. The National Museum of Denmark says women could not appear in court.

Rules varied from place to place, and historians still debate how strictly they applied in Norway. But for a married woman, the usual road to justice ran through a man.

So the boundary waits for Eirik. Your neighbor, meanwhile, waits for nothing.

While you wait, think back to how you got here. You did not meet Eirik on a cliff at sunset. Your marriage was arranged between families, and it was very much about property.

Eirik paid a bride-price, a gift that in the law became your own property. Your father provided a dowry. Both families counted carefully. Romance was optional. The paperwork was not, even without any paper.

Who did the negotiating? Fathers and male relatives. The bride was often asked, but not always. As the scholar Else Mundal sums up the laws of Norway and Iceland, a woman could not choose the man she married, unless she was a widow. Which leads to an uncomfortable conclusion: the woman with the most say over her own marriage was usually one who had already buried a husband.

A saga tells of Gudrun, an Icelandic woman promised in marriage at fifteen. Her father settled the contract. The saga says plainly that she was not asked about it, and that she was against it.

But here is where the Viking world surprises people. You could leave. A wife could seek a divorce by gathering witnesses and declaring her complaint and her intention to end the marriage. And she could claim her dowry back.

Gudrun used that right. In the saga, her husband slaps her during an argument. A friend advises her to make him a shirt with a neckline so low that it will give her grounds for divorce. That spring, she announces the divorce and goes home. When the estate is divided, she leaves with half of all the property.

In the same saga, a man divorces his wife on the grounds that she made herself breeches cut like a man's. In the saga world, the wrong clothes could end a marriage. Choose your sewing carefully.

The right itself was real enough to impress outsiders. A traveller from Muslim Spain who visited the trading town of Hedeby in the nine hundreds is reported to have said that women there could divorce whenever they wished. Some historians suspect he exaggerated. Still, by the standards of the time, a wife's right to leave was remarkable.

Before you celebrate, read the rest of the contract. Under the laws of both Norway and Iceland, a daughter inherited half as much as a son.

So yes, you have rights. Some of them were unusual for the age. They just come with a smaller share, a man's voice at the assembly, and a great deal of spinning.

Which brings us to the real engine of the Viking Age. It is not a sword. It is a stick with a weight on the end.

Every thread on this farm starts as a tuft of wool. You clean it and prepare it, then spin it on a drop spindle, a weighted stick twirling below your hand. Then you weave it on an upright loom, its threads pulled tight by hanging weights. You spin while you watch the porridge, while you talk, while you walk to the cowshed. The spindle goes wherever you go.

This was women's work. The National Museum of Denmark notes that finds point to textile production as women's work, and medieval sources suggest women handled every step, from gathering the wool to weaving the cloth.

Everyone's clothes come from your hands: tunics, cloaks, blankets, and the shirt Eirik is currently ruining at sea.

And then there is the sail. That great square sail carrying Eirik's ship across the sea? Someone made it, thread by thread.

How long did that take? Nobody kept a timesheet, so we only have estimates. Researchers at the Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde, working with Viking-style spindles and looms, estimated that five people could spin and weave the cloth for one reconstructed cargo ship's sail in about a year.

The archaeologist Lise Bender Jørgensen, who studies ancient textiles, went further. Counting the sail and the other textiles a crew needed at sea, she estimated around ten years of work for a cargo ship. For a warship with sixty-five to seventy men: fifty to sixty years of work, and more than one and a half tonnes of wool.

Those are estimates, and they vary. But they all point the same way. Behind every famous sail stood years of women's work, and a remarkable number of sheep.

Cloth was so valuable that in medieval Iceland, a little after your time, it became money. The law fixed the length and width of a standard woollen cloth called vaðmál, and people paid taxes and fines with it.

The archaeologist Michèle Hayeur Smith points out the irony. Women had no say in making those laws, yet they wove the cloth that the whole legal system ran on.

Eirik will come home talking about the sea. He will not mention the loom. Nobody ever mentions the loom.

At the end of summer, he does come home, with a cut arm, a small pouch of silver and a lot of stories. You look at the arm first. Then the pouch.

There is no time for a feast. Autumn is the season of hard choices. Some animals will be fed through the winter. Others must be slaughtered now.

Everything that can be kept, is kept. Grain, berries and vegetables are dried. Meat is dried, smoked or salted. Fish is dried and stacked. Milk becomes butter and cheese, precious fat for the long winter.

You count, and then you count again. The National Museum of Denmark notes that housekeeping had to be planned around the seasons. Winter is when you find out whether the plan was any good.

Then there is the silver in Eirik's pouch. You know where some of it came from. Two captives, sold at a trading place on the way home.

This is the part of the Viking story with no heroes. Free women like you had limited rights. Enslaved women and men could be bought and sold, and the National Museum of Denmark describes them as people without any personal rights.

On a richer farm down the valley, you can see what that means. Enslaved people do the heaviest work: mending fences, spreading dung on the fields, herding pigs and sheep, cutting turf.

The poem Rígsþula imagines the family of a thrall and hands them exactly those jobs. It is a poem about society, not a record of real people. But the list is grimly practical, and the Swedish History Museum describes thralls' work in almost the same words.

Most enslaved people left almost no trace in the ground, apart from the occasional iron collar or shackle. You do not own anyone. But you live in a world that does, and some of its silver now sits in your storeroom.

The days shrink. The hearth smokes. The stores go down a little faster than you planned. By midwinter, you know you are expecting a child. Here, that news comes with joy, and with fear.

Childbirth was one of the most dangerous things a woman in the past could do. Archaeologists studying early medieval England have called it the biggest single cause of death for women. The archaeologist Marianne Hem Eriksen stresses that maternal mortality was very high in pre-industrial societies.

Strangely, Viking graves barely show it. Eriksen and her colleague Katherine Olley went through the catalogues of Viking Age graves. Out of thousands, they found only fourteen where a woman might have been buried with a newborn or unborn child.

Not because mothers were safe. Babies are missing from the burial record in general, and mothers and infants seem usually not to have been buried together. Where those babies went, we still do not fully know.

There is no doctor coming. There are women who have done this before. In late spring, a neighbor woman who has helped at many births comes to stay. Your labor lasts through a night and a morning.

Your daughter is born alive. So are you. For a moment, that is the whole victory.

Then the worrying begins. Across pre-modern societies, researchers estimate that about a quarter of babies died in their first year, and around half of all children never reached adulthood. There is no exact figure for Viking Norway, but the National Museum of Denmark is blunt: infant mortality was high.

Runestones carry the same grief in a few hard words. Near Stockholm, a runic inscription carved on a rock tells the story of a woman named Geirlaug. She was married as a girl. Her first husband drowned, and their son died.

With her second husband, she had children, but only one girl lived: Inga. Inga married, lost her husband and then her son. When Inga died, her mother Geirlaug inherited from her. The carving is a family tree written in loss, and a record of who inherited what.

Your own body keeps score as well. Viking Age skeletons show arthritis in backs, hands and knees, the mark of years of heavy work.

Long lives were rare. A study of adult skeletons from the Danish town of Ribe found that women of the Viking period who survived childhood died, on average, at about thirty-nine. The National Museum of Denmark notes that only a few people lived past fifty.

Early summer brings the merchant back to the landing, with his scales and his opinions about your cloth.

You bargain hard. Trade was not only a man's business. In Trøndelag, in central Norway, balance scales, the tool of a trader, come mostly from men's graves, but a few women were buried with them too. The archaeologist Elisabeth Nordbladh points to women's graves with scales and weights as far away as Russia.

What did those scales mean? Perhaps these women traded themselves. Perhaps the scales belonged to the family, or meant something else. Archaeologists are careful here: an object in a grave does not always tell you what its owner did in life. Remember that. It will matter in a moment.

Some women went very far. A saga tells of Gudrid, who sailed west to the land the Norse called Vinland and gave birth to a son there. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, a Norse site from some decades after your time, archaeologists found a spindle whorl and a bone needle, signs that women were there too.

Other women left their mark in stone. Many runestones say that a woman raised them, a very public act in a world where women rarely spoke in public.

At Hadeland in Norway, about a century after your time, a mother named Gunnvor built a bridge in memory of her daughter Astrid, and raised a stone to say so. It ends with a mother's boast: she was the handiest maiden in Hadeland.

And the richest burial of the Viking Age, according to the National Museum of Denmark, was made for women. In eight hundred and thirty-four, two women, one elderly and one around fifty, were laid to rest in the great Oseberg ship in Norway.

So yes, some Norse women owned land, traded, travelled, ran households and were honored in stone. The catch is the word some. Most women's lives looked a lot more like yours.

Which brings us back to the sword. In eighteen seventy-eight, archaeologists excavated a grave at Birka, a Viking Age trading town in Sweden. Today it is known as grave B J five eighty-one.

It held a sword, an axe, two spears, armour-piercing arrows, a fighting knife, two shields, two horses and a full set of gaming pieces. For more than a century, the person inside was assumed to be a man.

An earlier study of the bones had already suggested a woman. In twenty seventeen, DNA settled it: the person in the warrior's grave was biologically female.

Critics first asked whether those bones really came from that grave, after more than a century in storage. The team answered with a detailed review of the old excavation records, and published a longer study in twenty nineteen.

The research team concluded that she most likely lived as a professional warrior of high rank, while saying openly that many other interpretations were possible.

Other scholars are less sure. The historian Judith Jesch pointed out that weapons in a grave do not prove that someone fought, and the skeleton shows no injuries from battle. Others have suggested that weapons could be a symbol, an heirloom, or a statement about family status.

So what do scholars actually conclude? That one woman at Birka was buried as a warrior. Whether she fought is still debated. And real evidence for women like her, as the research team itself wrote, has been sparse.

So why was being a Viking woman worse than you think? Because the shield-maiden was rare, and the loom was everywhere. You ran the farm without the title, held rights with a smaller share, and faced the most dangerous day of your life with no song written about it.

And yet, it was not all darkness. You could own property, leave a bad marriage, raise a stone, and run the household that kept everyone alive. Many women elsewhere in Europe at the time had fewer choices.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. Eirik will pretend not to listen. You will check the stores, the cradle and the loom, in that order. And somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped again. This is Viking Chapters: the lives behind the legends.
