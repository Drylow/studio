## How to read these notes

Each fact is tagged with the kind of evidence behind it, so the script never presents one kind as another:

- **[POEM]**: Eddic poetry (*Rígsþula*). The script calls it "a poem about society, not a school timetable".
- **[SAGA]**: Icelandic sagas written in the 1200s, reached here through scholarship. The script says "the sagas", "a saga", "In the sagas".
- **[LATER LAW]**: Icelandic law and the conversion compromise of about 1000, as reported later (*Íslendingabók*) and summarised by scholars.
- **[ARCH]**: archaeology (toys, skates).
- **[MUSEUM]**: a museum's summary of the evidence.
- **[SCHOLARSHIP]**: a historian's article, a reference site that cites sagas and laws (Hurstwic), or an encyclopedia.
- **[ESTIMATE]**: demographic estimates (from episode 3).

Numbers in the script are written as words. Each was checked against at least two sources. Where a detail rests on one source, the script names that source in the sentence ("The National Museum of Denmark points out", "Hurstwic retells a saga", "Patzuk-Russell notes") and the general claim behind it has a second, independent source (see each entry), or the detail was cut (see "Cut or softened").

## What is fiction

Thora (the daughter born in episode 3, named here), her age, clothes and green bead (the gift from episode 5), the little brother who lived one winter, the chores and their order, the dog, the pine toy boat with a sheep on board, the skating scenes, Orm (Hallvard's grandson) and his fostering, Colm carrying Orm's chest, Asmund's visit, and every scene of Thora's year are invented. P03 says so ("you are an imagined person"). The story runs from about 960 (Thora is nine, born around 951 in episode 3) through about a year. Every historical example is labelled with its place or date (Dublin's Winetavern Street, Birka, Fjörður in Iceland 940 to 1000, Iceland's conversion around 1000).

## Facts used

### F01: The newborn was accepted by the mother nursing it and the father taking it on his knee, naming it and sprinkling it with water [SCHOLARSHIP + POEM]
- **Used in:** P08 to P10.
- **Sources:**
  1. Hurstwic, *Demographics and Life Events in the Viking Age*: https://hurstwic.com/history/articles/daily_living/text/Demographics.htm ("The father showed acceptance by taking the infant onto his knee", then "giving the child a name, and sprinkling water on the child"; the mother accepted the child by nursing it).
  2. *Rígsþula*, st. 6, Olive Bray translation, ed. D. L. Ashliman, University of Pittsburgh: https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/rig.html ("with water they sprinkled him, called him Thrall").
  3. *Rígsþula*, Henry Adams Bellows translation (1923) with notes, wevikings.com: https://wevikings.com/?p=11259 (st. 7 note: the Norse custom of sprinkling children with water predates Christianity).
- **Caveat:** Hurstwic is a history and reenactment organisation that cites sagas and laws; it is named in the sentence ("The history site Hurstwic, drawing on the sagas and laws").

### F02: Newborns were sometimes exposed, usually for deformity or economic hardship; forbidden by law after Christianity; at Iceland's conversion (about 1000) the old law on exposure was at first left in place as a concession, later banned [MUSEUM + SCHOLARSHIP + LATER LAW]
- **Used in:** P11, P12.
- **Sources:**
  1. National Museum of Denmark, *Children in the Viking Age*: https://en.natmus.dk/historical-knowledge/denmark/prehistoric-period-until-1050-ad/the-viking-age/the-people/children/ ("We also know that newborn babies were sometimes put out to die"; "After the introduction of Christianity the putting out of children to die was forbidden by law").
  2. Hurstwic, *Demographics* (F01): "The unwanted baby was put outside, exposed to the elements, until death ensued", usually for birth deformity or economic hardship.
  3. Wikipedia, *Christianization of Iceland*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization_of_Iceland (Þorgeir's decision, reported in Ari's *Íslendingabók*: Christianity adopted "with the condition that old laws concerning the exposure of infants and the eating of horseflesh would remain"; banned "Once the Church was firmly in control in Iceland").
  4. Hurstwic, *Conversion in Iceland*: https://hurstwic.com/history/articles/mythology/religion/text/conversion_in_iceland.htm (*Íslendingabók*: "Some heathen practices could continue in public"; exposure and horse-meat eating continued afterwards; Óláfur Haraldsson, from 1015, convinced Icelanders to end heathen practices).
- **Handling:** No image of exposure, ever. P11 and P12 stay on parchment, an empty cradle in shadow, or the Althing landscape.

### F09: Bone ice skates: cut from the foot bones of horses or cattle, flat and polished underneath, sometimes rubbed with pig fat; pushed along with a pole (often iron-tipped); tied with leather thongs; the Swedish History Museum's pair from Birka; skating probably also for pleasure [ARCH + MUSEUM + SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P32 to P34.
- **Sources:**
  1. Swedish History Museum, *Viking Age ice skates*: https://historiska.se/en/explore-history/history-hub/viking-age-ice-skates/ ("Bone skates were made from the metatarsal bones of horses or cattle"; "instead propelling themselves forward with an ice pole"; "Their undersides were flat and polished to maximise speed"; "Rubbing pig fat on the bottom further reduced friction"; "Bone skates found at Birka, Uppland", AD 800–1100).
  2. ### F11: Fostering: a legal agreement and alliance, not adoption; usually a lower-status family fostered a higher-status child; foster ties as strong as or stronger than blood; children sent to other households to learn skills; Ketilríðr sent away because her mother would not teach her crafts; Njáll taught foster-children law; Ólof trained in fine needlework in Norway [SCHOLARSHIP + SAGA]
- **Used in:** P36 to P43, P46, P47.
- **Sources:**
  1. Hurstwic, *Demographics* (F01): "Fostering was not the same as adoption. It was a legal agreement, and an alliance"; "Ties between foster-relations could be as strong or stronger than those between blood-relations"; typically a child of a higher-status family raised by a lower-status one; Ketilríðr Hólmkelsdóttir sent to be fostered after her mother refused to teach her crafts.
  2. Ryder C. Patzuk-Russell (University of Silesia), *Education in Medieval Iceland*, Medievalists.net (2024): https://www.medievalists.net/2024/10/education-medieval-iceland/ ("One method was to simply send a child to be raised in another household"; "If that household had the capacity to teach important skills to their foster-children"; Njáll "fostered children and taught them law"; in *Víglundar saga*, Ólof "is carefully trained in hannyrðir (fine needlework/embroidery) in Norway").
  3. University of Iceland Science Web (why.is), Q&A distinguishing child fosterage from adult foster-brotherhood: https://why.is/svar.php?id=5432 (search summary: cited, not read).
- **Caveat:** The Ketilríðr, Njáll and Ólof examples are saga material, each attributed in the sentence. Orm's fostering by Eirik's family (lower status) follows Hurstwic's pattern.

### F12: No schools; learning by doing [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P44, P45, P48.
- **Sources:** Patzuk-Russell (F11: education happened through households and fosterage, with learned men teaching foster-children "probably in their free time"); National Museum of Denmark (F02: toys and play trained skills for adult life).
- **Caveat:** "There were no schools on your farm" is limited to the farm; church schools appear in Iceland only after the conversion (not narrated).

### F13: Adulthood: children probably became adults earlier than today; in Iceland males were legally adults at sixteen; by sixteen a boy took on all adult roles; girls often married very young and counted as adults once married; virtually all women married by twenty [MUSEUM + SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P50 to P52.
- **Sources:**
  1. National Museum of Denmark, *Children* (F02): "It is likely that children became adults earlier than they do today"; "In Iceland males were legally adults at the age of 16"; girls "were regarded as adults once they were married", often marrying very young.
  2. Hurstwic, *Demographics* (F01): "By the time a boy was 16 years old, he was expected to take on all the roles of an adult male"; "A male was considered to be an adult after he had passed 15 winters"; "Virtually all women were married by the age of 20."
- **Caveat:** Hurstwic's "perhaps as early as 12" for women's marriage is not narrated. P52 recalls Gudrun at fifteen (episode 3, F10, *Laxdæla saga*).

### F15: The Fjörður figurine, Seyðisfjörður, east Iceland: carved from volcanic tuff, about 5 cm long, dated 940–1000; pig, bear or dog debated; Ragnheiður Traustadóttir: "When you hold the object, it doesn't seem like a dog"; she owned an Icelandic dog; children not very visible, toys seldom survive [ARCH]
