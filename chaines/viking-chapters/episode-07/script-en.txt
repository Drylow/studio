You wanted to be a shield-maiden, like the women in the old stories. A painted shield, a fast ship, your braids flying in the wind. Instead, it is barely light, you are nine years old, your feet are soaked, and you are chasing a sheep through a hole in the fence.

The sheep has escaped again. Somebody has to catch it. Since last spring, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Thora, the daughter of Eirik and Aslaug, born in our chapter on Viking women. Like your parents, you are an imagined person, built from what archaeology, laws and written sources tell us, not a real girl from history.

You have your mother's chestnut hair, in two long braids that never stay tidy, a pale yellow wool dress with a brown belt, and a single green glass bead on a cord around your neck. Your uncle Asmund brought it back from the east. You have never taken it off.

Popular culture likes Viking children as tiny warriors, waving wooden swords and growing up to conquer the world. There were wooden swords. But most of a Viking childhood looked very different: short, busy, and, by our standards, frighteningly dangerous.

Your parents' world, it turns out, starts handing you jobs almost as soon as you can walk. And it expects you to be grown up long before you feel ready.

And at the end of this story, we will look at a tiny stone animal from Iceland, made around your lifetime, that archaeologists still cannot agree on. Is it a pig? A bear? A dog? Hold that thought.

Your first test came before you could remember anything. You passed it simply by being born and being accepted.

The history site Hurstwic, drawing on the sagas and laws, describes the ritual. The mother accepted a newborn by nursing it. The father showed acceptance by taking the baby onto his knee, giving it a name, and sprinkling it with water.

That sprinkling with water was older than Christianity in the north. The poem Rígsþula, which imagines how the classes of society began, describes newborns being sprinkled with water and named. Your mother tells the story of your birth every winter: a long night and a long morning, a neighbor woman who had helped at many births, and a cradle by the hearth. Your father, she likes to say, held you on his knee so carefully that he forgot to breathe.

Not every baby was so lucky. The National Museum of Denmark states it plainly: newborn babies were sometimes put out to die. Hurstwic notes that this was usually done because of a deformity, or because the family simply could not feed another child.

When Iceland accepted Christianity, around the year one thousand, the compromise reached at the Althing at first left the old law on exposing infants in place, as a concession to the pagans. It was banned later, once the Church was firmly in control. The National Museum of Denmark confirms that, after Christianity came, the practice was forbidden by law.

Even accepted babies faced terrible odds. As we saw in our chapter on Viking women, researchers estimate that around half of all children in pre-modern societies never reached adulthood. The National Museum of Denmark is blunt: infant mortality in the Viking period was high.

There was a little brother once. He lived for one winter. Your mother does not talk about him. Your father carved his name into a small piece of wood, and keeps it by the bed.

So, at nine, you are already a survivor. Your reward is a long list of jobs.

The National Museum of Denmark notes that children were put to work from a young age. Indoors, they helped tend the fire and prepare food. Outdoors, they helped with the animals and the fields, and gathered firewood, berries and fruit. Hurstwic agrees: children were expected to work and contribute to the farm.

So your day begins with the fire. You blow on last night's embers until they catch, then fetch water from the stream, twice, because the first time you spilled half of it on the dog.

Then come the animals. Your father has made you the official keeper of the sheep. This is a great honour. It is also because nobody else wants to chase that particular sheep anymore.

By now, you can milk a cow without being kicked, most days. You know which hen hides her eggs under the woodpile, which goat bites, and exactly how far the sheep can run before it gets bored. That is more than most adults in the valley know.

The farm next door has stood empty since Grim, the neighbor, sailed away to Iceland. The famous boundary stone is still there. Your father visits it now and then. He says he is checking the fence. You think he misses the arguments.

In late summer, you pick berries along the hillside, and eat roughly one for every three that go into the basket. In autumn, you gather firewood, small sticks at first, then bigger ones as your arms get stronger.

And always, there is wool. Your mother hands you a spindle almost as tall as your arm, and you start to learn the skill that will fill most of your life.

At first, your thread is lumpy, thin in one place and thick in the next. Your mother unwinds it, without a word, and hands it back. As we saw in our chapter on Viking women, researchers estimate that the cloth for a single ship's sail took years of spinning and weaving. Somewhere out there, a sail is waiting for your thread. It can wait a little longer.

By evening, you are so tired that you fall asleep on the bench before the porridge is finished. Your father carries you to bed. In the morning, the fire needs blowing, the water needs fetching, and the sheep, naturally, has found a new hole in the fence.

What you learned depended on who you were born to. The poem Rígsþula imagines three kinds of childhood. The thrall's children, as we saw in our chapter on thralls, spread dung and dug turf. The free farmer's son, in the poem, learns to break in oxen, build houses and barns, make carts and follow the plough.

The nobleman's son learns something else entirely: to swing the shield, bend the bow, ride horses, loose the hounds, handle a sword and swim. And the youngest noble son, the poem says, alone learned runes. It is a poem about society, not a school timetable. But it is very clear about who got to ride and who got to dig.

You are a free farmer's daughter. The poem gives the farmer's daughters names, but says nothing about what they learned. That tells you something too.

Even your toys were training. The National Museum of Denmark points out that a spinning top trained the fingers for spinning. You assumed it was just for fun. Your mother did not correct you.

Children did play, though, and the ground has kept some of their toys. The National Museum of Denmark lists wooden toys found from the Viking Age: small ships, swords, dolls and animal figures.

In Dublin, then a Norse town, archaeologists from the National Museum of Ireland found a little wooden boat about thirty-seven centimetres long on Winetavern Street. It has been interpreted as a child's toy. Somebody, about a thousand years ago, sailed it in a puddle. The museum's Viking galleries in Dublin also display other toys and board games from the city's excavations.

Your own boat was carved by your father, one winter evening, from a piece of pine. It has a mast, a tiny steering oar, and, at your insistence, a sheep on board. Your father said Viking ships did not carry sheep. You pointed out that his did, all the time, when they sailed to new lands. He had no answer to that.

Winter has its own games. When the lake freezes, you strap on skates made of bone. The Swedish History Museum describes them: skates cut from the foot bones of horses or cattle, flat and polished underneath, sometimes rubbed with pig fat to go faster. The museum's own pair was found at Birka, the Swedish trading town where your uncle Asmund used to work.

You did not lift your feet. You pushed yourself along with a pole. Hurstwic adds that the poles were often tipped with iron spikes, and that skating was probably done for pleasure as well as for getting around.

Your father says skating is for getting across the lake quickly. Then he borrows your skates when he thinks no one is looking.

And then there are the board games. The adults play them by the fire, moving little pieces across carved boards, and you watch until you understand the rules better than your father does. The National Museum of Denmark notes that adults played games together too. Sometimes, they even let you win. At Fjörður, a Viking Age farm in eastern Iceland, archaeologists have even found a board game carved from volcanic stone. Remember that site. We will be back there at the end of this story.

When you are ten, a boy arrives at the farm. He is seven, small, very serious, and he has a cloak far too fine for a farm like yours. His name is Orm, and he is Hallvard's grandson, from the rich farm down the valley.

He has come to be fostered. He will live with your family, eat at your table, and learn from your parents, for years.

Colm, Hallvard's thrall, carries Orm's wooden chest up the hill. He sets it down by the door, nods to your mother, and looks at your father for a long moment. Your father looks at the fence. Nobody explains it to you. You decide to ask Orm later. Orm does not know either.

Fostering was common in the Norse world. Hurstwic describes it not as adoption, but as a legal agreement and an alliance between families. Usually, a family of lower status fostered the child of a more prominent one. The rich family gained loyal friends. The foster family gained a powerful connection.

Children also went to other homes to learn. The historian Ryder Patzuk-Russell, writing about education in medieval Iceland, notes that one way to educate a child was simply to send them to be raised in another household, especially one that could teach important skills.

The sagas take these ties seriously. Hurstwic notes that the bond between foster relations could be as strong as blood, or stronger.

Not every fostering was sentimental. Hurstwic retells a saga in which a girl named Ketilríðr was sent away to be fostered because her own mother refused to teach her crafts. Families could be very practical about these arrangements.

At first, Orm cries every night. You decide, as the eldest child of the house, that this is your problem to solve. You teach him to skate, to blow on embers, and to catch the sheep. The first time he tries the bone skates, he falls over so many times that the dog stops watching. By spring, he follows you everywhere. By summer, he calls you his sister, and means it.

There were no schools on your farm. Not one. You learned by watching, copying and getting things wrong in front of adults who sighed.

Your mother teaches you to spin, to weave, to milk and to plan the winter stores. Your father teaches Orm to mend fences, handle a boat and judge the weather. Sometimes he teaches you too, when your mother is not looking.

In the sagas, foster parents could teach far more than farm work. Patzuk-Russell points to the saga of Njáll, a famous lawyer, who fostered children and taught them law. Your father knows a great deal about one boundary stone. That will have to do.

Another saga, Patzuk-Russell notes, tells of a girl named Ólof who was carefully trained in fine needlework in Norway. Your mother hears this, looks at your lumpy thread, and sighs.

The lessons that matter most are never called lessons. You learn which neighbor can be trusted, which one owes your family a favour, and how to stay quiet when men are bargaining. You learn that a good winter store is worth more than a good story. And you learn that the sheep always, always finds the weak spot in the fence.

The National Museum of Denmark notes that play, for the Norse, also meant sport, exercise, music and weapons. So Orm gets a wooden sword for his eighth winter. You get one too, after a long argument. The sheep, wisely, keeps its distance.

Childhood did not last long. The National Museum of Denmark notes that children probably became adults much earlier than today. In Iceland, males were legally adults at sixteen. Hurstwic adds that by sixteen, a boy was expected to take on all the roles of a grown man.

For girls, the line was different. According to the museum, girls often married very young, and were regarded as adults once they were married. Hurstwic notes that virtually all women were married by the age of twenty.

Remember Gudrun, from our chapter on Viking women, promised in marriage at fifteen, without being asked. In five or six years, the families in your valley will start looking at you and counting cows.

When your uncle Asmund visits, he tells stories about rapids called Do not sleep and a city with a single gate. You announce that you will go east too. Your mother says no. Your father says no. Asmund says no, and then winks at you when they are not looking.

You do not want to think about marriage yet. Your mother does not want you to either. But you notice that she has started teaching you how to keep the keys, how to count the stores, and how to argue with a merchant about the price of cloth.

You are ten. You are already being prepared to run a farm.

Orm, meanwhile, is being prepared to run a much bigger one. One day, he will go back down the valley to Hallvard's hall, with its silver and its thralls. You wonder what kind of master he will be. You hope your family's table will have taught him something.

So why was being a Viking child worse than you think? Because you were lucky just to be accepted, luckier still to survive, and then put to work before you could carry a full bucket. Childhood ended at sixteen, or with a wedding, and nobody asked if you were ready.

And yet, there were toy boats with sheep on board, bone skates on a frozen lake, board games by the fire, and foster brothers who became family. Viking childhood was hard, but it was not without joy.

And it is almost invisible. As we saw in our chapter on Viking women, babies and children are under-represented in Viking burials. Their toys were made of wood and rotted away. Their names were rarely carved in stone.

Which brings us to that little animal. In twenty twenty-four, archaeologists digging a Viking Age farm at Fjörður, in Seyðisfjörður in eastern Iceland, found a tiny figure carved from volcanic stone. It is about five centimetres long, and dates to between nine hundred and forty and one thousand.

It may have been a toy. And nobody can agree what it is. Some of the team see a pig. Others see a bear. Some people suggest a dog. The excavation manager, Ragnheiður Traustadóttir, who has owned an Icelandic dog herself, said that when you hold the object, it does not feel like a dog. She called the find amazing, because children are not very visible in the Viking Age, and their toys seldom survive.

Somewhere, a thousand years ago, a child knew exactly what that animal was. That child probably named it, too.

Perhaps one day, someone will dig up a little pine boat with a sheep on board, and argue about that as well.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. Your father will pretend not to listen, and Orm will beg to go. You will be at the fence, with your spindle in one hand and a rope in the other. Because somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped again, and this time, it is your job. This is Viking Chapters. The lives behind the legends.
