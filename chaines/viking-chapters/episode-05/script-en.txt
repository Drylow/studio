You wanted to be rich. A silk shirt, a sword with a patterned blade, and a pouch of silver coins covered in writing that nobody at home can read. Instead, it is barely light, you are up to your waist in a freezing river, and you are pushing a boat over rocks while somebody shouts the name of the rapid. It is called Do not sleep.

Somebody has to push the boat. Today, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Asmund, the younger brother of Aslaug from our earlier chapters. Like Eirik and Aslaug, you are an imagined person, built from what archaeology and written sources tell us, not a real man from history.

You are a lean young man with fair hair tied back, a short fair beard, a rust-orange tunic and a grey cloak. At your belt hangs a small bronze case, and inside it is the most important thing you own: a folding scale.

Years ago, you left the fjords to work for a fur merchant in Birka, the Swedish trading town. Your sister thinks you are doing very well. You have not corrected her.

Popular culture sends the Vikings west, to England and Ireland. But Scandinavians, and especially Swedes, also travelled east, along the great rivers of what is now Russia and Ukraine. There, they and their descendants became known as the Rus.

This chapter follows one of their trade roads, from the Baltic all the way to the capital of the Byzantine Empire. And at the end of this story, we will read a painted stone on the island of Gotland, raised for a man who, it seems, never got past a rapid called Aifur.

Your road east starts at Birka, with furs: sable, squirrel and other skins from the northern forests. Light to carry, easy to pack, and worth a great deal of silver in the right market.

A Persian geographer named Ibn Rustah, writing around the year nine hundred, described the Rus as people with no fields and no villages, whose only business was trading sable, squirrel and other furs. They kept the coins they were paid in their belts.

He was describing strangers from a distance, and your master owns a house, two boats and an opinion about everything. But the furs were real, and so was the silver they brought.

Ibn Rustah adds something darker. The Rus, he says, attacked the Slavs from their ships, took them captive, and sold them in the lands of the Khazars and the Bulgars. The National Museum of Denmark also names Bolghar, on the Volga, as a place where Vikings sold slaves.

Your master's cargo is furs, honey and wax. Some of the other traders on the route carry people. You learn not to look at their boats for too long.

You also learn languages, badly. A few words of Slavonic for food and water, a few words of Greek for numbers, and the one word every market on earth understands: no.

What comes back up the rivers is silver. Hundreds of thousands of Islamic silver coins, called dirhams, have been found in Viking Age hoards across northern Europe. The scholar Manar Hammad counts almost half a million dirhams in Scandinavian museums.

On the Baltic island of Gotland, in nineteen ninety-nine, a find at a farm called Spillings turned out to be one of the largest Viking silver hoards ever found: more than fourteen thousand coins, most of them dirhams.

Somebody hid that silver and never came back for it. You intend to come back for yours.

In spring, your master's boat leaves Birka and crosses the Baltic. Historians reconstruct the road like this: up the river Neva into Lake Ladoga, up the Volkhov past Novgorod, then through a chain of smaller rivers and lakes towards the Dnieper.

Where one river ends and the next has not yet begun, there is only land. So you unload, carry, drag and reload. These crossings are called portages, and each one is exactly as much fun as it sounds.

Constantine, the emperor we are about to meet, also describes the Rus winter. In November, he says, their chiefs left Kiev with their men to spend the winter among the Slavs who paid them tribute, and came back in April, when the ice on the river melted. He calls it a severe manner of life. You believe him.

By the time you reach the Dnieper, your shoulders have opinions. Your master tells you this is the easy part. He is right, which is the worst news you have heard all year.

The best guide to what comes next was written by that emperor. Constantine the Seventh, ruler of the Byzantine Empire, wrote a handbook in Greek in the nine hundreds, and one chapter describes traders like you, coming down the river to his city.

According to Constantine, Slavs cut boats called monoxyla in the winter, hollowed out of trees, and sold them to the Rus in the spring. The Rus bought only the hulls, and fitted them with oars, rowlocks and the rest of their tackle.

So at Kiev, your master leaves his northern boat behind and buys a dugout, a long, heavy hull made from a single great tree. You load it with furs, food, water, and two live chickens, for reasons he refuses to explain.

Constantine says the boats came down to Kiev from Novgorod, Smolensk and other towns, and in the month of June, they moved off down the Dnieper together. Kiev is crowded with boats, traders and arguments. Everyone has heard a story about the river below. Nobody agrees on the details, except one: you do not go alone.

Why together? Because of what lies downstream.

Below Kiev, the Dnieper drops through a long chain of rapids. Constantine names them one by one, in the language of the Rus and in Slavonic. The first, he says, is called Essoupi, which means: Do not sleep.

Some modern scholars read the name differently. But as advice, it is excellent.

At the rapids, you put in to the bank and unload the cargo. Then the men strip, climb into the water, and feel their way with their feet so as not to strike a rock, guiding the boat along, while others push from the stern with poles.

The water is cold, the rocks are slippery, and the boat weighs more than you, your master and your ambitions combined. Nobody is singing.

The worst is the great rapid, which the Rus called Aeifor. There, the boats come out of the water altogether. The crews drag them, or carry them on their shoulders, for about six miles.

Six miles under a dugout is a long way. The hull presses into your neck, the ground is stony, and the man in front of you keeps stopping without warning. Your master walks beside the boat, carrying the chickens.

Constantine adds one more detail, and it is the darkest line in his chapter. While the boats go round, the traders lead their slaves past the rapid on foot, in chains.

And all the while, you watch the grasslands. Constantine says that it is at this point that the Pechenegs, the nomad riders of the steppe, come down and attack.

That is why you travel in a fleet. A boat in the water is a ship. A boat on twenty men's shoulders is an invitation.

Your fleet is lucky. A few riders watch from a ridge, count your spears, and decide to wait for someone smaller. You do not stop shaking until the boats are back in the water.

Further down, the fleet reaches an island the Byzantines called Saint Gregory. Constantine says the Rus made sacrifices there, under a gigantic oak tree. They sacrificed live cocks, and cast lots to decide whether to kill the birds, eat them, or let them live.

So that is what the chickens were for. One of yours, by the lot, survives. It rides the rest of the way to the sea looking extremely pleased with itself. You give it a name. This is a mistake.

At last the river opens into the Black Sea. The crews rest for two or three days on an island near the river mouth, and fit their boats with masts and sails. Constantine sums up the journey so far as a voyage of travail and terror, difficulty and danger. He was not even on it.

On the other great river road, the Volga, traders like you met a man who wrote everything down. In nine hundred and twenty-one, the caliph in Baghdad sent an embassy north to the king of the Volga Bulgars. Its secretary was Ahmad ibn Fadlan.

On the banks of the Volga, he watched the Rus arrive with their goods. He had never seen people so tall, he wrote. They were as tall as palm trees, blond and ruddy. Every man carried an axe, a sword and a knife, and was never seen without them.

He was much less impressed by their manners. He called them the filthiest of God's creatures. Ibn Fadlan was a man from Baghdad. To him, the Rus were strangers, and he wrote like it.

He noticed their wealth, too. A Rus woman, he says, wore a neck ring for every ten thousand dirhams her husband was worth. And their most prized ornaments were beads of green glass, for which they paid a whole silver dirham each.

Then he describes a scene every trader will recognise. When a Rus merchant arrived, he carried bread, meat, onions, milk and drink to a tall wooden post carved with a human face, surrounded by smaller figures.

He bowed down before it and listed what he had brought: so many slave girls, so many sable furs. Then came the request: send me a buyer with plenty of gold and silver, who buys whatever I want him to buy.

If business was slow, Ibn Fadlan says, the merchant came back with a second offering, and then a third. If that still failed, he brought gifts to the smaller figures as well, and asked them to put in a good word with the big one.

Selling has changed a great deal in a thousand years. The prayer has not.

On your own river, your master makes no offerings to wooden posts. He simply mutters at the sky every time a buyer walks away. It seems to work about as well.

Your own fleet hugs the coast of the Black Sea, and one morning, the skyline changes. This is Miklagard, the Great City. That is what the Norse called Constantinople, capital of the Byzantine Empire. On runestones back home, its whole empire is simply called Greece.

You might expect a hero's welcome. You get paperwork.

The city had its reasons. On the eighteenth of June, eight hundred and sixty, about two hundred Rus ships had appeared outside Constantinople. They could not take the city, and they withdrew. But Patriarch Photius, the head of the Church there, described the attack in a sermon as a terrible northern storm. Nobody in the city had forgotten.

A chronicle kept later in Kiev, known as the Primary Chronicle, preserves treaties between the Rus and the emperors from the early nine hundreds. Under them, Rus merchants could enter the city through only one gate, unarmed, in groups of no more than fifty, escorted by an imperial official.

They stayed near the church of Saint Mamas. Officials wrote down their names, and only then could they receive their monthly supplies, for up to half a year.

So you stand in line with forty-nine other northerners, no axe, no sword, no knife, while a clerk writes down a name he cannot pronounce. Behind him rise walls taller than any building you have ever seen. You try very hard to look like a merchant and not like a storm.

Think about what those rules say. The emperor's officials wanted the furs and the silver changing hands. They did not want fifty armed northerners wandering the city at once.

Your master sells his furs in the market, and buys what the north wants: silk, wine, spices, glass and fine cloth. You buy something too: a string of green glass beads. Ibn Fadlan's Rus would have approved.

Others left more than money behind. In the upper gallery of the great church of Hagia Sophia, someone carved runes into the marble. Most of it is worn away, but scholars read the name Halfdan. Nobody knows exactly when he was there. Every tourist in history has had the same idea.

Back home, your master's silver will not be counted. It will be weighed. Coins and ornaments are cut into pieces, tested with a knife and placed on a scale, just like the hacksilver your brother-in-law brought home from his raid. That is why you carry the folding scale.

Traders of your time used small folding balances that packed away into metal cases, with standard weights, some shaped like cubes with their corners cut off, others round with flattened ends. The archaeologist Lotta Fernståhl notes that some of the round weights even carry imitation Arabic writing, copied from Islamic coins by people who could not read it.

Even the jewellery travelled. At Birka, in a woman's grave from the eight hundreds, archaeologists found a silver ring set with a violet stone. For more than a century, it was catalogued as an amethyst.

In twenty fifteen, researchers showed that the stone is coloured glass, and that the engraving on it is Arabic. It reads: for Allah, or to Allah. The ring shows little wear, which suggests it passed through few hands between the silversmith and the grave.

Who was the woman buried with it? A traveller herself, the wife of a trader, or simply someone who liked a beautiful ring? Researchers still debate it. The grave does not say.

The road home is the same road in reverse, against the current. The rapids are no friendlier when you are carrying silver. You pass the island of Saint Gregory. The chicken does not come ashore.

By the second autumn, you are back on the Baltic, salt-stained, thinner, and carrying a small leather bag of silver that is entirely yours. It is not a fortune. It is a cow, perhaps two, and a winter without worry.

Your master counts his profit. It is good. Then he subtracts the boats, the portages, the officials, the gifts, the chickens and a year of his life. It is still good, he decides. He decides this loudly.

Not everyone came home from the east. On Gotland, in the museum at Visby, stands a stone from the late nine hundreds. It was raised by a man named Hegbjorn and his brothers, in memory of a man named Hrafn.

The inscription says that the stone was brightly painted, and that they came far into Aifur. Scholars connect Aifur with Aeifor, the great rapid named by Constantine, the one where the boats came out of the water.

Hrafn's family could not bring him home. So they brought his name home instead, and painted it bright enough to be seen.

He was not the only one. About thirty runestones in Sweden mention Greece, meaning the Byzantine Empire. Most remember men who died on their travels. A few simply say that a man had been there.

So why was being a Viking trader on the eastern rivers worse than you think? Because the road to the silk was a road of rocks. You carried boats across land, waded through rapids named Do not sleep, watched the steppe for riders, and queued at a single gate with fifty unarmed men.

And the silver you carried home was tied, more often than anyone liked to say, to people sold along the same rivers.

And yet, that road linked the fjords to Constantinople and Baghdad. A ring engraved in Arabic ended up in a Swedish grave. Green glass beads ended up on a Norwegian farm.

When you finally walk up the path to your sister's farm, Eirik is fixing the fence, Aslaug is at the loom, and a small girl is sitting in the grass, trying to eat a shoe. You give your niece a single green glass bead, for when she is older. Aslaug asks how rich you are now. You tell her the story about the chicken instead.

Next spring, your master will talk about another voyage. You will pretend not to listen. And somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped again.
