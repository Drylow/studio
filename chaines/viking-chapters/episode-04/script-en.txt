You wanted to be a farmer, like your father. A small field, a few cows, and the church bell across the bay on a Sunday morning. Instead, it is barely light, your wrists ache from the rope, and you are sitting at the foot of a mast on a Norse ship, watching your coast shrink to a grey line.

The Viking legend always has a price. Somebody always pays it. This summer, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Colm, one of the two villagers taken in Bjorn's raid, two chapters ago. Like Eirik and Aslaug, you are an imagined person, built from what archaeology, laws and written sources tell us, not a real man from history.

You are about twenty, with short dark hair, no beard, a rough undyed tunic, and the shoes you happened to be wearing when the bell started ringing. Everything else you owned is behind you.

You recognise a few faces at the oars. One of them is a red-bearded man with a fresh cut across his forearm. He does not look at you. Most of them do not.

Popular culture rarely shows people like you. In films, you are the figure carrying firewood in the background. In the sagas, you are often a joke or a villain. But the Norse had a word for you, and English borrowed it: thrall.

Today, when a story holds an audience in thrall, the phrase still carries the memory of your new job.

You may have heard that Viking thralls could earn their freedom. Some did. This chapter is about the fine print. And at the end of this story, we will visit a group of islands off Iceland that still carry the name of runaway thralls, and find out what happened to them.

The ship does not take you to Norway. Not yet. On the way home, Bjorn stops at a trading place, and you find out what you are worth to him: a few pieces of silver, weighed on a scale.

You are not a rare case. The National Museum of Denmark notes that Vikings acquired slaves mainly on expeditions to Eastern Europe and the British Isles, and sold them at trading centres such as Hedeby, in what is now northern Germany, and Bolghar, on the river Volga.

Some captures were enormous. The Annals of Ulster, a year-by-year chronicle kept in Ireland, record that in eight hundred and seventy-one, two Norse kings returned to Dublin from Scotland with two hundred ships, bringing a great number of Angles, Britons and Picts into captivity.

Dublin, then ruled by Norse kings, grew into a major slave market of the Irish Sea. The historian Poul Holm argues that its trade grew busiest in the second half of the ten hundreds, long after your time. The business you have just entered will outlive you by generations.

It was old in the east as well. A biography of the missionary Ansgar, written soon after his death in eight hundred and sixty-five, says that at the Swedish trading town of Birka, many Christians were held captive. The Swedish History Museum describes both Birka and Hedeby as slave markets.

The man who buys you deals in everything: furs, salt, iron, and people. He takes you north along the Norwegian coast and sells you again. You do not understand a word of the bargaining. You understand the scale.

Your new owner is Hallvard, a wealthy farmer from a valley in western Norway. He is a big man with a black beard, a dark blue cloak with a fur collar, and a silver brooch that he touches whenever he thinks about money, which is often.

Hallvard looks at your hands and your shoulders, the way a farmer looks at a horse. Then he pays. The whole thing takes less time than choosing a horse would.

The valley he brings you to is quiet, green and wet. You have never seen it before. You will soon learn that one of its smaller farms belongs to a man with a red beard and a cut on his forearm.

On Hallvard's farm, you learn your place in the law, and it is barely a place at all. The National Museum of Denmark describes slaves as people without any personal rights.

The Norwegian encyclopedia Store norske leksikon is even colder. In the oldest Nordic provincial laws, it says, a thrall was treated as movable property, with no legal standing. In the eyes of the law, you belong on the same list as Hallvard's carts and cattle.

Your owner can punish you, and the museum notes that punishments could be severe. You cannot take a case to the assembly. Nobody there is waiting to hear your side.

And your status does not end with you. If you ever have children here, historians note, they could inherit your thralldom.

Not every thrall on this farm was captured abroad. You share a corner of the work shed with an older man called Ketil, a Norwegian with grey stubble, a limp, and a tunic patched so many times that the patches have patches. He was born free. Years ago, in a bad winter, he borrowed grain from Hallvard and could not pay it back.

That really could happen. The Swedish History Museum notes that even a free person could enter temporary thralldom through poverty or to settle a debt, and Store norske leksikon describes debt slaves in the oldest laws. The National Museum of Denmark adds that crimes such as murder and theft could also be punished with slavery.

Ketil teaches you your first Norse words: work, fence, pig, hurry. He also teaches you the most useful skill on the farm: looking busy whenever Hallvard walks past.

Norse poetry did not think much of either of you. The poem Rígsþula, which imagines how the classes of society began, describes the very first thrall: dark hair, wrinkled skin, knotted knuckles, thick fingers, an ugly face and a bent back.

Then it names his children. In translation, the names include Horsefly, Sluggard, Stumpy and Log. The Swedish History Museum notes that the sagas, too, portray thralls as dark, ugly and foolish.

Remember who wrote these texts. Not thralls. Historians warn that thralls in the sagas are often literary devices, shaped by the views of authors writing in the twelve hundreds. The people in the background of the story never got to tell it.

Your days begin before Hallvard's, and end after his. The work is whatever nobody free wants to do.

The Swedish History Museum lists it plainly: repairing fences, fertilising fields, herding pigs and sheep, and cutting turf. The National Museum of Denmark says owners used slaves for the hardest and most unpleasant work in the fields. Rígsþula hands the thrall's children exactly the same chores.

So you carry dung to the fields in a basket on your back. You cut turf, heavy wet blocks of earth and grass, and stack them to patch the roof. You mend fences, and Hallvard's pigs break them again by evening. The pigs, at least, seem to enjoy their work.

Rígsþula's first thrall also hauls home firewood all day long. In that one detail, the poem gets you exactly right.

Autumn brings the slaughter, and the farm prepares for winter. You carry meat to be dried and salted, and fish to be stacked. You do not decide what goes into the stores. You do find out who eats last when they run low.

Winter is long, dark and smoky. Hallvard's household sits by the hearth, telling stories, and you start to follow a few words. Most of the stories are about men like Hallvard's grandfather, who sailed west and came home rich.

Nobody explains where the silver came from. Nobody needs to. You are sitting in the corner, proof of it.

Late in the winter, a sheep wanders down the valley and into Hallvard's yard. It is a scruffy, determined animal with a gift for finding broken fences. Ketil recognises it at once. It belongs to the small farm up the hill.

Hallvard sends you to take it back. It is the first time you have walked anywhere alone since the raid. You think about running. Then you think about the snow, the fjord, the sea, and the fact that every farm in the valley knows exactly whose thrall you are.

At the gate of the small farm, a man with a red beard is mending a fence. On his forearm is a long, pale scar.

You know him. He was at the oars when you sat by the mast. He takes the sheep, thanks you briefly, and turns back to his fence. He does not know you at all.

A woman in a mauve dress comes out of the house. She is expecting a child, and she looks at you a little longer than he does. She gives you a piece of bread for the walk back. It is kind. It does not change anything.

This is the uncomfortable part of the Viking story. Eirik is no monster. He is an ordinary farmer who went on one raid, came home with a cut arm and a small pouch of silver, and went back to his fences. And some of that silver exists because you were sold.

He was far from alone. Hundreds of thousands of Islamic silver coins, called dirhams, have been found in hoards across northern Europe. The Swedish History Museum suggests that many of them may partly reflect the slave trade, and a research project at the University of Oxford explored the possibility that captives, rather than furs, were the main goods exchanged for that silver.

Historians still debate how large that trade was. But the silver never cared who had been weighed for it.

Walking back down the valley, you eat the bread. Then you go back to the dung baskets.

In early spring, Hallvard's old father dies. The household builds him a grave mound and lays him in it with his sword, his drinking horn and his best cloak. Ketil goes very quiet. That night, he tells you why.

Some Viking graves hold more than one person. The National Museum of Denmark mentions burials in which slaves were forced to follow their owners in death. The Swedish History Museum says that some double graves may contain a thrall who went into the grave with an owner.

At Flakstad, in the Lofoten islands of northern Norway, a team led by the archaeologist Elise Naumann of the University of Oslo re-examined ten skeletons from single, double and triple graves. At least three of the people in the shared graves had been buried without their heads.

DNA showed that the people buried together were most likely not related through their mothers. And chemical traces in the bones showed that the headless people had eaten mostly fish, while others in the graves had eaten much more meat and food from the land.

The researchers suggest that the headless people were slaves, killed and buried with their masters. It is a hypothesis, not a certainty. The graves were only partly excavated in the nineteen eighties, and may have been disturbed. And such killings seem to have been uncommon. But the fear in Ketil's voice is not a hypothesis.

Hallvard's father goes into his mound alone, with his sword and his cloak. You stand at the back with Ketil, and you breathe again.

Then it is time to plough. Hallvard's ox is lame, the field cannot wait, and so you and Ketil are put in the harness. You drag the plough across the stony field while Hallvard's son walks behind it, giving directions the way he would to an ox.

That may sound like an exaggeration. But Iceland's Book of Settlements, written down centuries later, tells of a settler named Hjörleif who had only one ox, and made his thralls drag the plough. Remember Hjörleif. He comes back in this story.

Ketil has a plan, and it involves counting. He is a debt thrall, and debt thralldom could be temporary. If he pays back what he owes, he believes, Hallvard must let him go.

So every evening, after the day's work, Ketil carves wooden spoons and bowls, and trades them for scraps of silver. He keeps them in a little cloth bag, hidden in the roof. He counts them more often than Hallvard counts his cows.

Could thralls really buy their way out? Yes. The National Museum of Denmark notes that slaves could have their freedom bought by others, or in some cases be set free by their owners. Historians also describe thralls who bought their own freedom.

A saga written in the twelve hundreds describes the most generous version. It says that the Norwegian chieftain Erling Skjalgsson, who lived a couple of generations after your time, kept thirty slaves on his farm. He gave each a set day's work, then let them work for themselves in the twilight and at night, with land to grow their own grain.

Each was given a set amount of work to earn their freedom. According to the saga, many managed it within one or two years, and anyone with any luck within three. With that money, Erling bought more slaves.

Read that last sentence again. Even the kindest example in the sources is still a business plan. And historians are not sure the saga's picture is reliable at all. Store norske leksikon calls it uncertain.

Some owners gave freedom away. At Hørning, in Denmark, a farmer ploughing his field in eighteen forty-nine found a runestone raised by a man called Tóki the smith, in memory of a man named Thorgisl. The inscription says that Thorgisl gave him gold, though that word is uncertain, and freedom.

A man who had once been unfree paid for a stone in his own name. That is a real success story, carved for everyone to read.

But freedom came with strings. A freed thrall was called a leysingi, and still owed loyalty to the former master. Store norske leksikon notes that the laws expected it to take several generations before a freed family was fully free of those ties.

So even Ketil's plan has a long tail. His grandchildren may still owe Hallvard's grandchildren a favour.

Which brings us back to Hjörleif. The Book of Settlements says he raided in Ireland and took ten thralls there. Then he sailed with his foster-brother Ingólf to settle Iceland, in the eight hundred and seventies.

In the spring, with one ox and a field to sow, he made his Irish thralls drag the plough. One of them, a man named Dufthak, came up with a plan. They killed the ox and said that a bear had done it. When Hjörleif and his men spread out into the woods to hunt the bear, the thralls attacked and killed them.

Then they fled by boat to islands they could see out at sea, to the southwest. Ingólf found Hjörleif's body, guessed where the boat had gone, and followed. He found the thralls on the islands, and killed them all.

The Norse called the Irish Westmen. So the islands were named Vestmannaeyjar, the Westman Islands, and that is still their name today.

Remember who wrote this story down: the descendants of the settlers, centuries later, not the descendants of the thralls. We will never hear Dufthak's version.

Thralldom did not vanish overnight. The National Museum of Denmark notes that slavery declined with Christianity, but was not fully abolished until the Middle Ages. In Sweden, thralldom was formally abolished in its last remaining provinces in thirteen thirty-five. That is almost four hundred years after you were weighed on a scale at a trading place.

So why was being a Viking thrall worse than you think? Because you were in the story all along, and nobody wrote you in. You built the fences, carried the dung, cut the turf and pulled the plough, and the poems named your children Horsefly and Log.

And yet, some thralls did get out. Some bought their freedom, some were given it, and one freed smith in Denmark raised a stone that you can still see in a museum today. Most, like you, simply kept going, which may be the hardest thing in this whole story.

By summer, Ketil's bag in the roof is a little heavier. Hallvard, to everyone's surprise, has given you a strip of stony ground to dig in the evenings. Maybe in three years. Maybe never.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. You will hear about it from the field, and you will know exactly what it means for somebody's village. And somewhere up the valley, Eirik's sheep has escaped again. Guess who gets sent to bring it back. This is Viking Chapters: the lives behind the legends.
