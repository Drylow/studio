You wanted to be a Viking raider. A fast ship, a chest of silver, your name in a song. Instead, it is the middle of the night, the rain is coming in sideways, and you are lying on a row of oars with another man's feet against your ear.

Nobody is singing. Somebody is bailing. In a few minutes, that somebody will be you.

Imagine you are Eirik again, the Norse farmer from our last chapter, a few years older and not noticeably richer. He is still an imagined person, built from what archaeology and written sources tell us, not a real man from history.

Last time, your crew backed away from a fight and went trading instead. This summer, Bjorn has bigger plans. He is gathering men for a raid across the sea, and he wants you on an oar.

Why would a farmer say yes? Your roof still leaks. Your neighbor still argues about that boundary. And a few pieces of silver could solve problems that hard work keeps politely postponing.

The National Museum of Denmark puts it plainly: expeditions required great investment in ships and men, but they could be a profitable business. Notice the word could. The only guaranteed part is the rowing.

Early raids, the museum adds, were quick surprise attacks, usually involving only a few ships. Later, whole armies would cross the sea. Bjorn's plan is the small kind: one ship, one crew, one stretch of coast, and a great deal of optimism.

Bjorn's standing matters here. Leaders kept followers through generosity, and the museum notes that skaldic poems praise kings and magnates who gave gold rings to those who fought for them. Bjorn is no king, but feeding his crew is how he keeps one.

Generosity also creates obligations. Once you accept his food, his ship and his protection, your word becomes part of the bargain. Backing out later would cost more than silver. It would cost you your standing in the valley.

What did a crew agreement actually say? Here the evidence goes quiet. The Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde admits it directly: we don't know much about the crews of Viking Age warships.

Norwegian law, written down later, in the eleven hundreds and twelve hundreds, describes ordinary crewmen as young men chosen by the steersman, who had no choice about sailing and kept fixed watches. That law was written for a king's levy, not a private raid. Still, the general mood sounds familiar.

So you bring your spear and your round shield, the same ones from your last voyage. You still do not own a sword. Fortunately, the sea has never once asked to see one.

Aslaug does not give a speech. She checks your cloak, your spare shoes and your food bag, then reminds you that the harvest will not wait for heroes. You promise to be back in time. You both know the weather gets a vote.

The ship is bigger than the boat you knew. For scale, the Gokstad ship, buried in Norway around the year nine hundred, had sixteen pairs of oars. More oars means more rowers, and more rowers means more people sharing everything, including the smell.

Oars are for calm water, narrow channels and landings. On the open sea, the sail does the heavy work. Researchers who sail reconstructions today find the wind far more effective than any number of tired arms. Your arms are delighted to hear this. They are still sore.

There is no cabin, no galley and no dry corner. The ship is open to sky and spray. Everything you own is either wet, about to be wet, or underneath someone else.

Sleep comes in shifts. When the Viking Ship Museum sailed its reconstruction of a great longship, it could only provide proper sleeping places for about half the crew at a time. The others stayed at their posts, ready to handle the sail.

The sleepers laid the oars across the benches to make a kind of floor. Crew logs from those modern voyages describe nights so cold that many preferred to stay on their feet rather than try to sleep. You discover the same thing without needing a logbook.

Food is simple, because it has to survive the journey. The Viking Ship Museum lists porridge, flour, dried fish, butter and bread, with water carried in barrels or vats. Beer, it notes, belonged on chieftains' and kings' ships. Bjorn is generous, but not that generous.

Dried fish travels remarkably well. Researchers at the University of Cambridge traced Viking Age cod bones from the trading town of Haithabu back to Arctic waters off northern Norway. They could not tell whether the fish was trade goods or someone's food for the voyage. Either way, it was extremely well-travelled cod.

Hot meals are harder. The museum notes there was no fireplace on Viking Age warships, and later Norwegian law sends the ship's cook ashore to fetch water and prepare meals. On a crowded wooden ship, an open fire is a bold idea.

The same law says the steersman must not keep the ship at sea longer than its supplies allow. A sensible rule. It is also a reminder that running out of food was a real possibility, not a dramatic flourish.

You follow the coast when you can, cross open water when you must, and bail whenever the sea comes aboard. The sea comes aboard often. It does not care how terrifying your reputation is supposed to be.

Storms were not just uncomfortable. They killed raiders too. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, a year-by-year record kept in England, says that in seven hundred and ninety-four, raiders who plundered a Northumbrian monastery lost some of their leaders, then lost ships to the weather. Many drowned. Those who reached the shore were killed there.

In eight hundred and seventy-seven, the Chronicle says, the fleet of a Danish army lost one hundred and twenty ships at Swanage, on the south coast of England. Some copies blame a great storm. Others blame a great mist. Either way, the sea needed no weapons.

Your own crossing is merely miserable. You lose a water barrel, most of the feeling in your toes, and a significant portion of your dignity. By the standards of the Chronicle, that counts as a good trip.

Eventually, a foreign coast rises out of the grey. Bjorn has heard about a small settlement near the shore, with a church. A church means objects of value, and people who are not expecting an army. That is the cold logic of a raid.

Remember who is on the other side. More than a century and a half before your voyage, in seven hundred and ninety-three, raiders struck the monastery of Lindisfarne. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says heathen men destroyed God's church there, with plunder and slaughter.

The scholar Alcuin wrote to the king of Northumbria that never before had such terror appeared in Britain. To you, this is a summer voyage. To the people on that shore, it may be the worst day of their lives.

You land in the grey light before morning. Bjorn's plan sounds simple: move fast, take what can be carried, leave before anyone organizes. Plans always sound simple before your feet hit cold shingle.

Someone in the settlement sees the sail. A bell starts ringing. People run, carrying children, driving animals, grabbing whatever they can. Within moments, the place you imagined full of treasure is mostly full of shouting.

Inside the little church, the crew finds a silver dish, some fine cloth and a few decorated fittings. It is not a mountain of gold. It is a small community's carefully kept treasure, and now it is going into a sack.

The crew also takes food: grain, a few animals, whatever stores are close. This is the unglamorous side of plunder. Raiders needed to eat, and the people they robbed would now face winter without it.

Then comes the part of the Viking story that the songs skip. Raiders took people. Two villagers do not escape in time, and Bjorn's men force them down to the ship.

This was not rare, and it was not a side detail. The Annals of Ulster record that in eight hundred and twenty-one, raiders plundered Étar, in Ireland, and carried off a great number of women into captivity. The National Museum of Denmark notes that Vikings acquired slaves mainly on expeditions to Eastern Europe and the British Isles, and sold them at trading centres such as Hedeby.

For the two people now sitting near the mast, there is nothing adventurous about this. Their homes, their families and their freedom have been taken in a single morning. From now on, others will treat them as property.

Eirik is imagined, but what he is part of here was real, and it happened on many coasts. There is no clever joke to make about it, so we won't make one.

Then the locals come back, and this time they are not running. Men arrive with spears, axes and shields, and more are coming. Raids worked through surprise. Once the surprise is gone, so is the advantage.

Bjorn orders everyone to the ship. You run with a sack in one hand and your shield in the other, which is one hand too few. You stumble on the shingle, and the rim of your own shield opens a long cut across your forearm. Your first raiding wound is self-inflicted.

Not everyone is that lucky. A young oarsman from the next valley, whose name you only learned at the start of the voyage, is struck down in the scramble. The crew gets him aboard. By evening, he is dead.

There is no ship's doctor. The Viking Ship Museum notes that no source or law text mentions actual medical care on board Viking ships. Someone binds your arm with a strip of cloth and tells you not to touch it. You will touch it constantly.

A cut that needs a few stitches today could turn dangerous without modern medicine. For days, you check whether the redness is spreading, and try to remember which gods you might have recently offended.

You bury your crewmate on a quiet stretch of foreign shore. Back home, his family might one day raise a stone. In Småland, in Sweden, a runestone raised by a man named Vrái for his brother Gunni ends with four plain words: He died in England.

Sometimes, raiders did not get away at all. In two thousand and nine, archaeologists working ahead of a new road near Weymouth, in Dorset, found a pit holding around fifty men, most of them young, all beheaded. Isotope tests point to Scandinavian origins for many of them, and radiocarbon dating places their deaths around the year one thousand.

Archaeologists note that their number would fit a single ship's crew. We do not know exactly who they were, or why they were killed. But they are a reminder that the people on the receiving end of raids could, and did, strike back.

Back at sea, the crew finally asks the question everyone has been saving: what do we actually get?

Here the evidence thins again. No crew contract from the Viking Age survives. Sagas written down centuries later describe loot being gathered and shared out, but they are stories about the past, not pay records.

A reasonable guess, and it is only a guess, is that the man who provided the ship and took the biggest risk expected the biggest share. In the later Norwegian law the museum describes, the steersman owned the ship and held command. Bjorn does not need a law to work that out.

So the plunder is laid out on a cloak. The silver dish. The fittings. A bent brooch someone found on the path. Then somebody picks up an axe, and the dish stops being a dish.

In the Viking world, silver was often valued by weight, not by appearance. Objects were chopped into pieces, called hacksilver, and weighed on small scales. Your share of a beautiful dish may be a jagged triangle of it.

Every piece gets tested. Archaeologists in Scotland describe hoards where most of the silver is heavily nicked: small cuts made as it passed from hand to hand, to check its purity. Trust was pleasant. A knife was better.

You can see this system in the ground. At Torksey in Lincolnshire, where a Viking army spent the winter of eight hundred and seventy-two, archaeologists from the universities of York and Sheffield recorded hacksilver, cut coins, more than a hundred weights, and hundreds of lead gaming pieces.

Silver, scales and games: an army weighing its wealth, then filling long winter evenings. Nobody recorded who lost their share on a bad throw. It is not hard to imagine.

Your own share is a small pouch of hacksilver. Enough to pay the smith and fix the roof. Not enough to change your life.

And there is a darker part to the arithmetic. On the way home, Bjorn sells the two captives at a trading place. You watch it happen. Some of the silver in your pouch exists because two people lost their freedom. That, too, was Viking wealth.

What about the legendary fortunes? They existed, but mostly for fleets led by powerful men. Decades after Eirik's imagined voyage, in nine hundred and ninety-one, the Chronicle records that tribute was paid to the Danes for the first time: ten thousand pounds.

The payments grew. In one thousand and twelve, the Chronicle records forty-eight thousand pounds. And in Frankia, the Annals of Saint-Bertin record that King Charles the Bald paid seven thousand pounds to make a fleet leave Paris, in eight hundred and forty-five.

Huge numbers. But split across whole armies, with leaders taking their share first, the headline shrinks fast. The men at the oars were not each carrying home a king's ransom.

One man did want everyone to know he had done well. At Orkesta church, north of Stockholm, a runestone says that a man named Ulf took three payments in England. Nobody carves a stone to say: I went once, cut my arm, and came back with a small pouch.

Other stones tell the opposite story. The Swedish National Heritage Board counts around twenty runestones in the Mälaren region raised for men who died on a single expedition to the east, led by a man named Ingvar. Their families got stones instead of homecomings.

Your own voyage home is long, wet and quiet. The empty place at the oar is hard to ignore. Eventually someone complains about the porridge, and it feels almost like comfort.

When the Norwegian coast appears, you realize how little the farm cares about your adventure. The fields need harvesting. The hay is late. Your neighbor has, impressively, found something new to argue about.

Aslaug looks at your arm before she looks at the pouch. Then she looks at the pouch. Then back at your arm. You both understand the exchange rate.

The silver helps. It pays the smith, mends the roof, and buys a little security for winter. It does not make you rich. It does not make you famous. It definitely does not make you a legend.

Some raiders never came home at all, and not only because they died. In eight hundred and seventy-six, the Chronicle says, the leader Halfdan shared out the land of the Northumbrians, and his men began to plough and support themselves.

So the reward for years of raiding could be a farm. Historians such as Dawn Hadley have suggested that Halfdan's leading followers probably took over whole estates, while the ploughing itself fell to humbler warriors and the people already living there. Even in victory, someone still had to walk behind the plough.

Buried silver tells its own quiet story. In eighteen forty, workmen by the River Ribble in Lancashire uncovered the Cuerdale hoard: about forty kilograms of silver, including coins, ingots and hacksilver, hidden in the early nine hundreds. Whoever buried it never came back for it.

So why was being a Viking raider worse than you think? Because the glory belonged to the poems, and the cold belonged to you. You rowed, bailed, slept on oars and ate cold porridge before you ever saw a coast.

You came home with a wound from your own shield, a crewmate left on a foreign beach, and a small pouch of silver. And for the people on the other side of the raid, it was far worse than that.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. You will be fixing the fence. Again. And somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped. This is Viking Chapters: the lives behind the legends. If you would like to see what Eirik does next, subscribe, and we will meet at the next landing.
