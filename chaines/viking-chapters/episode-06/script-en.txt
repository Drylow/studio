You wanted land. Lots of it. Wide green meadows, a river full of fish, and not a single neighbor arguing about a boundary stone. Instead, it is barely light, you are being sick over the side of a cargo ship in the middle of the ocean, and a cow is standing on your foot.

Somebody always believes the grass is greener on the other side of the sea. This year, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Grim, Eirik's neighbor from our earlier chapters, the man with the boundary stone. Like Eirik and Aslaug, you are an imagined person, built from what archaeology, laws and written sources tell us, not a real man from history.

You have short dark-brown hair, a short neat dark beard, and an olive-green wool tunic that has seen a great many arguments. You are not a bad man. You simply believe, very sincerely, that boundary stones have a habit of wandering away from your land.

You have heard the stories. Out in the ocean to the west lies Iceland, where a man can claim as much land as he can walk around. No fences. No stones. No Eirik.

So you sell your farm, and load your wife Halla, your two grown sons, your cattle, your sheep and your opinions onto a ship, and sail west.

Eirik watches you go from the landing. He does not wave. Later, he will admit that the valley feels strangely quiet. He will never admit that he misses the arguments.

Popular culture imagines Iceland as an empty frontier, waiting for brave pioneers. It was, once. But you are setting out around the year nine hundred and fifty, and in Iceland, that is very late indeed.

And at the end of this story, we will meet a man who gave a land of ice and rock a very attractive name, for reasons any estate agent would understand.

Your ship is a sturdy ocean-going cargo ship. Archaeologists raised one like it from a Danish fjord, built in western Norway some decades after your time: about sixteen metres long, with open holds in the middle and small decks at each end.

The holds are for cargo. This year, the cargo is your entire life. Sacks of grain, tools, timber, cooking pots, a loom, and as many animals as the ship can carry without sinking or mutiny.

The historian Jesse Byock lists what the settlers brought with them: dogs, cats, pigs, goats, sheep, cattle and horses. Bones from the earliest farms in Iceland tell the same story. A whole farmyard crossed the ocean in open boats.

There is no shelter from the spray. The cows are frightened, the sheep are seasick, and your sons are arguing about whose turn it is to clean up after both. Halla has stopped speaking to you somewhere around the second day.

Then, one morning, Iceland rises out of the grey: black sand, dark cliffs, mountains with snow on them in the middle of summer, and here and there, a thin column of steam coming straight out of the ground. It is the most beautiful place you have ever seen. It is also, you will soon discover, the most beautiful place you have ever been cold.

The settlers had a tradition about how to find a home. Iceland's Book of Settlements says that Ingólf, the first settler, threw his high-seat pillars overboard, the carved posts from his hall, and settled where the sea washed them ashore. His slaves found them at the place that became Reykjavík.

Other settlers did the same, according to the sagas. You consider throwing your pillars overboard. Then you remember that you paid good money for them, and that the sea has never once agreed with you about anything.

The same book also tells of an early explorer named Flóki, who sailed with three ravens to help him find land. He released them one by one, and the third flew on ahead, towards the coast.

The ravens make a better story than what happened next. According to the book, Flóki's crew spent the summer fishing and forgot to make hay. That winter, all their livestock died. In spring, Flóki climbed a mountain, saw a fjord full of sea ice, and named the whole country Iceland. The name stuck. So did the lesson.

When the crew got home, the book says, they gave three very different reviews. Flóki said the land was worthless. A man named Herjólf said it had good points and bad. And a man named Thorolf claimed that butter dripped from every straw. From then on, he was known as Butter-Thorolf. You can guess which review reached your valley.

Iceland's own medieval historians tell the story of how the island was settled. They date Ingólf's arrival, the first permanent settlement, to around the year eight hundred and seventy-four.

Even he was not quite the first. The Book of Settlements says that before the Norse came, Irish Christian hermits, called papar, had lived there, and left behind Irish books, bells and croziers. Ari the Wise, Iceland's first historian, adds that they left because they did not want to live alongside heathens. Archaeologists have never found clear proof that they were there. But even Iceland's founding story begins with somebody who arrived earlier.

Archaeology agrees surprisingly well. In central Reykjavík, excavators found a scrap of turf wall built shortly before a layer of volcanic ash fell, in about eight hundred and seventy-one, give or take two years. It is one of the oldest human structures ever found in Iceland.

After that, the settlers came fast. Medieval Icelandic writers said that the whole country was settled within about sixty years. Archaeologists still argue about the exact pace. Nobody argues about the result. By the time you arrive, the best land is gone.

There had been rules for claiming it, at least in later memory. One version of the Book of Settlements says that a man could claim no more land than he and his crew could carry fire around in a single day.

A woman, the same version says, could claim as much as she could walk around on a spring day, from sunrise to sunset, leading a well-fed two-year-old heifer.

The historian Jesse Byock points out that nobody knows for certain whether a king ever set such rules. But a saga does show a settler named Thorolf carrying fire around his land claim, marking his new boundary with flames.

You find this extremely romantic. You also notice that nobody is handing out fire anymore. By your time, the land has owners, and owners have prices. Written sources describe land that was claimed, given, seized and, more and more often, bought.

So you buy. The farm you can afford is a narrow valley in the north, the land a settler's grandson has decided he does not need. The meadow is small, the hillside is stony, and the river is mostly rocks. You pay with most of the silver from your old farm, two of your cows, and a good deal of pride. The seller assures you the fishing is excellent. The seller is moving to the coast.

You are not the only latecomer. A few years after you, according to the Book of Settlements, a man named Thorvald and his son, a red-haired youth called Eirik, leave Norway because of some killings and take land on the remote north-western coast, at a farm called Drangar. A university project on Norse history describes it as rather poor land. Remember that young man.

The first thing you need is a house, and the first thing you notice is that there are almost no trees. Not real trees, anyway. Just low birch scrub, bent by the wind.

It was not always like this. When the first settlers arrived, birch woodland covered a large part of the island. The Icelandic Forest Service estimates between a quarter and two fifths of the land. Today, birch woodland covers about one and a half percent.

The settlers cleared it for pasture, for building and for fuel, and their grazing animals kept it from growing back. By your time, three generations in, the damage is well under way.

Your neighbors help you raise the frame, and you promise to help with theirs. In Iceland, you discover, nobody builds alone, and everybody remembers exactly who owes whom a day's work.

So you build with what Iceland has plenty of: grass. Your house is made of turf, cut into blocks and stacked into walls around a frame of wood. The finished walls can be about two metres thick. They are warm, they keep out the wind, and they make your home feel like living inside a very comfortable hill.

The wooden frame is the hard part. Long, straight timber is scarce. You can import it from Norway at a terrible price, or walk the beaches looking for driftwood that the ocean has carried in from far away.

The historian Jesse Byock notes that driftwood beams were often short and imperfect once the rotten parts were cut away. Yours certainly are. One rafter is so twisted that your sons call it the Snake. It holds up the roof anyway.

Inside, the light comes mostly through the smoke holes above the hearth. Turf houses did not last forever either. In Iceland, a turf longhouse lasted perhaps fifty to a hundred years before it had to be rebuilt. You are building for your grandchildren, whether they like twisted rafters or not.

In your first autumn, an old man from the next farm comes by to inspect your haystacks. He finds them inadequate. Then he tells you about the summer the sun turned red.

He was a boy then. The ground split open far to the south, and fire poured out of it for months. Ash fell, the sun went dim and bloody, and the summer was cold. He says many animals died that winter.

Modern science has found his eruption. It is called Eldgjá. Researchers led by the University of Cambridge, using ice cores from Greenland, date it from around the spring of nine hundred and thirty-nine to at least the autumn of nine hundred and forty.

It poured out around twenty cubic kilometres of lava, the largest lava flood in Iceland's history. Chronicles in Ireland, Germany and Italy describe a blood-red, weakened sun, and tree rings show one of the coolest summers of the last fifteen hundred years.

He may well be right. The researchers think the young colony probably suffered badly, though no Icelandic text describes the eruption directly. They also suggest that the famous poem Völuspá, with its fire and its end of the old gods, may carry memories of it. The old man has never heard of Völuspá. He just knows to keep extra hay.

You remember Flóki and his dead livestock. That winter, you count your haystacks every week. Then every day. Your sons start hiding them from you, just to keep you calm.

The whole point of a farm in Iceland, as Byock puts it, is to provide the fodder needed to keep a minimum of livestock alive through the winter. Grass is wealth. Hay is survival. Your cows are not pets. They are a very slow, very hungry bank.

Your first winter is long and dark. The cows lose weight. So do you. Halla starts speaking to you again around midwinter, mostly to tell you which animal looks the thinnest.

Her loom never stops. Wool is the one thing Iceland has in abundance, and in a few generations, as Aslaug could have told you, Icelandic law will turn woven cloth into money. Halla is quietly the richest person in the house. She knows it. You suspect it.

But you survive. In spring, the meadow turns green, the lambs arrive, and you discover that your new neighbor across the river believes that the boundary between your farms follows a stream that you are quite sure has moved.

Some things travel well.

Fortunately, Iceland has an answer to this, and it is the reason you will spend two weeks every summer sleeping in a booth on a lava field. In nine hundred and thirty, the Icelanders founded the Althing, a national assembly that met every year at Thingvellir, in the south-west.

It met for about two weeks in summer. Free men came from all over the island, and camped in booths, temporary structures of stone and turf roofed with cloth. It was court, parliament, market and festival all at once.

Iceland had no king. Instead, it had a lawspeaker, who stood on the Law Rock and recited the law aloud, a third of it each year, so that over his three-year term, everyone heard the whole thing. Before writing, the law lived in his memory.

In time, the Althing would even decide the island's religion. Around the year one thousand, according to the Book of Settlements and the sagas, Iceland took Christianity into its law there, so that the whole country would have one law and one religion.

Your first Althing is a revelation. The booth you share with three other farmers leaks, the speeches are long, and everybody seems to be related to everybody else. You have never been happier. Hundreds of people, and every one of them with a grievance. You feel at home at last.

You bring your stream to the Althing. So does your neighbor. Witnesses are called. Old men remember things differently. Eventually, the case is settled, mostly in your neighbor's favor.

And here is the catch. The Althing could judge, but it had no power to carry out its judgments. The winning side had to enforce the verdict itself, with the help of family and friends. The most serious punishment was full outlawry: a man lost his property and all protection, and nobody was allowed to feed or shelter him.

For a man with friends, the system worked. For a man with few friends and a new farm, it was a lesson in politics. You go home and start making friends. It is much harder than making hay.

When you visit the coast, you notice something else. Not everyone in Iceland came from Norway, and not everyone came willingly.

DNA studies of modern and ancient Icelanders suggest that a large share of the first settlers' women had their roots in Scotland and Ireland. Many of them, researchers suggest, probably came as slaves. The Book of Settlements remembers Irish thralls, and even names islands after them, as we saw in our chapter on thralls.

On the farm next to the harbour, an old woman sings to her grandchildren in a language you do not understand. Nobody has written down her name. Her great-grandchildren will be Icelanders. And somewhere back in Norway, in your old valley, a young man called Colm is still cutting turf for Hallvard.

So why was being a Viking settler in Iceland worse than you think? Because the frontier closed before you arrived. The free land was gone, the trees were going, the hay was never enough, and the ground itself could catch fire.

And because even at the edge of the world, you still had a neighbor, and a boundary, and an argument.

And yet, you built something. A turf house under a twisted rafter, a herd that survived the winter, and a voice at an assembly with no king. For a quarrelsome farmer from a small Norwegian valley, that is not nothing.

Thirty years later, you are an old man, and that red-haired youth from Drangar, now known as Eirik the Red, no relation to your old neighbor, is recruiting settlers for a new land in the west. He calls it Greenland. The Book of Settlements says he chose the name because people would be more eager to go there if the land had a good name.

It worked. According to the same book, about twenty-five ships set out from Iceland, around nine hundred and eighty-five. Fourteen arrived. Some were driven back, and some were lost at sea.

Your sons want to go. You tell them that the grass is never greener on the other side of the sea. They point out that you are the one who said it was. And somewhere behind the turf house, your sheep has escaped again, onto your neighbor's land. This is Viking Chapters: the lives behind the legends.
