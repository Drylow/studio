You wanted to be a berserker. A bearskin on your shoulders, a roar that empties a beach, and a place in the bow of the jarl's own ship, where no blade can touch you. Instead, it is the morning after, you are lying on a bench by the door, you cannot lift a cup of water, and nobody in the hall will sit next to you.

Somebody has to stand at the front. Since last summer, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Ulf, the younger son of Hallvard, from the rich farm down the valley of our earlier chapters. Like Eirik and Brand, you are an imagined person, built from what poems, sagas, laws and scholars tell us, not a real man from history.

You are the biggest man anyone in the valley has ever seen, with your family's short black hair, a thick black beard, a dark red tunic that had to be made twice, a grey bearskin cloak, and hands that have already broken two plough handles. Your nephew Orm thinks you are the strongest man in Norway. Your father thinks you are the most expensive.

Popular culture imagines berserkers as heroes: fearless, half-naked, unstoppable, laughing in the middle of battle. The real picture is stranger. The same men the old poems praised as a king's best warriors became, in later sagas, the villains of every feast, and in the end, outlaws.

And at the end of this story, we will look at a small ivory chess piece, carved in the north and found on a Scottish island, that shows a warrior doing the berserker's most famous trick: biting his own shield.

It starts at a wedding. A drunk guest insults your father, and you pick him up, bench and all, and carry him out into the yard. Nobody is hurt. Everybody talks about it for a year.

That autumn, Brand comes home from the jarl's hall in the north with a gold ring and a new poem. The jarl, he says, likes poets. He likes big men even more, and keeps a few of them close: the kind who stand at the front. Your father gives you an axe that cost more than the ox. Your mother gives you bread and a look that says: come back with your temper.

Men like the ones Brand describes were already old news when you were born.

The oldest surviving poem to use the word berserk was composed in the late eight hundreds by Thorbjörn Hornklofi, a skald at the court of King Harald Fairhair of Norway. In it, a raven is questioned about the berserks and the men who wade into battle. They are called wolf-skins, the raven answers, and a wise king trusts himself only to men of courage.

Snorri Sturluson, writing his history of the kings of Norway in the twelve hundreds, describes Harald's great warship. From the stem to the middle of the ship, he says, was the fore-defence, and there were the berserks. Only men remarkable for strength and courage got a place on that ship.

The front was the most honoured place on board, and the most exposed. Egil's saga, written down in the same century, says that at the great sea battle of Hafrsfjord, traditionally dated to around eight hundred and seventy-two, twelve of the king's berserks stood in the prow.

After the battle, the saga says, every man in front of the mast was wounded, except the berserks, whom iron did not bite. That was the legend: men no weapon could touch.

The legend had a catch. The other side had berserks too. Snorri says that at Hafrsfjord, a great berserk named Thor Haklang laid his ship against King Harald's, and fought until he fell and his whole ship was cleared. Iron, it turned out, did bite some of them.

Snorri went further. In his saga of the earliest kings, he describes the warriors of the god Odin. They went without mail-coats, he says, were as mad as dogs or wolves, bit their shields, and were as strong as bears or bulls, and neither fire nor iron could hurt them. That, he says, was called going berserk.

Even the name is a puzzle. Snorri seems to read berserk as bare-shirt: a man who fights without armour. Most scholars today prefer bear-shirt: a man in a bearskin. And the poems speak of wolf-skins too.

On the Swedish island of Öland, in eighteen seventy, two men digging in a cairn found four bronze dies, made in the six hundreds or earlier, to stamp decorations for helmets. One shows a warrior dressed in a wolfskin, which many scholars connect with these wolf-warriors. The idea was centuries older than you.

Brand introduces you at the jarl's hall. The jarl looks at you the way your father looks at an ox, then puts you on the bench nearest the door, next to three big men in bearskins who do not talk much. Next summer, he says, your place will be in the bow.

The bow, you soon learn, is also the wettest place on the ship. Every wave lands on you first. The bearskin, soaked, weighs as much as a calf and smells like one too. The men at the oars behind you are dry, warm, and very happy with the arrangement.

The three big men teach you the act first. Because, at least in the sagas, going berserk was something people could see coming.

Egil's saga describes a berserk named Ljot the Pale stepping onto a duelling ground marked out with stones. As he came forward, the saga says, the berserk fury seized him: he began to bellow hideously, and bit his shield. Grettir's saga describes another berserk who roared, bit the rim of his shield, and thrust it up into his mouth.

You practise the roar behind the stables. The horses do not like it. Neither does the jarl's wife.

Then there is the part nobody puts in the songs. Egil's saga says that men seized by berserk fury were so strong, while the fit lasted, that nothing could withstand them. But when it passed, they were weaker than usual.

The saga gives an example: Egil's own grandfather, Kveldulf. After one of his fits, on a ship at sea, exhaustion hit him so hard that he took to his bed. He grew sicker on the voyage to Iceland, and died at sea. Before the end, he asked his crew to put his body in a coffin and send it overboard, sure that it would find its own way to Iceland.

Even his name came with a warning. Kveldulf means evening wolf. The saga says that in the morning he was a wise and careful farmer, but as evening drew on he grew sullen, so that few people dared to speak to him.

Your own first fit comes that summer, in a harbour, during a quarrel with another crew over a mooring place. You remember the shouting. You remember nothing after it. You wake on the beach in the afternoon, with a split lip, a chewed shield rim and somebody else's cloak.

Nobody tells you what happened. Everybody moves their bench a little further away. For two days, you cannot lift a cup. Brand puts it in a poem. You ask him to take it out.

So what made a berserker? Nobody knows. And the sources never say.

Since the nineteen-fifties, some researchers have suggested hallucinogenic mushrooms, and the red-and-white fly agaric became the most popular suspect.

In twenty nineteen, the ethnobotanist Karsten Fatur, of the University of Ljubljana, argued that another plant fitted better: henbane. The sagas describe a trance followed by exhaustion, and fly agaric, he noted, does not usually make people violent. Henbane, he argued, could also explain the berserkers' famous indifference to pain, and the headaches afterwards. A woman's grave near Fyrkat, in Denmark, from the late nine hundreds, held a pouch of henbane seeds.

But Fatur himself called his idea speculative. The Norwegian scholar Karoline Kjesrud, quoted by the site ScienceNorway, said she knew of no description of berserkers eating or drinking anything before battle. Others have suggested alcohol, epilepsy, mental illness, or a frenzy the warriors worked themselves into.

And there is one more problem. The sagas were written down two or three centuries after the Viking Age, by Christian authors. The historian Roberto Biolzi, of the University of Lausanne, points out that their berserkers change to suit the story: a king's elite guard, Odin's invincible warriors, a monster for the hero to defeat, or a thief and a bully.

Nobody at the jarl's court offers you mushrooms. The cook offers you porridge. Brand asks you, for the poem, what it feels like. You tell him the truth: very angry, then very tired, then very lonely.

Because by the time the sagas were written, the berserker was no longer the hero.

In the family sagas, a berserk is often the man who turns up at a farm at Yule, picks a quarrel and demands something he cannot have. Egil's saga introduces Ljot the Pale in three short phrases: he is a berserk and a duellist; he is hated. He had asked for a farmer's daughter, been refused, and challenged her brother to a duel.

Grettir's saga explains why people hated them. Berserks and raiders, it says, would challenge respectable men to a duel for their money or their women, and whoever fell got no compensation. Many were shamed and lost their property this way, the saga says, and some lost their lives.

In those stories, the berserk always loses in the end, usually to the hero. In Grettir's saga, a berserk named Snaekoll was busy biting his shield rim when Grettir kicked the bottom of the shield. The saga's description of what happened next is not for this channel. Let us just say: never bite your shield near Grettir.

At the jarl's Yule feast, you sit at the far end. Mothers move their daughters to the other side of the hall. When you ask the jarl's steward for his daughter's hand, he laughs. Then he sees your face and stops laughing. He says he will think about it. He is still thinking about it when you leave.

You were not the only one. In Eyrbyggja saga, a berserk asks his chieftain to find him a good marriage. The chieftain answers that he sees no hope of any woman of good family tying herself, or her fortune, to a berserk.

Being a berserker also meant belonging to someone. And that someone could give you away.

Eyrbyggja saga, written in Iceland in the twelve hundreds, tells of two Swedish brothers, Halli and Leiknir, so big and strong that no one in Norway could match them. The saga says that King Eirik the Victorious of Sweden had sent them to Earl Hákon, who ruled Norway in the late nine hundreds, with a warning: treat them well, and pay attention to their moods.

An Icelander named Vermund, leaving the earl's court, was offered any reward he liked. He asked for the berserks. The earl warned him that they would turn hard and proud as soon as there was trouble, and that most farmers' sons could not control them.

The earl was right. Back in Iceland, Halli wanted a wife, Vermund would not help, and things turned sour. So Vermund gave the brothers away again, to his own brother, Styr, who had said he did not want them either.

The berserks protested, the saga says, that Vermund had no right to sell them or give them away like unfree men. A king's gift had become a family's problem, handed from house to house.

Then Halli asked Styr for his daughter, Ásdís. Styr set a price: break a road through the lava field to the next farm, and raise a wall across it. Halli answered that he was not used to work, but agreed.

The brothers did it. And then, according to the saga, Styr had both of them killed, and buried beside their own road. The bride was never part of the deal.

The lava field, on the Snæfellsnes peninsula in western Iceland, is still called Berserkjahraun: the berserkers' lava. An old path still crosses it, and a cairn beside it is said to mark their grave. Some writers suspect the saga simply explained a path that was already there.

You make a note: never ask a chieftain for his daughter. Then you make another note: never agree to build a road.

In the end, it was not an enemy that finished the berserkers. It was the law.

Grettir's saga says that Earl Eirik Hákonarson, ruling Norway in the early ten hundreds, met with the great landowners before leaving for England, abolished duels, and outlawed berserks who went about raiding and causing trouble. The ban is usually dated to around ten fifteen.

Iceland went further. Its Christian laws, written down in the early eleven hundreds and preserved in the law collection called Grágás, made going berserk a crime in itself. A man seized by berserk fury faced lesser outlawry: exile for three years, and the loss of his property.

And so did the men who were present, the law adds, unless they restrained him. In other words, your neighbours were legally obliged to sit on you.

By then, of course, you are long gone from the jarl's bench. After two summers in the bow, you came home, sold the bearskin to a merchant, and went back to breaking plough handles.

The jarl did not stop you. Brand says he was relieved. A berserk who stays too long, the old men at court told you, either becomes a story or a problem, and the jarl had enough of both.

The berserkers did not disappear. They changed shape. In eighteen thirty-one, on the Isle of Lewis, off the west coast of Scotland, a hoard of chess pieces came to light, carved mostly from walrus ivory, probably between eleven fifty and twelve hundred, and commonly thought to have been made in Trondheim, in Norway.

Most of them are now in the British Museum, and the rest in the National Museum of Scotland. Among the warders, the pieces that play the part of rooks, several are wild-eyed warriors biting the tops of their shields.

Two centuries after your time, the berserker had become a game piece: a little ivory man, frozen forever in the middle of his fit, moved around a board by people who had never met one.

And the word kept travelling. According to the word historian Dave Wilton, one of the earliest known uses of the phrase "went berserk" in English comes from a story by Rudyard Kipling, published in eighteen ninety-five. Today, people go berserk over parking spaces.

So why was being a Viking berserker worse than you think? Because the front of the ship was the most exposed place on it, and the fury left you too weak to lift a cup. Because the same stories that praised you turned you into everybody's villain. You could be given away like an object, refused every marriage, and, in the end, outlawed for the very thing that made you famous.

And yet, you are still here: on a chessboard in a museum case, and in a word that half the world uses without knowing where it came from.

That winter, at Bjorn's hearth, Brand recites his poem about you. In his version, you are taller, the other crew is larger, and the split lip is a heroic wound. Everybody knows the truth. Nobody minds.

Orm asks you to do the roar. You refuse. He asks again. You do it once, very quietly, and the dog leaves the house.

You marry a widow from the next valley who is not afraid of anything, least of all you. She makes you carry everything, and she says the bearskin smelled anyway.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. He will ask you to stand at the front. You will say no, and mean it. Because somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped again, and for once, you are the only man in the valley it is afraid of. This is Viking Chapters, the lives behind the legends.
