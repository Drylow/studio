## How to read these notes

Each fact is tagged with the kind of evidence behind it, so the script never presents one kind as another:

- **[PRIMARY: VERSE]**: skaldic verse quoted in later texts (Thorbjörn Hornklofi's *Haraldskvæði*).
- **[SAGA]**: kings' sagas and family sagas written in the 1200s (*Heimskringla*, *Egils saga*, *Eyrbyggja saga*, *Grettis saga*). The script says "the saga says", "according to the saga", "Egil's saga describes".
- **[SNORRI]**: Snorri Sturluson's *Heimskringla* (about 1220–1230), including *Ynglinga saga*.
- **[LATER LAW]**: *Grágás*, the Icelandic law collection; the Christian laws section dates from the early 1100s. Reached through scholarship.
- **[ARCH]**: archaeological objects (Torslunda dies, Lewis chessmen, the Fyrkat grave).
- **[SCHOLARSHIP]**: scholarship, encyclopedias, science journalism, reference sites.

Numbers in the script are written as words. Each was checked against at least two sources, except where noted. Where a detail rests on one text, the script names it in the sentence ("Egil's saga says", "Grettir's saga says", "According to the word historian Dave Wilton") or the detail was cut (see "Cut or softened").

## What is fiction

Ulf (Hallvard's younger son, introduced here), his size, clothes, bearskin and broken plough handles, the wedding, Brand's report, the axe, the jarl's bench and the three big men, the wet bow, the roar practice, the harbour quarrel and his first fit, the split lip and chewed shield rim, the porridge, the steward's daughter, his return home after two summers, the sold bearskin, Brand's poem, the roar for Orm, the widow and the sheep are invented. P03 says so ("you are an imagined person"). The jarl is the same unnamed, fictional jarl as in episode 8; no real ruler is a character. The story is set around 966 to 969, just after episode 8 (Brand's return around 965). Ulf never takes any substance (P33). Every historical example is labelled with its date or source ("the late eight hundreds" for Hornklofi, "around eight hundred and seventy-two" for Hafrsfjord, "the late nine hundreds" for Earl Hákon and the Fyrkat grave, "around ten fifteen" for Earl Eirik's ban, "the early eleven hundreds" for Grágás, "between eleven fifty and twelve hundred" for the Lewis chessmen, "eighteen ninety-five" for Kipling).

  3. Wikipedia, *Berserker* (F01): first joint bear- and wolf-warrior fighting at Hafrsfjord, dated to 872.

### F04: The other side had berserks too: Thor Haklang, "a great berserk", laid his ship against Harald's and fought until he fell and his ship was cleared [SNORRI + SAGA]

### F05: *Ynglinga saga*, ch. 6: Odin's men went without mail-coats, were mad as dogs or wolves, bit their shields, were strong as bears or bulls; neither fire nor iron hurt them; this was called going berserk [SNORRI]

### F07: Torslunda dies: four cast bronze dies, found in 1870 in a cairn in Torslunda parish on Öland; Vendel period (500s–600s); dies for helmet plates; one shows a man wearing a wolfskin, interpreted as an *úlfheðinn* [ARCH + SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P17.
- **Sources:**
  1. Wikipedia, *Torslunda plates*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torslunda_plates ("four cast bronze dies"; "dated to the Vendel Period of the 6th and 7th centuries"; found in 1870 in a cairn; "man with spear wearing wolfskin", "interpreted as a berserker, specifically a Úlfhéðinn"; Swedish History Museum).

### F09: After the fit, berserks were weaker than usual; Kveldulf took to his bed after a fit at sea, died on the voyage to Iceland and asked for a coffin to be put overboard; his name and his evening sullenness [SAGA + SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P23 to P25.
- **Sources:**
  1. *Egil's Saga*, ch. 27 (F03): "It is said of shape-strong men, or men with a fit of Berserk fury on them, that while the fit lasted they were so strong that nought could withstand them; but when it passed off, then they were weaker than their wont. Even so it was with Kveldulf ... became so utterly weak that he lay in bed"; "then make me a coffin, and put me overboard".
  2. *Egil's Saga*, ch. 1 (F03): Ulf rose early, went round his labourers, gave good counsel, "for he was very wise. But everyday as evening drew on he became sullen, so that few could come to speak with him ... He was called Kveldulf."
  3. ScienceNorway (F10): Norse descriptions of a trance-like state "followed by illness and exhaustion" (Fatur's summary).
- **Caveat:** "Kveldulf means evening wolf" is the plain translation of the name (*kveld* evening, *úlfr* wolf). The saga calls him "shape-strong"; the script ties him to the berserk fury as the saga's own sentence does.

### F10: Theories: hallucinogenic mushrooms (fly agaric) suggested since the 1950s; Karsten Fatur (University of Ljubljana, 2019) proposed henbane; fly agaric does not usually provoke aggression; henbane could explain pain tolerance and later headaches; henbane seeds in a woman's grave near Fyrkat, Denmark, late 900s; Fatur calls it speculative; Karoline Kjesrud knows of no description of berserkers ingesting anything; alternatives: alcohol, epilepsy, mental illness, self-induced frenzy [SCHOLARSHIP + ARCH]
- **Used in:** P28 to P31.
- **Sources:**
  1. ScienceNorway, *Crazed Viking warriors may have been high on henbane*: https://sciencenorway.no/drugs-history-plants/crazed-viking-warriors-may-have-been-high-on-henbane/1571431 (Fatur, ethnobotanist, University of Ljubljana; fly agaric does not usually cause violent aggression; anaesthetic effect and pain tolerance; headaches and blurred vision afterwards; "no archaeological findings to prove his theory"; Karoline Kjesrud knows of no descriptions of berserkers ingesting anything before battle; other causes: rituals, epilepsy, mental illness, alcohol).
  2. Wikipedia, *Berserker* (F01): Fabing (1956) and Hoffer (1967) on hallucinogenic mushrooms; "seeds belonging to black henbane" found "in a Viking grave that was unearthed near Fyrkat, Denmark in 1977"; "self-induced hysteria, epilepsy, or mental illness".
  3. - **Caveat:** The often-repeated claim that the fly agaric theory goes back to Samuel Ödman in 1784 was not confirmed by a source read here; the script says only "Since the nineteen-fifties, some researchers". The Fyrkat grave is described as a woman's grave; it is not claimed to be a berserker's.

### F11: The sagas were written by later Christian authors whose berserkers vary with the story: royal guard, Odin's warriors, an enemy to overcome, a thief and bully [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P32, P34.
- **Sources:**
  1. Biolzi (F01): the sources "may appear as an elite military troop, the King's personal guard, invincible mythical warriors of Odin, as a pseudo-totemic enemy to overcome, and finally as a bloodthirsty social outcast"; the berserker was "easily instrumentalized to serve various narrative purposes".
  2. Wikipedia, *Berserker* (F01): early sagas show them as "bodyguards, elite soldiers, and champions of kings"; later ones as "boasters rather than heroes".
- **Caveat:** "two or three centuries after the Viking Age" is a round figure (Viking Age ends around 1050; sagas mostly 1200s).

### F17: *Grágás*, Christian laws section (early 1100s): a man seized by berserk fury incurs lesser outlawry, and so do the men present unless they restrain him; lesser outlawry = three years' exile and loss of property [LATER LAW + SCHOLARSHIP]

- **Sources:**
  1. Biolzi (F01), quoting *Grágás* ed.
