## How to read these notes

Each fact is tagged with the kind of evidence behind it, so the script never presents one kind as another:

- **[PRIMARY: VERSE]**: skaldic verse quoted in later texts, or carved in runes (Karlevi).
- **[SAGA]**: kings' sagas and family sagas written in the 1200s (*Heimskringla*, *Egils saga*). The script says "the saga says", "According to the saga", "A saga written in the twelve hundreds".
- **[SNORRI]**: Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (handbook for poets) and the prologue to *Heimskringla*, both about 1220–1230.
- **[LATER LAW]**: *Grágás*, the Icelandic law collection of the 1100s and 1200s, reached through scholarship. The script says "Icelandic law, written down later".
- **[SCHOLARSHIP]**: peer-reviewed scholarship, encyclopedias, reference sites.

Numbers in the script are written as words. Each was checked against at least two sources. Where a detail rests on one text, the script names it in the sentence ("Hurstwic notes", "Snorri says", "According to his verses", "the saga says") and the general claim behind it has a second, independent source (see each entry), or the detail was cut (see "Cut or softened").

## What is fiction

Brand (Bjorn's nephew, introduced here), his clothes and silver ring, his verse about Bjorn, the jarl's man, the unnamed jarl and his hall, the skirmish poem, the ring thrown to him, the Icelandic rival, the joke about the horse and the night poem, the visit to Eirik's farm, Thora's kenning and the winter recital are invented. P03 says so ("you are an imagined person"). The jarl is unnamed and fictional; no real ruler is a character. The story is set around 965, when Thora (born around 951) is "nearly grown". Every historical example is labelled with its date or source and placed relative to Brand ("only a few years ago" for Eyvind in the 960s, "not long before your time" for Egil at York, "In ten nineteen" for Sigvat, "in ten thirty" for Stiklestad, "the late nine hundreds" for Karlevi).

### F01: Skalds composed court poetry for rulers; dróttkvætt, "that which is recited before the retinue", was the commonest metre [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P05, P10, P24.
- **Sources:**
  1. Margaret Clunies Ross, "Concepts of Truth and Falsehood, Fair Description and Misrepresentation in Medieval Icelandic Writings", *Sydney Studies in Society and Culture*, University of Sydney: https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/SSSC/article/view/8716/8766 (skalds composed "verse in special metres and style of diction for an elite social group, the retinue (called drott) of Norwegian princes and petty kings"; "The commonest metre of skaldic verse was called dróttkvætt, 'that which is recited before the retinue'"; "the earliest known skalds whose works survive were Norwegians").

### F02: Dróttkvætt rules: eight lines; six syllables per line, three stressed, the last unstressed; alliterating pairs; internal rhyme in every line; word order shifted to fit [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P11, P12.
- **Sources:**
  1. Hurstwic, *Skaldic Poetry*: https://hurstwic.com/history/articles/literature/text/Skaldic_Poetry.htm ("Each stanza has eight lines, and each line has six syllables"; three stressed, the last unstressed; alliterating pairs; internal rhyme in all lines; "normal word order was frequently shifted in order to make the meter come out right").
  2. Britannica, *drott-kvaett* (F01, search summary): "stanzas of eight regular lines, each of which has three stresses and ends with a trochee", with internal and terminal rhyme and alliteration.
  3. Wikipedia, *Skald* (F01): internal rhyme and alliteration.
- **Caveat:** The word-order remark is attributed to Hurstwic in the sentence.

### F03: Bragi the Old, a Norwegian of the early 800s, is the earliest skald whose verses survive; after the settlement of Iceland, Icelanders came to dominate the craft [SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P14, P27.
- **Sources:**
  1. Wikipedia, *Skald* (F01): Bragi Boddason, "the earliest skald whose verses survive", Norwegian, early 9th century; from the late 10th century "Icelandic skalds came to dominate at Norwegian courts".
  2. Clunies Ross (F01): "After the settlement of Iceland (c.870–930), however, there was a growing tendency for skaldic poets to be Icelanders rather than Norwegians, and it may be said that Icelanders established themselves as specialists in this art."

### F07: Skaldic poems praised generous rulers; patrons rewarded poets for praise; poets who went too far could be begging for their lives; the "Head-Ransom" genre [MUSEUM + SCHOLARSHIP]
- **Used in:** P24, P36.
- **Sources:**
  1. National Museum of Denmark (episode 2 source, *Power and aristocracy*): skaldic poems praise kings and magnates who gave gold rings to those who fought for them.
  2. Clunies Ross (F01): "The patrons rewarded their poets for effective encomia, but, if the poets overstepped the limits of criticism their patrons would tolerate, might find themselves begging for their lives"; "a special appeasement genre of skaldic verse … the Head Ransom".

### F08: Snorri's prologue: poets praise most the man in front of them, but no one would dare tell a chief about deeds everyone knew were false: "that would be mockery, not praise"; modern scholars are less trusting [SNORRI + SCHOLARSHIP]
