You wanted to be a poet. A seat near the jarl's high table, a gold ring pulled from his own arm, and your verses repeated in every hall for a hundred years. Instead, it is the middle of the night, the lamp is smoking, your head hurts, and you need eight perfect lines by morning.

Somebody has to find the next word. Tonight, that somebody is you.

Imagine you are Brand, Bjorn's nephew, from the valley of our earlier chapters. Like Eirik and Aslaug, you are an imagined person, built from what poems, sagas, laws and scholars tell us, not a real man from history.

You are a slim young man with long light-brown hair tied at the neck, no beard yet, a pale blue tunic, a grey-green cloak, and one thin silver ring on your arm. It is the first thing poetry ever paid you. You polish it more than your shoes.

Popular culture imagines Viking poets as cheerful bards, strumming a harp at a feast. The real court poets of the north, the skalds, worked in one of the most demanding styles of poetry ever invented. It was a job with real money, real danger and very real enemies.

And at the end of this story, we will read a stone on the Swedish island of Öland that carries the only complete skaldic stanza ever found on a runestone.

It starts with Bjorn's voyages. All your life, you have heard him tell the same stories at the hearth: the storm, the raid, the silver, the cut on Eirik's arm that he swears was heroic.

One winter, you put the stories into verse. People stop eating to listen. Bjorn pretends not to be moved, then asks you to say it again, louder, with more about him.

That spring, a jarl's man passing through the valley hears you. The jarl in the north likes poets, he says. Come and try your luck. Your mother cries. Bjorn gives you his second-best cloak. Aslaug gives you bread for the road and a look that says: come back with your head.

The form you will be judged on is called dróttkvætt, court metre. The scholar Margaret Clunies Ross calls it the commonest metre of skaldic verse, and explains that its name means something like: that which is recited before the retinue.

The rules are brutal. Hurstwic, a history site that studies the sagas, sets them out. Each stanza has eight lines. Each line has six syllables, three of them stressed, and it must end on an unstressed one. The lines come in pairs bound together by alliteration, and every single line needs an internal rhyme.

The rules were so tight that, as Hurstwic notes, poets often shuffled the normal word order just to make the metre work. Listeners had to untangle the sentence in their heads, at speed, while drinking.

So imagine a crossword, a riddle and a rap battle, all at once, performed in front of armed men who have been drinking since noon. That is the job.

The art was old. The earliest skald whose verses survive is Bragi the Old, a Norwegian of the early eight hundreds. But after Iceland was settled, Clunies Ross notes, Icelanders increasingly took over the craft, and made themselves its specialists. Somewhere in Iceland, Grim's grandchildren may yet put you out of work.

Then there are the kennings. A kenning is a poetic name for something. You never say the plain word if you can say something stranger.

A ship becomes a wave's horse. A warrior becomes a feeder of ravens, because of what he leaves on the battlefield. One poem calls blood the sea of wounds. Every skaldic battle is, by definition, a very good day for the ravens. In one poem quoted in his saga, Egil, a poet we will meet shortly, calls the English king Athelstan the feeder of the battle-hawk.

Gold has dozens of names. In his handbook for poets, written in the twelve hundreds, the Icelander Snorri Sturluson lists some of them: Sif's hair, Freyja's tears, the seed of the Fyris plains. To understand that last one, you need to know a whole legend.

That was the point. Kennings demanded a deep knowledge of Norse myth and legend. A good poem flattered the patron and the audience at the same time: only clever people could follow it.

Snorri, a Christian writing more than two centuries after the conversion, addressed his handbook to young skalds who wanted to learn the old language of poetry, and told them to master his book. In the same breath, he warned them not to believe in the heathen gods. By his time, the old myths had become a kind of grammar.

Poetry itself had kennings too: Odin's drink, Odin's gift, Kvasir's blood. Snorri tells the myth behind them. Odin stole the mead of poetry from a giant, turned himself into an eagle, flew home, and spat the mead into jars that the gods had set out in their yard.

But the giant was close behind. So, in his hurry, Odin let some of the mead escape out of his back end. Nobody bothered to collect it, Snorri says. Anyone who wanted it could have it. And that, he says, is called the share of the poetasters: the bad poets.

Every skald believes he drank from the jars. Every skald suspects his rivals drank from the other part.

The jarl's hall is the biggest building you have ever seen. Long benches, a fire down the middle, shields on the walls, and a hundred men who all seem to be related to someone important.

Skalds worked mainly at the courts of Norwegian kings and sometimes of jarls. Their main business was praise. The National Museum of Denmark notes that skaldic poems praise kings and magnates who gave gold rings to those who fought for them. Clunies Ross puts it simply: patrons rewarded their poets for effective praise.

Your first poem is about a battle the jarl fought the summer before. You were not there. It was, by most accounts, a short, muddy skirmish. In your poem, the ravens are very well fed.

The jarl listens without moving. Then he pulls a ring from his arm and tosses it to you. It is heavier than your whole cloak. The hall cheers. A rival poet stops cheering rather quickly.

He is an Icelander, of course: a sharp-nosed man in a black cloak who knows a hundred names for gold and uses all of them in every poem. He nods at your ring and says nothing. In a hall full of warriors, he is the one you are most afraid of.

Life at court is less glamorous than the poems. You sleep on a bench near the door, eat at the far end of the table, and spend a great deal of time watching the jarl's face. A good mood means a ring. A bad mood means you are suddenly very interested in the fire.

But there was a limit to flattery. In the prologue to his history of the kings of Norway, Snorri explains why he trusted these poems. Poets, he says, praise most the man they are standing in front of. But no one would dare to tell a chief, to his face, about deeds that everyone listening knew were false. That would be mockery, not praise.

Modern scholars are less trusting, and argue about how far the poems can be relied on. But you learn Snorri's rule fast: exaggerate if you must, but never invent. Everybody in the hall was at that skirmish. Everybody remembers who ran.

The old men at court tell you a story to make the point. Only a few years ago, the saga says, a veteran poet named Eyvind, who had served the generous King Hákon, made a verse about the new kings, the sons of Gunnhild. He mocked them for burying their gold in the ground instead of giving it away, as Hákon had.

King Harald, one of those sons, summoned him and accused him of disloyalty. Eyvind had to hand over his great gold ring, called Molde, as a fine. He went home, and the saga says nothing of him ever coming near that king again. The lesson was clear: the king's gold was for praising the king.

Not every poet got a ring. Some got a death sentence.

The most famous escape belongs to the Icelandic poet and Viking Egil Skallagrímsson, not long before your time. His saga, written down in the twelve hundreds, says that Egil was shipwrecked in Northumbria and fell into the hands of his enemy, King Eirik Bloodaxe, at York.

A friend advised him to stay up all night and compose a great praise poem for the very king who had condemned him. In the morning, Egil recited it. According to the saga, the king was so impressed that he let Egil live. The poem is called Höfuðlausn: the Head-Ransom.

It was not a one-off. Clunies Ross explains that patrons rewarded poets for praise, but a poet who overstepped the limits of what they would tolerate might find himself begging for his life. The Head-Ransom became a whole kind of poem, written to calm a furious patron.

The same saga claims that Egil composed his first poem at the age of three. Your mother says that you composed your first poem at three as well. It was about a sheep. It did not save anyone's head.

Which brings us back to tonight. At supper, you made a joke about the jarl's new horse. It was a good joke. The jarl did not laugh. Now he wants a poem in its honour by morning, and you have a strong feeling that this is a test.

You work until the lamp goes out. By dawn, the horse is the swiftest steed of the north, the terror of the wave's horses, and a credit to its owner. The jarl listens, nods once, and says nothing. You will take nothing as a yes.

Praise was safe. Mockery was dangerous. Clunies Ross points out that a skald's role could also stretch to social and political criticism, and some scholars even think the word skald is related to the English word scold.

Icelandic law, written down later in the law collection called Grágás, took words very seriously. Clunies Ross shows that it was both explicit and severe about verbal offences, especially in poetic form. A lampoon, a mocking poem about someone, could be punished with outlawry.

In the same section of the law, a love poem about a woman carried the same punishment, because of what it implied about her and her family. A single clever verse could cost a man everything he owned.

And yet, poets used insults as weapons, even against kings. A saga written in the twelve hundreds says that when an Icelandic ship was stranded on the coast of Denmark, the Danes seized its cargo as wreck. The Icelanders responded by passing a law that lampoons should be composed about the Danish king, another Harald, and his steward.

How many lampoons? It depends on the translation. One English version says one lampoon for every headland in the king's country. Another says one for every person in Iceland. Either way, it was a great deal of rude poetry.

The saga adds that Harald was so angry that he planned to sail to Iceland in revenge. First, it says, he sent a wizard there in the shape of a whale, to scout the coast. But the land was guarded by spirits: a dragon, a great bird, a bull and a rock giant. The wizard reported back, and the king turned his fleet around and sailed home to Denmark.

Those four guardians, as Heimskringla describes them, still stand on Iceland's coat of arms today. A row about a shipwreck and some very rude poems ended up on a national symbol.

You make a note: never mock the man who feeds you. Then you make another note: never mock his horse.

The Icelander, you notice, never makes jokes at supper. He saves them for verses, which he recites very quietly, to other Icelanders, far from the high table.

Being a court poet also meant travelling, and travelling meant depending on other people's doors.

In ten nineteen, the poet Sigvat Thordarson set out from Norway to Sweden on a mission for his king. The saga preserves his verses about the journey, and they are not happy. A hundred miles through the forest, he complains, and not a single alehouse. Every man in his party had a bleeding sore on each foot.

At a farm called Hof, the door was bolted. The people inside said it was a holy time, and would not let him in. At the next farm, a woman stood in the doorway and told him not to come in: they were heathens there, afraid of Odin's wrath, and busy with a sacrifice to the elves.

According to his verses, more farms turned him away after that, and the man said to be the most hospitable in the district only scowled at him. Sigvat put all of it into poetry. When you are a skald, even your bad days end up in verse.

Your own travels are less dramatic. On your way home that autumn, you stop at Eirik's farm. Thora, who is nearly grown now, asks you to recite. Eirik asks you, politely, to leave him out of it. You do not.

Skalds were also witnesses. The saga of King Olaf says that before the battle of Stiklestad, in ten thirty, the king asked his skalds to stand inside the shield wall. That way, he told them, they would see with their own eyes what happened, and would not have to follow other men's reports in what they later sang.

One of those skalds, Thormod, fell at Stiklestad with his king. A poet was not a spectator. He could be the last witness, or one of the dead.

That was the strange power of the job. Kings died. Halls burned. But a well-made stanza could survive for centuries, memorised and repeated, until someone finally wrote it down. Snorri says that the poems of the skalds at King Harald Fairhair's court, more than three centuries before him, were still known by heart in his day. Most surviving skaldic verse is known to us because later writers quoted it in the sagas, and in Snorri's handbook.

A few poets did something more permanent. Which brings us to Öland. Near Karlevi, on that Swedish island, stands a runestone from the late nine hundreds, raised in memory of a chieftain named Sibbi.

Part of its inscription is a full stanza in dróttkvætt, court metre, with kennings for a chieftain built on the names of gods. It is the only complete skaldic stanza ever found on a runestone. Someone paid to have a court poem carved in stone, so that no one would ever have to remember it again.

So why was being a Viking skald worse than you think? Because the rules were brutal, the audience was armed, and your patron's mood decided whether you got a ring or a death sentence. You could not lie, you could not mock, and you could not write a love poem without risking outlawry.

And yet, your words could outlast the men you praised. Kings are dust. Some of the poems are still being read, a thousand years later.

That winter, at Bjorn's hearth, you recite your new poem about Bjorn's voyages. The storm is bigger, the silver is brighter, and Eirik's arm is wounded in the most heroic way possible. Everybody knows the truth. Nobody minds.

Thora helps you with a difficult line about the storm. Her kenning for the sea is better than yours. You tell her so, which no court poet has ever said to a rival, and she never lets you forget it.

Next spring, Bjorn will talk about another voyage. This time, he will ask you to come along and watch. And somewhere behind the house, the sheep has escaped again, which is, you decide, the start of a very promising poem. This is Viking Chapters: the lives behind the legends.
