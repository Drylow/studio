# The Comanche Raid That Reached the Sea — And Burned a Texas Town

It is dawn, 8 August 1840, at Linnville on Lavaca Bay, and you are about to see how prairie riders reached the sea and burned a Texas town. John Henry Brown wrote that people first take them for Mexican horse traders. On the waterfront, families push into sailboats roughly 100 yards offshore and watch men enter the port. That mistake lasts only minutes, and it's nothing like the paintings. But Linnville is only the middle. The column has passed Victoria, where perhaps 600 riders were seen. It began 5 months earlier, in a San Antonio council room.

## The Port Builder
Twenty miles inland from Lavaca Bay, John J. Linn is in Victoria, not on a boat.

That matters.

Linn was born in County Antrim, Ireland, on 19 June 1798. He became a merchant in New Orleans, came west into Mexican Texas, and settled at Victoria in 1829. Around 1831, he helped found the little port first called New Port on Lavaca Bay, according to his memoir prepared later with Victor M. Rose.

The place will take his name.

By 1839, Linnville is a customs port for the Lavaca district, with roughly 200 people and a line of warehouses sitting close to shallow water. Linn has goods there. So do W. G. Ewing, Judge James W. Robinson, Samuel Maverick, Judge John Hays, and other merchants whose business depends on cloth, hardware, hats, umbrellas, tools, and animal power moving in and out of the bay.

Hugh Oran Watts is customs collector from 1839. He is young, recently married, and tied to the town by office and family. His name will enter the record because he stays too long.

The map is cruelly simple. Victoria sits inland on the Guadalupe. Linnville sits about 20 miles away on Lavaca Bay. A rider can cover that distance fast if the ground is open. A town can believe the water protects it because the sea is in front of it, while the prairie lies behind it with no wall at all.

Texas in 1840 is still a thin republic. Settlements are scattered. Roads are mostly tracks.

From the waterfront, the bay can make the town feel protected.

But every bale in those warehouses has to cross open country first.

San Antonio, 10 January 1840.

## Peace Terms
According to John Henry Brown, 3 Comanche chiefs come into San Antonio on 10 January 1840 asking for peace.

Colonel Henry Karnes gives the hard condition. The prisoners must be returned first.

On paper, that sounds direct. On the ground, it is already dangerous. The Penateka Comanches are under pressure in 1840, as the Handbook of Texas summarizes it. Cheyenne and Arapaho attacks press them from the north. Smallpox has struck. Texas Ranger successes have made war costly for some bands. Peace is attractive to some leaders because horses, trade, captives, and grazing country all depend on movement, and movement is becoming harder.

But Comanche authority is not a single army chain. One chief cannot command every Comanche band scattered across the Comancheria. He cannot simply produce every captive held by every related group, every family camp, or every allied party.

That is the central misunderstanding.

The Texans will treat the next delegation as responsible for all captives. The Comanche delegates will come into a town where a promise made by one group is judged against the actions of many.

On 30 January 1840, Secretary of War Albert Sidney Johnston sends an order that Brown later prints. If the Comanches come in without the prisoners, Lieutenant Colonel William S. Fisher is to hold some of them as hostages. Presents are to be stopped.

Before the March delegation rides into San Antonio, the trap is already written down in official language.

The commissioners appointed to handle the meeting are Hugh McLeod and William G. Cooke. They are not improvising in an empty room. They are operating inside an order from the government of the Republic of Texas, under pressure from families demanding captives back, soldiers who have fought raids for years, and settlers who believe delay means more deaths.

This is where the raid that reaches the sea begins to take shape.

The hostage order would come back to haunt every settlement between San Antonio and Lavaca Bay.

## Through The Fence
San Antonio, Tuesday, 19 March 1840, dia de San Jose.

Mary Maverick is 22 years old, living near the plaza at Commerce and Soledad, and writing decades later from notes and memory when she gives the lived scene. Her sentence is plain: "On Tuesday, 19th of March, 1840, 'dia de San Jose' sixty-five Comanches came into town to make a treaty of peace."

Sixty-five people enter town.

Not all are fighting men. Comanche women and boys wait outside while the chiefs go toward the council. Maverick and Mrs. Higginbotham watch from behind a picket fence. It is close enough to see hands, bows, coins, faces.

Her remembered line is almost ordinary, which is why it sits so uneasily in the record: "The young Indians amused themselves shooting arrows at pieces of money put up by some of the Americans; and Mrs. Higginbotham and myself amused ourselves looking through the picket fence at them."

That is the surface.

Children are shooting at coins. Women are watching through boards. Men are walking into a room where the January order waits for them.

The captive brought in is Matilda Lockhart. Her exact age is not settled. Brown, Maverick, the Lockhart family letter, and the Handbook place her as a teenage girl, roughly 13 to 16. She has been held by Comanches, and her return becomes evidence in the eyes of Texas officials that more prisoners can be produced.

Hugh McLeod's official report gives what Matilda allegedly told the commissioners. He writes: "The little girl was very intelligent and told us that she had seen several of the other prisoners at the principal camp a few days before she left, and that they brought her in to see if they could get a high price for her, and, if so, they intended to bring in the rest, one at a time."

That sentence changes the room.

If McLeod's report is accepted, the officials believe the Comanches are bargaining the captives out one by one. But the source has to be handled carefully. Maverick later described severe injuries to Matilda. Historian Gary Clayton Anderson has questioned whether that decades-later description exaggerated her condition, and the March officials did not describe those injuries in the same way.

Still, the officials believe they have enough.

Now pay attention to Muk-wah-ruh's answer, because it explains why the room is about to close in.

## The Council Room
McLeod's official report, written on 20 March and later printed by John Henry Brown, says the commissioners ask again where the other captives are.

Muk-wah-ruh answers, "We have brought in the only one we had; the others are with other tribes."

That is one of the few Comanche sentences from the meeting that survives. It survives through the report of the opposing side. There is no Comanche written account of the exchange, and no independent recording of the words. Still, the sentence presents a clear mechanical problem. If Muk-wah-ruh is telling the truth, he cannot return prisoners held by other bands. If the Texans believe he is bargaining in bad faith, the hostage order written in January is about to be enforced.

McLeod says the chiefs are told they will remain in custody until the other prisoners are brought in. The delegation resists. The chiefs try to break out of the Council House, and the struggle spreads into the room and around its doors.

This is where the simplified version usually takes over. Later accounts split blame hard, but McLeod's report and Maverick's memoir show a meeting whose rules are understood differently inside the same room. The record is harder than either explanation. A peace delegation has entered a town under rules its members believe protect them. The Texans believe the delegation is withholding captives, and they have already been ordered to hold hostages. The meaning of the meeting has changed, but the men inside do not share the same meaning.

McLeod's report gives the result in one sentence: "After a short but desperate struggle every one of the twelve chiefs and captains in the council house lay dead upon the floor."

Outside, Mary Maverick hears the sound without seeing the room. She later writes, "When the deafening war-whoop sounded in the Court room, it was so loud, so shrill and so inexpressibly horrible and suddenly raised, that we women ... for a moment could not comprehend its purport."

Paterson Colt revolvers were involved in the Council House Fight, according to the Handbook of Texas. Brown specifically names Henry Clay Davis using one of the first Colt revolvers brought into Texas. These weapons offered several shots without the normal pause for loading a muzzleloader, but they did not turn the room into a machine. Men still had to draw, aim, fire, and fight at arm's length.

The reported losses differ. McLeod gives 35 Comanche dead. Maverick gives 33. The Handbook summarizes the deaths as about 30 men and roughly 5 women and children. Texans lost 7 men according to McLeod and Maverick. McLeod reports 8 wounded, while Maverick gives 10, including Mathew Caldwell.

Maverick's sentence for that night is short: "What a day of horrors! And the night was as bad which followed."

The fighting did not end when the bodies were counted. It moved into the calaboose, the mission, the camp outside town, and the broken trust between ambassadors and soldiers.

## Captives Afterward
Maverick records a 12-day truce after the Council House Fight. On 20 March, the widow of one of the dead chiefs is released to carry the terms back to the Comanche camp.

The Comanche prisoners are held first in the calaboose, then at Mission San Jose, according to Maverick. She writes that they did not understand or trust the kindness shown to them, and that most eventually slipped away. From their position, kindness after a killing could look like another form of control.

Maverick also records what Booker L. Webster, a captive boy, reportedly heard from the Comanche camp. The people mourned with howling, cutting themselves, and killing horses. Webster reportedly said that 13 American captives had been killed. That is a second-hand report passed through a child. It is not a confirmed count of every captive death, but it shows the scale of grief and fear carried back from San Antonio.

The exchanges begin soon afterward. McLeod says several white children are returned. A transcription of Catherine Lockhart's 1840 letter says the Comanches returned two American children and seven Mexicans. The numbers do not settle every question, but they show that prisoners remain the center of the dispute.

On 28 March, Maverick says about 250 to 300 Comanches under Isimanica ride to the edge of San Antonio. Isimanica circles the plaza and challenges the Texans. Captain Redd refuses to break the truce.

The Handbook summary of the Council House Fight says Comanches considered ambassadors protected from acts of war. After the Council House killings, many Comanches saw the deaths of their delegates as treachery. The truce held for a time, but the meaning of peace had been damaged beyond San Antonio.

After San Antonio, the next major response came as a mounted raid moving south toward Victoria and the coast.

## Buffalo Hump Moves
The Penateka war chief leading the raid is identified by the Handbook of Texas as Buffalo Hump. His name appears in several forms, including Pochanaquarhip and Ko-cho-naw quoip.

The size of his column is disputed. Maverick says about 300 warriors. Linn sees 600 mounted Comanches at Victoria. Huston later estimates nearly 400 warriors, while Morrell gives 400 to 500. Wilbarger claims 500 to 1,000, and Brown gives about 1,000. The Handbook offers a more careful estimate: perhaps 1,000 people in total, including roughly 400 to 500 warriors.

That difference matters. The column is larger and slower than a small war party disappearing into the grass. The column includes families, Kiowas, and Mexican guides, according to the Handbook. It carries animals, captives, weapons, and the expectation of gathering goods along the route. Every additional person and horse changes how fast it can travel.

There is also a Mexican connection, but the evidence must be stated carefully. Brown says the raid was known to and encouraged by Centralist General Valentin Canalizo. The Handbook reports evidence of a Centralist scheme and says some plunder reached Canalizo and Adrian Woll. That suggests support or cooperation, but it does not prove that Mexican officials controlled every movement of the column.

Two months earlier, Secretary of War Branch T. Archer warned that a major Comanche attack was coming. Brown says nothing came of the warning, and Texans mocked it as the Archer war.

Now the warning becomes physical. On 5 August, Brown says 27 warriors kill Tucker Foley and wound Dr. Joel Ponton near Ponton's Creek. The raiders cross the Gonzales-Columbus road at the Big Hill, about 16 miles east of Gonzales.

The riders are no longer a rumor or a failed negotiation. They are moving across named roads with stolen horses and a coastal target ahead.

By the next afternoon, they are on the outskirts of Victoria.

## Victoria Sees Them
It is the afternoon of 6 August 1840, at Victoria, on the Guadalupe River, about 20 miles inland from the bay.

John J. Linn is there.

Linn later misdates the shock as the fourth day of August, but John Henry Brown's chronology places the main appearance at Victoria on the sixth, after the killing near Ponton's Creek and the crossing at Big Hill. Brown's chronology makes it the same column moving toward the coast, not a separate band near Victoria.

Linn writes that "we of Victoria were startled by the apparition presented by the sudden appearance of six hundred mounted Comanches in the immediate outskirts of the village."

At first, even that does not explain itself.

"The first supposition was that they were Lipans, who occasionally paid us friendly visits," Linn says. That is not foolishness. Lipan Apaches did come near the settlements and trade. A town on the frontier learns to read riders fast, but it does not always read them correctly in the first minute.

Then the scale becomes clear.

If you are in Victoria, you do not see a few traders. You see hundreds of mounted people on the edge of town, with horses already taken, and more animals being driven in. Linn says about 50 men gather what arms they can. Fifty men on foot, with scattered firearms, are not an answer to several hundred mounted raiders in open country.

They can hold buildings. They can crowd together. They cannot control the prairie.

Placido Benavides, the Tejano leader of Victoria, is part of this scene, and he should not be confused with the Tonkawa Chief Placido who appears later at Plum Creek. According to the Handbook of Texas, Benavides's Round Top House is one of the fortified places in town.

Outside that shelter, people die.

Brown gives the list in one hard passage: four enslaved people of Mr. Poage at Spring Creek, Colonel Pinkney Caldwell on the Texana road, an unknown German, a Mexican, three more enslaved people, and Dr. Gray, Varlan Richardson, William McNuner, and Mr. Daniels in the suburbs. Brown's total is 13 killed around Victoria.

The horse loss is larger than the death list. Brown gives the horse loss at more than 1,500, a figure the Handbook of Texas also uses. Wilbarger later raises the broader raid loss to 3,000 to 4,000 head.

Linn remembers the helplessness more than the numbers.

"The panic-stricken citizens all collected at the public square, and all were speculating with agonizing suspense upon the fate that would probably befall us. But fortunately for us, as it was fatal for others, the Indians passed Victoria and proceeded toward the bay, literally sweeping the whole country of horse stock as they went."

That is the part the simple version leaves out.

Victoria is spared full destruction because the column keeps moving.

And where it goes next is worse.

## Placido Creek
On 7 August 1840, the riders are still near Victoria.

Brown says they make another demonstration around the town, but they do not stop there. The pull is toward the bay, toward Linnville, where warehouses sit close to shallow water, full of goods that came by vessel and still have to leave by wagon or pack animal.

Between Victoria and the coast, the column gathers more weight.

Brown writes that young Mrs. Crosby, whom he describes as a granddaughter of Daniel Boone, is captured with her infant at Nine Mile Point. She becomes one more captive in a moving mass that already includes seized animals, fighters, families, and plunder from the countryside.

That night, the raiders camp on Placido Creek, about 12 miles from Linnville. Brown records that a teamster is killed there. The Handbook places the killing in the same night camp on Placido Creek.

This is the last night before the town burns.

The danger is close enough to see by firelight, but still easy to misunderstand. Linn tells the story of W. G. Ewing, the merchant, riding toward Victoria and passing near the camp. Ewing sees the fires and the movement, and his mind supplies the ordinary explanation.

"He imagined the Indian camp was some large Mexican train of wagons going to Linnville for goods."

That mistake fits the country. Mexican traders did move goods. Wagons did come and go. A camp on the road to a port could mean commerce.

But not this camp.

Ewing has another reason to matter. His sister, Juliet Constance Ewing Watts, is in Linnville. According to the Handbook, she has been married to Hugh Oran Watts for only 21 days.

By morning, the column will be at the water.

The raiders have horses, captives, and momentum now. But every animal and every bundle will soon make them slower.

Ewing rides past the campfires and keeps going.

## Linnville Burns
Dawn comes on Sunday, 8 August 1840, at Linnville, on Lavaca Bay.

The town still has time to misunderstand what it sees.

John Henry Brown gives the central scene plainly: "The people, believing the enemy to be friendly Mexicans with horses to sell, realized the fearful truth only in time to escape into the sailboats anchored in shoal water about one hundred yards from shore."

Linnville sits on the bay. The boats are about 100 yards out. The water is shallow enough that men can wade through parts of it, but deep enough that mounted raiders coming by land cannot simply ride out and take the vessels.

If you are on one of those boats, you can see your town. You can see the shore. You are close enough to recognize buildings, animals, and people. But 100 yards of shoal water is now the difference between life and death.

Not everyone reaches it.

Brown and the Handbook say O'Neal and two enslaved men of Major Watts are killed outside town. Hugh Oran Watts is killed in the water. Juliet Watts, an enslaved woman, and her child are captured. Mary Maverick and the Handbook say Watts had gone back for a gold watch.

John J. Linn writes about the destruction of his warehouse and the town that bears his name, but here the record has to be corrected again. Linn is in Victoria. His Linnville pages are second-hand, written from reports that reached him afterward, not from his own eyes on the bay.

Still, he preserves what the refugees endured offshore.

"While the Indians were cutting up fantastic antics before high heaven in Linnville, the refugees on the schooner were the spectators, and witnessed with whatever feelings they could command the wanton destruction of their property."

The looting lasts through the day.

Brown and the Handbook describe feather beds and cloth dragged behind horses. Goods are taken from warehouses. Cattle are penned and slaughtered. By late afternoon, every building but one warehouse has burned.

Linn gives the small detail that later becomes famous.

"In my warehouse were several cases of hats and umbrellas belonging to Mr. James Robinson, a merchant of San Antonio."

Later, he writes of the riders leaving "with Robinson's hats and Robinson's umbrellas bobbing about on every side like tipsy young balloons."

That image is often pulled loose from the event, as if the raid becomes a painting of men in stolen finery.

The record shows warehouses being emptied, captives being carried away, and a coastal port destroyed in a single day. The hats and umbrellas are pieces of plunder from Linnville's storehouses, and later Z. N. Morrell will see one war leader at Plum Creek wearing goods taken from the town.

Even resistance from the water nearly fails before it begins.

Linn says Judge John Hays wades back toward shore through 3 or 4 feet of water to fire an old fusee at the raiders. Afterward, Linn learns the weapon was not loaded.

That is the terror of the day in one object.

Hays had risked his life with a gun that could not fire.

The Handbook places the goods at Linnville at about $300,000. By night, the port is effectively dead.

But reaching the sea is not the end of the raid. It is the point where the column has to turn back carrying everything it has taken.

## Too Much Plunder
Morning comes after Linnville, and the raid has changed shape.

Until now, the Comanche column has chosen the pace. It has moved past Victoria, reached Lavaca Bay, stripped warehouses, taken captives, gathered stock, and burned a port in sight of people sitting helpless in boats.

But now it has to go home.

That is the part the painting misses. A raid that reaches the sea must turn around and cross the same open country with everything tied to it. Horses. Mules. Captives. Cloth. Bedding. Trade goods. Loose animals that don't want to move in a straight line.

Brown and the Handbook say more than 1,500 horses and mules were taken. Wilbarger, writing later, raises the broader stock loss to 3,000 or 4,000. With 1,500 to 4,000 animals, the stolen stock slows the whole column and makes every creek crossing harder.

On 9 August, near Casa Blanca Creek, John Henry Brown places Captain John J. Tumlinson with about 125 men facing the column on open prairie. Linn remembers a similar fight differently, and calls the commander Zumwalt, but Brown's chronology fits the pursuit better.

The chance is there.

Brown says Ben McCulloch urges a charge. The men hesitate. One Comanche warrior is killed. So is Mr. Mordecai of Victoria. Then the moment slips.

That hesitation was not simple fear. Linn writes, "A few of the Indians used guns, the primitive bow and arrow being the arm mainly relied on." Against men on foot, mounted fighters with shields and bows can close, turn, shoot, and leave before a man with a single-shot firearm can reload cleanly.

If you're standing there with one shot in the barrel, the distance matters.

You can die while reaching for powder.

At Casa Blanca Creek, the local force cannot stop the column. McCulloch sees the truth faster than most of them. If these men won't hit hard enough here, riders have to get around the column, spread the alarm, and build a larger fight ahead of it.

For the first time, the pace begins to change.

## The Warning Ride
Ben McCulloch and three men ride out of the failed pursuit and swing around the Comanche column by way of Gonzales.

Brown makes the point sharply. Of the earlier pursuers, he says these four alone go on to fight at Plum Creek. The rest have seen the moving mass, the shields, the bows, the stolen animals, and the risk of charging too soon. They do not all continue.

Now the warning has to outrun the raid.

Z. N. Morrell, the Baptist preacher who later writes as a participant, remembers McCulloch's dispatch only approximately. He says it was about as follows, that the Indians had sacked and burned Linnville, and that the Texans wanted to fight them before they reached the mountains.

The message shows the problem. The column has to be intercepted before it reaches open country.

Distance and time matter first, because the column is trying to regain the open plains with captives and plunder before enough armed men can gather.

Morrell is between the Guadalupe and Lavaca when he crosses the trail. He drives his ox wagons 30 miles in 12 hours to La Grange, then rides on toward Edward Burleson. Oxen do not move like saddle horses. A man pushing wagons that far in half a day is treating the news as an emergency.

The rendezvous, Morrell says, is at Good's on Plum Creek, 27 miles below Austin.

Men begin to come in.

Brown and General Felix Huston put the first fighting body at about 200 men. There are Mathew Caldwell's roughly 100. There are Burleson's 87. There are 13 Tonkawas on foot under Chief Placido. Linn later says about 400 volunteers gathered, and Morrell says nearly 500 were present by night, so the first fight is best understood as roughly 200 men, with more arriving as the day lengthens.

Even command is unsettled.

Huston takes command at the men's request, but Brown says many of them really wanted Caldwell. That matters, because hesitation and authority have already cost one chance near Casa Blanca Creek.

And this Placido is not Placido Benavides of Victoria. Chief Placido at Plum Creek is Tonkawa, the son of a Tonkawa warrior and a Comanche woman, leading men on foot into a fight built around horses.

But the missed detail is this. The next decision depends on weapon range and reload time.

## Bows And Rifles
Just before Plum Creek, the story has to slow down.

Because this fight is often remembered as a frontier charge, with hard men galloping straight into a raiding column. The records put the first turn on weapons, distance, and reload time.

The Comanche force is not armed in one uniform way. Linn says the bow is the main weapon. Huston and Brown describe lances. Morrell, Brown, and Sowell describe buffalo-hide shields. Brown also says some warriors use guns, including muskets and some long-range rifles firing from timber.

That mix matters.

A bow on horseback is a close-range weapon. At short range, a mounted archer can shoot quickly, shift direction, and keep moving while he fires. Walter P. Webb later estimated that while a Texan dismounted and reloaded, a Plains rider could cover several hundred yards and loose many arrows. Webb gives it as an estimate, and it explains the fear.

Now put yourself in the Texan line.

Most of the firearms are single-shot muzzle-loading muskets and rifles. Brown notes some long-range rifles. A man has to pour powder, place ball and patch, ram it down, cap or prime depending on the arm, and bring the barrel back up. If he wants accuracy, a patched ball helps. If he wants speed, an unpatched extra ball loads faster.

Sowell says Huston orders men to load with an extra ball without patching. That tells you what kind of fight he expects.

Huston expects a fast, close fight where speed matters more than perfect loading.

There is also something absent from the record.

There is no source placing Colt revolvers at Plum Creek. Colts belong earlier in this story, at the Council House Fight, where the Handbook notes Paterson revolvers and Brown names Henry Clay Davis using one of the first Colts brought to Texas. The later fame of revolvers with Hays's Rangers comes at Walker's Creek in 1844, according to the Handbook.

Plum Creek is not that fight.

So when Brown says the Texans halt and dismount about 200 yards from the warriors, that distance is a calculation. At roughly 200 yards, rifles may still matter. At much closer range, mounted fighters with shields, bows, and lances can make the reload deadly.

The Comanche shields can turn balls. The Texans need time between shots. The stolen horses and mules slow the retreat. The Linnville goods mark the column for every pursuer who has heard what burned on the bay.

All of it is about to meet in one field near present Lockhart.

## Plum Creek Line
It is 12 August 1840, near the forks of Plum Creek, two or three miles southeast of present Lockhart, in the broken country below Austin. Morrell places the fight there. The Handbook of Texas places it there. Brown says the previous day's ride over burnt prairie had been intensely hot, the kind of heat that turns a pursuit into punishment before the first shot.

Now the column appears.

Brown says it passes about a mile from the Texans, with warriors singing and gyrating, the packed animals moving ahead, and the stolen goods visible in the line. If you are standing with Huston's men, you are not looking at a clean battle line. You are looking at fighters, captives, horses, mules, loose stock, and property from a burned port, all moving together.

Morrell, who was there, remembered one war leader in a way no later painting could improve. "He was dressed in elegant style, from the goods stolen at Linnville, with a high-top silk hat, fine pair of boots and leather gloves, an elegant broadcloth coat, hind part before, with brass buttons shining brightly right up and down his back. When he first made his appearance he was carrying a large umbrella stretched."

The hat and umbrella prove that Linnville's warehouses have reached Plum Creek.

The Texans dismount. The warriors ride forward with shields. Morrell says, "Soon the discovery was made that he wore a shield, and although our men took good aim the balls glanced." Brown writes that "Bands of warriors then began encircling us, firing and using their shields with great effect. From the timber a steady fire was kept up, by muskets and some long range rifles".

The fight begins as a standoff at range, with shields, timber, rifles, bows, lances, and time.

Then one prominent war leader falls. The sources do not agree cleanly enough to name him here. Morrell says Comanche men risk their lives carrying him away. Brown records Mathew Caldwell pressing Huston with the line, "Now, General, is your time to charge them! they are whipped!"

Now the line moves.

In Huston's and Brown's accounts, Burleson's wing works around the point of woods. Caldwell's men charge. The delay breaks.

But what comes next is the part the sources still do not make clean.

## Running Fight
Once the pursuit starts, the field disappears. Morrell says the dust grows so thick that men can see only a short distance. That matters, because a running fight does not leave a neat map. It leaves fragments.

Huston says the chase extends 15 miles. Brown gives 10 to 12 miles. Wilbarger gives 12 to 15. In that distance, the raid that reached the sea is finally being stopped, because it is no longer light. It is carrying captives, goods, horses, mules, and the weight of four days of movement.

The human cost is harder to fix than the distance.

Brown says Mrs. Crosby tries to reach a thicket and is killed by a lance, and he says he saw it. Linn says her infant had already been killed earlier near Linnville. Juliet Watts is found alive. Morrell wrote, "Just as the retreat commenced, I heard the scream of a female voice, in a bunch of bushes close by. Approaching the spot, I discovered a lady endeavoring to pull an arrow out that was lodged firmly in her breast."

Linn and Hall say a steel corset helped keep the wound from being worse. Morrell says Dr. Brown of Gonzales treated her. The accounts do not fit together perfectly, and forcing them to fit would be false neatness.

The unnamed enslaved woman is even harder to recover from the record. Brown says she was wounded and that her son was unhurt. Morrell says he found a white woman and a Black woman both dead. The sources disagree, and they do not record the enslaved woman's name.

The dead are disputed too. Linn gives some 25 Comanche dead. Huston's report says upwards of 40. Hall says about 40. Wilbarger gives 50 to 80, attributing it to Huston. The Handbook says some 50. Brown later gives 86 killed in the action, plus 52 who died afterward, for 138. Huston's contemporary figure, upwards of 40, stands closer to the event than Brown's higher later number.

Huston wrote, as a report and a boast, "I believe we have given the Comanches a lesson which they will long remember; near four hundred of their bravest warriors have been defeated by half their number".

For Texan losses, Huston reports 1 killed, 7 wounded, and 1 mortally wounded. Maverick and Hall say none killed. Even victory does not make the count simple.

The raid is stopped after Linnville because the captured goods and animals slow the column on the return.

## What Remained
After Plum Creek, Huston reports upwards of 200 horses and mules recovered, many packed with Linnville goods. Linn says several hundred head. Brown says the spoils were drawn by lot.

The recovery is small. Ewing gets little back. J. O. Wheeler, who had about 150 horses of his brand, gets little back. Linnville's warehouses, homes, and merchandise are gone, and the town itself is not rebuilt.

In October 1840, Colonel John H. Moore leads about 90 volunteers and 12 Lipans against a Penateka camp on the upper Colorado near present Colorado City, according to Brown and Wilbarger. Brown gives about 130 killed, including women, with 34 women and children captured. Wilbarger gives 48 in the village and 80 at the river, and calls it a war of extermination. Linnville goods are found in the camp.

The Handbook's Penateka entry puts the later move beyond the Red River. Linnville is not rebuilt. Survivors shift about 3 and a half miles southwest to Lavaca, later Port Lavaca. Watts becomes the first burial in Ranger Cemetery. The old Linnville site is now partly under water, with later markers from 1936 and 1964.

Juliet Watts survives, later remarries, keeps a hotel at Port Lavaca, and dies in 1878. Buffalo Hump later meets Sam Houston in 1844, helps lead the Comanche delegation that signs the 1846 Council Springs treaty, moves to the Brazos reservation in 1856, suffers Van Dorn's 1858 attack despite recent peace, and dies in 1870.

The Comanche side of the raid reaches us mostly through what Texans wrote down.

And Linn was not offshore. He was in Victoria, on the square, while the town that bore his name burned 20 miles away, waiting as the riders passed toward the bay.
