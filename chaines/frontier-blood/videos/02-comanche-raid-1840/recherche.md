NOTES: "The Comanche Raid That Reached the Sea — And Burned a Texas Town"
Channel: Frontier Blood. Event: the Great Comanche Raid of 1840 (Council House Fight, San Antonio, 19 March 1840 ->
attack on Victoria, 6-7 Aug -> sack and burning of Linnville on Lavaca Bay, 8 Aug -> Battle of Plum Creek near
present Lockhart, 12 Aug 1840). Republic of Texas, coastal plain between the Guadalupe and Lavaca rivers.

!! CORRECTION TO THE BRIEF: John J. Linn was NOT at Linnville and did NOT flee by boat. He writes "We of Victoria
were startled..." and waited on Victoria's square ("the fate that would probably befall us"). Linnville, 20 miles
away, was the port he founded, where his warehouse burned; his Linnville pages are second-hand. Narrate him as the
Victoria eyewitness and the man whose town burned.

Context: Victoria, 7 Aug, after a second day of riders around the town.
4. “On Tuesday, 19th of March, 1840, "dia de San Jose" sixty-five Comanches came into town to make a treaty of peace.” — MARY MAVERICK. Context: San Antonio; she lived on the plaza, aged 22.

6. “The little girl was very intelligent and told us that she had seen several of the other prisoners at the principal camp a few days before she left, and that they brought her in to see if they could get a high price for her, and, if so, they intended to bring in the rest, one at a time.” — COL. HUGH McLEOD, official report of 20 March 1840 (printed in Brown). Context: Matilda Lockhart, the only captive brought in.
7. “'We have brought in the only one we had; the others are with other tribes.'” — the Comanche chief Muke-war-rah (Muguara), as recorded by McLEOD. Context: the Council House, asked where the other captives were. One of the very few Comanche sentences we have; recorded by the other side.

Context: the moment the fight began; she did not see inside the room.
10. “What a day of horrors! And the night was as bad which followed.” — MAVERICK. Context: night of 19 March.
11. “He imagined the Indian camp was some large Mexican train of wagons going to Linnville for goods.” — LINN. Context: night of 7 Aug; merchant W. G. Ewing rode past the campfires on Placido Creek toward Victoria; his sister, Mrs. Watts, was in Linnville.
12. “The people, believing the enemy to be friendly Mexicans with horses to sell, realized the fearful truth only in time to escape into the sailboats anchored in shoal water about one hundred yards from shore.” — JOHN HENRY BROWN. Context: dawn, Sunday 8 Aug, Linnville.

14. “In my warehouse were several cases of hats and umbrellas belonging to Mr. James Robinson, a merchant of San Antonio.” ... “with Robinson's hats and Robinson's umbrellas bobbing about on every side like tipsy young balloons.” — LINN. 15. “A few of the Indians used guns, the primitive bow and arrow being the arm mainly relied on.” — LINN. Context: the skirmish east of Victoria (Brown dates it 9 Aug, Casa Blanca Creek).

VERIFIED FACTS (source in each line; disputed numbers as ranges)
The people
- Linn: born 19 June 1798, County Antrim, Ireland; New Orleans merchant; settled at Victoria 1829; wharf and warehouse at Linnville about 1831; Victoria's first mayor (1839); memoirs 1883, ghostwritten by Victor Marion Rose; died 27 Oct 1885 in the house he had built 56 years earlier (Handbook, "Linn, John Joseph").
- Linnville: founded 1831 as New Port; about 200 people c. 1839; customs house of the Lavaca district; warehouses of Linn, W. G. Ewing, Judge James W. Robinson, Samuel Maverick, Judge John Hays and others; Hugh Oran Watts customs collector from 1839 (Handbook, "Linnville, TX"). About 20 miles from Victoria (same).
- John Henry Brown (1820-1895): lived with his uncle James Kerr on the Lavaca; private at Plum Creek; later editor, legislator, mayor of Galveston and of Dallas, historian (Handbook). He says he gathered notes "at once" and lectured on the raid at Lavaca in 1871 (his book).
- Mary Maverick, 22 in 1840, wife of Samuel Maverick, lived on San Antonio's Commerce/Soledad corner (her memoir).
The Council House Fight, 19 March 1840
- 10 Jan 1840: three Comanche chiefs asked for peace at San Antonio; Karnes demanded all prisoners first (Brown). Penateka motives: Cheyenne and Arapaho attacks, smallpox, Ranger successes (Handbook, "Council House Fight").
- The hostage plan was ordered in advance: Johnston's order of 30 Jan told Lt. Col. Fisher to march three companies to San Antonio and, if the Comanches came without the prisoners, hold some as hostages; presents to be stopped (Brown prints the order). Commissioners: McLeod and William G. Cooke.
- Who came: 65 Comanches incl. women and children (Maverick; Brown; McLeod's tally adds up to 65); Handbook: 33 chiefs and warriors plus 32 others; leader the peace chief Muk-wah-ruh (Muguara). Captives brought: Matilda Lockhart only (McLeod), Matilda and a Mexican (Maverick), Matilda and several Mexican children (Handbook).
- Muk-wah-ruh said the other captives were held by other bands; the Handbook says the commissioners failed "to comprehend the diffuse nature of Comanche political authority."
- Dead (ranges): Comanches 35 (McLeod: 30 chiefs and warriors, 3 women, 2 children) / 33 (Maverick) / about 30 men plus some 5 women and children (Handbook); 12 chiefs killed in the room (McLeod). Prisoners 29 (McLeod), 32 (Maverick), 27 (Handbook). Texans: 7 killed (McLeod; Maverick: 6 Americans and 1 Mexican), 8 wounded (McLeod) or 10 (Maverick), incl. Capt. Mathew Caldwell.
- Weapons that day: Paterson Colt revolvers "are known to have been involved in the Council House Fight" (Handbook, "Colt Revolvers"); Brown names Henry Clay Davis killing a man with "one of the first lot of Colt's revolvers ever brought to Texas".
- Contemporary criticism: Brown admits "a few papers in the United States ... criticised the affair with more or less condemnation". Historian Gary Clayton Anderson ("The Conquest of Texas", 2005) calls it the "Council House Massacre" (cited by Wikipedia; Anderson not read directly).
- The Handbook: the fight "outraged Comanche sensibilities, for they considered ambassadors immune from acts of war"; Comanches regarded Texans as treacherous from then on.
Captives on both sides
- Matilda Lockhart: captured late 1838 (Maverick: December 1838; Catherine Lockhart's letter: 9 Dec) with four Putnam children (Handbook, "Lockhart, Matilda"); age about 13 at capture and 15 at release (Maverick); "about thirteen" at release (Brown); about 13 at capture (Handbook, "Lockhart, Matilda"); "about sixteen" at capture (Catherine Lockhart's letter); 16 at release (Handbook CHF). Say "a teenage girl, 13 to 16".
- Maverick (writing decades later) describes burns and a nose "burnt off to the bone" and says Matilda told of "fifteen other American captives". Anderson notes officials in March 1840 did not mention abuse and says "Maverick may have exaggerated Lockhart's condition" (Anderson p. 419, quoted in Michno & Michno, "A Fate Worse Than Death", 2007, seen via archive.org search). Catherine Lockhart's letter of 9 April 1840 describes the fight but no injuries. PRESENT MATILDA'S INJURIES AS MAVERICK'S ACCOUNT, WITH THE DOUBT.
- After: a 12-day truce; a chief's widow freed on 20 March to carry the terms; prisoners held in the calaboose, then San José Mission (Maverick). Maverick records the boy Booker L. Webster saying the band mourned (howling, cutting themselves, killing horses) and killed 13 American captives; this is a single second-hand report. Some captives were exchanged soon after: "several white children" (McLeod via Brown); "two American children and seven Mexicans" (Catherine Lockhart, transcription).
- 28 March: 250-300 Comanches under Isimanica rode to the edge of San Antonio; he circled the plaza challenging the Texans; Capt. Redd refused to break the truce (Maverick).
The raid: leaders, numbers, route
- Leader: Buffalo Hump, Penateka war chief (Handbook); name recorded as Pochanaquarhip and Ko-cho-naw quoip, among other forms (Handbook, "Buffalo Hump"; "Potsanaquahip" is a common modern spelling). Party included Kiowas and Mexican guides (Handbook, "Linnville Raid").
- Size (all estimates): Linn 600 at Victoria; Maverick about 300 warriors; Morrell 400-500 from the trail, "supposed" 500 at Plum Creek; Huston "near four hundred of their bravest warriors"; Wilbarger 500-1,000; Brown about 1,000; Handbook: perhaps 1,000 with families, warriors probably 400-500; Handbook "Buffalo Hump": nearly 400 warriors plus as many women and children.
- Mexican link: Brown says the raid was "known to and encouraged by" Centralist Gen. Valentín Canalizo; the Handbook reports "evidence" of a Centralist scheme and that plunder reached Canalizo and Adrián Woll. Present as evidence/claim.
- Warnings: Sec. of War Branch T. Archer warned two months before; nothing came, so it was mocked as the "Archer war" (Brown).
- 5 Aug: Tucker Foley killed and Dr. Joel Ponton wounded near Ponton's Creek, Lavaca County, by 27 warriors (Brown). Raiders crossed the Gonzales-Columbus road at the Big Hill, about 16 miles east of Gonzales (Brown).
- 6 Aug, Victoria: four enslaved people of Mr. Poage killed at Spring Creek, Col. Pinkney Caldwell on the Texana road, an unknown German, a Mexican and three more enslaved people; Dr. Gray, Varlan Richardson, William McNuner and Mr. Daniels killed in the suburbs: "a total of thirteen" (Brown). About 1,500 horses and mules taken (Brown; Handbook "over 1,500"). Plácido Benavides' fortified Round Top House served as a citadel (Handbook).
- 7 Aug: second demonstration at Victoria; young Mrs. Crosby (Brown: granddaughter of Daniel Boone) and her infant captured at Nine Mile Point; night camp on Placido Creek, about 12 miles from Linnville, where a teamster was killed (Brown, Linn, Handbook).
- 8 Aug (Sunday), Linnville: O'Neal and two enslaved men of Maj. Watts killed outside town; raiders taken for Mexican horse traders; boats ~100 yards out in shoal water; Watts killed in the water; his bride of 21 days Juliet Constance (née Ewing), an enslaved woman and her child captured (Brown; Handbook). Maverick and the Handbook: Watts had gone back for a gold watch. Refugees on boats and a schooner (captain William G. Marshall, Handbook). Looting took all day; feather beds and cloth dragged behind horses; cattle penned and slaughtered (Handbook). Every building but one warehouse burned (Brown). Goods valued at $300,000 at Linnville (Handbook).
- 9 Aug: Capt. John J. Tumlinson's ~125 men faced the column on open prairie near Casa Blanca Creek; McCulloch urged a charge, the men hesitated; one warrior and Mr. Mordecai of Victoria killed (Brown). Linn dates this the 7th and calls the commander Zumwalt ("Zumaldt").
- Killed in the raid: 20 persons from Foley to Mordecai (Brown); 23 known, incl. 8 Black people and 1 Mexican (Handbook, "Linnville Raid"); 25 Texans (Handbook, "Comanche Indians").
The pursuit, the weapons, why they mattered
- McCulloch's dispatch reached Burleson (text as Morrell recalled it "about as follows": "The Indians have sacked and burned the town of Linnville ... we want to fight them before they get to the mountains"). McCulloch and three men rode around the column via Gonzales; Brown: "these four alone" of the pursuers fought at Plum Creek.
- Morrell crossed the trail between the Guadalupe and Lavaca, drove his ox wagons 30 miles in 12 hours to La Grange, then rode to Burleson (Morrell). Rendezvous: Good's, on Plum Creek, 27 miles below Austin (Morrell).
- Comanche arms: mainly bows (Linn); lances (Huston: Mrs. Crosby "was speared"; Brown: lance); buffalo-hide shields that turned rifle balls (Morrell, Brown, Sowell); Hall (1898, transcription) says they carried "new white shields". McLeod: arrows "driven to the feather".
- Texan arms: single-shot muzzle-loading muskets and rifles, "some long range rifles" (Brown); they dismounted to fire (Huston; Brown: halted ~200 yards out). Huston had men load an extra unpatched ball, i.e. a quicker, looser second load (Sowell; the patch/no-patch trade-off is general firearms background, not in the source). No source found puts Colt revolvers at Plum Creek; Colts won their fame with Hays's rangers at Walker's Creek, 1844 (Handbook).
- Why it mattered: Walter P. Webb ("The Great Plains", 1931, pp. 168-169) estimated that while a Texan dismounted and reloaded, a Plains rider "could in that time ride three hundred yards and discharge twenty arrows". An often-repeated estimate, not a measurement; copyrighted, paraphrase or attribute.
- What slowed the Comanches: about 2,000 captured horses and mules and pack animals (Brown; Wilbarger says 3,000-4,000), captives and loot; at Plum Creek warriors fought a delaying action so "the packed mules might be driven ahead with the spoils" (Morrell).
Plum Creek, 12 August 1840
- Texans about 200: Caldwell's ~100 incl. Bird and Ward + Burleson's 87 and 13 Tonkawas on foot under Chief Plácido (Brown); Huston: about 100 + about 100; Morrell about 200 at first, near 500 by night; Linn says "some four hundred volunteers". Huston took command at the men's request; Brown says the men really wanted Caldwell.
- Site: forks of Plum Creek, two or three miles southeast of present Lockhart (Morrell); now Caldwell County, named for Mathew Caldwell (Handbook).
- 11 Aug "intensely hot", ride over burnt prairie (Brown). Fight: Texans dismounted; leaders rode in front with shields; one leader fell, the warriors howled, Caldwell urged the charge; Burleson's wing went round the point of woods, Caldwell's charged in (Huston, Morrell, Brown). Dust so thick men saw only a short way (Morrell). Running fight 12-15 miles (Huston 15; Brown 10-12; Wilbarger 12-15).
- Captives: Mrs. Crosby killed by a lance as she tried to reach a thicket (Brown, who says he saw it); her infant had been killed earlier near Linnville (Linn). Mrs. Watts hit by an arrow, saved partly by her corset (Linn; Hall) and treated by Dr. Brown of Gonzales (Morrell). The enslaved woman: Brown says wounded and her son unhurt; Morrell found "a white woman and a negro woman, both dead". Accounts differ.
- Comanche dead (ranges): 14-15 before the rout "and a great many more" (Morrell); "some 25" on the field (Linn); "upwards of forty" (Huston); about 40 (Hall); 50-80 (Wilbarger, attributing it to Huston); "some fifty" (Handbook, "Comanche Indians"); 86 in the action plus 52 who died later, 138 (Brown, partly from prisoners' reports). Two prisoners, a woman and a child (Huston).
- Texan losses: 1 killed, 7 wounded, 1 mortally (Huston); none killed (Maverick; Hall). Reed of Bastrop shot through the lungs, survived (Brown).
- Recovered: upwards of 200 horses and mules, many packed with Linnville goods (Huston); "several hundred head" (Linn). Spoils were drawn by lot (Brown); Ewing and J. O. Wheeler (150 horses of his brand) got little back (Linn).
Hats, umbrellas, ribbons: what is actually documented
- Linn (1883): Robinson's cases of hats and umbrellas in his warehouse; raiders dashed about the burning town with them.
- Morrell (1872, eyewitness): ONE war leader at Plum Creek in a high-top silk hat, boots, gloves, broadcloth coat worn back to front, carrying a large open umbrella, a long red ribbon on his horse's tail.
- Maverick: scouts saw bolts of ribbon, muslin and calico streaming from saddles; her husband's law books on saddle strings. Wilbarger: horses decked with red streamers and ribbons. Sowell: a warrior in a broad-brimmed Linnville hat mistaken for McCulloch. Hall (1898): cloth coats buttoned behind, stolen hats, calico on horses.
Aftermath
- Oct 1840: Col. John H. Moore with about 90 volunteers and 12 Lipans attacked a Penateka camp on the upper Colorado near present Colorado City; about 130 killed incl. women, 34 women and children captured, Linnville goods found in the camp (Brown; Wilbarger: 48 in the village + 80 at the river; he calls it "a war of extermination"). Penatekas then moved beyond the Red River (Handbook, "Comanche Indians").
- Linnville was not rebuilt; survivors moved 3.5 miles southwest to Lavaca (Port Lavaca); Watts was the first burial in Ranger Cemetery; the site is now partly under water, with markers from 1936 and 1964 (Handbook).
- Juliet Watts remarried (Stanton per the Handbook, Fretwell per Linn and Hall), kept a hotel at Port Lavaca and died in 1878 (Maverick; Wilbarger note; Handbook).
- Buffalo Hump: met Sam Houston in 1844; led the Comanche delegation that signed the 1846 Council Springs treaty with the US; moved to the Brazos reservation 1856; his band was attacked by Van Dorn's troops in 1858 despite a recent peace; died 1870 (Handbook).
- Ben McCulloch: killed at Pea Ridge, 7 March 1862, as a Confederate general (Handbook). Caldwell died in Gonzales, 28 Dec 1842 (Handbook). Plácido was the son of a Tonkawa warrior and a Comanche woman (Handbook).
THE COMANCHE SIDE (what exists)
- No first-person Comanche account of the 1840 raid was found. Comanche words survive only as recorded by Texans (Muk-wah-ruh's reply; Isimanica's challenge) and via captives (Juliet Watts: placed under "an old chief", given to an old woman, made to read books, strapped to a mule; Booker Webster on the mourning). Say so on screen.
- Historians' framing (paraphrase only): revenge for envoys killed under truce (Handbook); Comanche authority spread across bands, so one chief could not hand over every captive (Handbook); captive-taking and ransom were part of Plains warfare on all sides (Handbook, "Indian Captives"). Pekka Hämäläinen ("The Comanche Empire", 2008) was NOT read this session: do not attribute specific claims to him.

STORY SHAPE (~38 min, chronological; Linn's Victoria eyes as the spine)
1. Cold open (0-3): dawn, Sunday 8 Aug 1840, Linnville: a dust cloud taken for Mexican horse traders; people splash to boats 100 yards out; Watts turns back for a watch. Twenty miles away, the man whose name the town bore had seen the same riders two days before (Q1).
2. The merchant and his port (3-7): Linn, Antrim to Victoria; Linnville's warehouses; Texas in 1840; the Penatekas and why they sought peace.
3. The Council House (7-12): the hostage order of 30 Jan; 19 March through Maverick's fence (Q4, Q5); Matilda (Q6); Muk-wah-ruh's answer (Q7); the order, the war-whoop, twelve chiefs dead (Q8, Q9, Q10); Colts in the room; the dead on both sides.
4. Captives on both sides (12-15): Matilda's story and the doubt about it; Comanche prisoners in the calaboose, the messenger widow, the mourning, Webster's report, the exchange, prisoners slipping away; Isimanica's challenge.
5. Buffalo Hump's answer (15-18): leader, numbers as ranges, families, Kiowas, the Mexican link; past the Big Hill; Foley killed.
6. Victoria (18-22): 6 Aug: Lipans? (Q1, Q2); thirteen dead, 1,500 horses; the square (Q3); Wheeler's race; Mrs. Crosby taken; Ewing passes the campfires (Q11).
7. Linnville burns (22-27): the boats (Q12, Q13); Watts killed, Juliet taken; a day of looting; Robinson's hats and umbrellas (Q14); Judge Hays's unloaded gun; becalmed refugees; every building but one burned.
8. Bows against rifles (27-30): Casa Blanca, the hesitation (Q15); weapons explained (bows, lances, shields vs single-shot rifles, dismounted fire, Webb's estimate); McCulloch rides round the column; Morrell's 30 miles; Burleson, Plácido and the Tonkawas.
9. Plum Creek (30-35): 12 Aug, 200 Texans, the column a mile off; the man with the silk hat and umbrella (Q16, Q17); shields and dust (Q18); the fallen leader, the howl, the charge; Mrs. Crosby killed, Mrs. Watts found (Q19); 15 miles of running fight; the dead as ranges (Q20).
10. After (35-38): spoils by lot, owners left with nothing; Moore's October attack on a Penateka camp; Linnville abandoned for Port Lavaca; Juliet Watts's hotel; Buffalo Hump's treaty and death; McCulloch at Pea Ridge; Linn, in his eighties, writes it down with Victor Rose (1883), dies 1885; Comanche voices missing. Close on the square at Victoria (Q3) or Morrell's "How long shall these things be?"

RULES
- Linn was in VICTORIA, not on the boats (see top). His Linnville and Plum Creek pages are hearsay: "Linn wrote", "Linn was told". His dates are off (Victoria on "the fourth"; the Casa Blanca skirmish on "the 7th"): use Brown's dates (6, 7, 8, 9, 12 Aug).
- Linn's hats line: use only the version in Q14. The Handbook, Wikipedia and several modern books print "with Robinson's hats on their heads and Robinson's umbrellas"; "on their heads" is NOT in the 1968 reprint or the transcription. Never read the slurs ("screeching squaws", "little Injuns") or "savages", "red devils", "wily", "fiendish" in narration; if a quote holds such a word, cut it or choose another.
- Numbers always as ranges with who gives them: raiders 300-1,000; Comanche dead at Plum Creek ~25-138 (most contemporary: "upwards of forty"); settlers killed 20-25; horses 1,500-4,000; Texans ~200. Huston's "near four hundred ... by half their number" is a boast in a report, not a fact.
- Council House: say plainly the Comanches came to negotiate, Texas had ordered hostage-taking in advance, the chiefs fought when told they were prisoners, women and children died. Never call the raid "unprovoked"; justify no one's killings.
- Matilda Lockhart's injuries: Maverick's account, written decades later, questioned by Anderson. No close-up images of her face. Webster's report of 13 captives killed: one boy's report, not established fact.
- Mrs. Crosby, her infant, Watts, the enslaved people killed: state once, plainly, no gore, no close-up. Name enslaved victims as enslaved men and women; their names are not recorded in these sources. Do not invent names.
- Leave out sensational late claims: Hall's Tonkawa cannibalism and "headless rider" (1898), Dewees' human-flesh story (Brown calls Dewees false on Plum Creek), Wilbarger's raiders hidden on their horses' flanks and his Tonkawa trophy-cutting.
- Two different Plácidos: Plácido Benavides (Victoria rancher, Placido Creek, Round Top House) and Plácido, Tonkawa chief at Plum Creek.
- Buffalo Hump: Handbook names him leader; spelling of his Comanche name varies (Pochanaquarhip etc.); never translate it (the Handbook says its sense is not precisely recorded). Do not name the war leader shot at Plum Creek or the man Brown killed; sources do not agree.
- Hats/umbrellas: Linn's cases plus one umbrella-carrier seen by Morrell, not an army in top hats. Revolvers: Council House only. Webb's "twenty arrows" is an estimate.
- Title promise: the raid is in history books (Handbook of Texas, Brown, Wilbarger); never claim it was hidden or censored. "Too brutal" = what the sources describe, told soberly.
- No real modern people, no invented dialogue; spoken lines only as quoted above (Muk-wah-ruh, Caldwell, McCulloch's dispatch "about as follows").
