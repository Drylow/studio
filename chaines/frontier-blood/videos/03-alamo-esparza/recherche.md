VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim; never rephrase)
1. “All the others are dead. I alone live of they who were within the Alamo when it fell.” — Esparza, 1907, as quoted in the Handbook of Texas. Context: opening of his interview with Barnes, aged about 78. The Handbook continues the passage: “It is burned into my brain and indelibly seared there.”
2. “It took the whole day to move and an hour before sundown we were inside the fort.” — Esparza 1902. Context: 23 Feb 1836; John W. Smith brought word that Santa Anna had come; Gregorio chose the fort and the family followed.
3. “just as we got to it we could hear Santa Anna's drums beating on Milam Square; and just as we were crossing the ditch going into the fort Santa Anna fired his salute on Milam Square.” — Esparza 1902. Context: the bridge over the river near today's Commerce Street, at sundown.
4. “The first thing I remember after getting inside the fort was seeing Mrs. Melton making circles on the ground with an umbrella. I had seen very few umbrellas.” — Esparza 1902. Context: first evening; Juana Melton (née Losoya), wife of quartermaster Eliel Melton.
5. “The Mexicans had a cannon somewhere near where Dwyer Avenue now is and every fifteen minutes they dropped a shot into the fort.” — Esparza 1902. Context: the siege bombardment.
6. “I remember Crockett. He was a tall, slim man with black whiskers. He was always at the head. The Mexicans called him Don Benito.” — Esparza 1902. Context: the siege; the man warmed his hands at the families' fire and spoke to them in Spanish. See RULES on "Don Benito".
7. “During the armistice my father told my mother she had better take the children and go, while she could do so safely. But my mother said: "No! If you're going to stay, so am I. If they kill one they can kill us all."” — Esparza 1902. Context: a pause he places about a week into the siege (see RULES).
8. “About two o'clock in the morning there was a great shooting and firing at the northwest corner of the fort and I heard my mother say: "Gregorio, the soldiers have jumped the wall. The fight's begun."” — Esparza 1902. Context: the last night; his parents asleep together in the quarters. His hour conflicts with other accounts (RULES).
9. “He got up and picked up his arms and went into the fight. I never saw him again.” — Esparza 1902. Context: right after quote 8; Gregorio served a cannon.
10. “It was so dark that we couldn't see anything and the families that were in the quarters just huddled up in the corners. My mother's children were near her.” — Esparza 1902. Context: they heard officers shouting to the men to jump over, and the fighting close by.
11. “The Mexicans fired into the room for at least fifteen minutes. It was a miracle, but none of us children were touched.” — Esparza 1902. Context: soldiers fired into the dark room; he says a boy wrapped in a blanket in a corner was killed (state once, plainly).
12. “By daybreak the firing had almost stopped and through the window we could see shadows of men moving around inside the fort.” — Esparza 1902. Context: first light, 6 March.
13. “Then an officer stepped in and said: "What are you doing? The women and children are not to be hurt."” — Esparza 1902. Context: a soldier had pointed his bayonet at his mother demanding the Americans' money; she replied "If they had any, you may look for it." The officer told each woman to gather her family and belongings.
14. “Capt. Dickinson galloped up to our dwelling and hurriedly exclaimed: "The Mexicans are upon us, give me the babe, and jump up behind me."” — Susanna Dickinson (Morphis, 1874). Context: 23 Feb; she rode in behind Almeron with Angelina, 14 months old.
15. “Great God, Sue, the Mexicans are inside our walls! All is lost! If they spare you, save my child.” — Almeron Dickinson, as his widow remembered (Morphis, 1874). Context: he ran into the church where she hid with the baby, then went back into the fight; she never saw him again.
16. “At this moment a Mexican officer came into the room, and, addressing me in English, asked: 'Are you Mrs. Dickinson?' I answered 'Yes.' Then said he, 'If you wish to save your life, follow me.'” — Dickinson (Morphis, 1874). Context: end of the fighting, in the church; she says she was "shot at and wounded" but spared.
17. “He mounted the wall, and called to his men--"Come on Boys, the Mexicans are upon us, and we'll give them Hell."” — Joe, as reported by Gray (Alexandria Gazette, 1836). Context: just before daybreak; Adjutant John Baugh had run into Travis's quarters crying "the Mexicans are coming"; Travis took rifle and sword, Joe his gun. (Comma after "us" faint in this print.)
18. “After the fight, when they were searching the houses, an officer called out in English "are there any negroes here?" Joe then emerged from his concealment, and said, "Yes here's one."” — Joe via Gray (1836). Context: Joe had hidden in a building; two soldiers then attacked him (a bayonet scratch, one buckshot in the side) until a Captain Baragan stopped them. "Negroes" is the 1836 text's word: never in narration.
19. “Victory belongs to the Army, which at this very moment, 8 o'clock A.M., achieved a complete and glorious triumph that will render its memory imperishable.” — Santa Anna's report of 6 March 1836 (1886 printing). Context: written at Béxar that morning for Mexico City.
20. “I myself wrote that report and must now confess that I put down that number at the command of His Excellency. In stating the truth now, I must say that only 183 men were killed.” — Ramón Martínez Caro, 1837 (Castañeda 1928). Context: his footnote on the report's "more than six hundred" rebel dead ("I my-/self" split by a page break in the scan).

VERIFIED FACTS (source on each line; disputed numbers as ranges)
Background
- Santa Anna's abolition of the 1824 Constitution set off civil war in Mexico and the Texas revolt, which began at Gonzales on 2 Oct 1835 (Alamo official site). Texans and Tejanos took San Antonio from Gen. Cos in Dec 1835 (Handbook of Texas, "Alamo, Battle of the", Stephen L. Hardin).
- Slavery: Mexico's 1829 abolition decree exempted Texas; about 5,000 enslaved people lived there in 1836; the 1836 Texas constitution protected slavery (Handbook, "Slavery").
- Houston did not order the Alamo abandoned: on 17 Jan 1836 he asked Governor Smith's permission to blow it up; Smith refused (Hardin; Alamo site, "Myths and Legends").
People
- Enrique Esparza: son of Gregorio Esparza and Ana Salazar; probably born Sept 1828, though he claimed 1824 (Handbook, "Esparza, Enrique"). In the fort with his mother, half-sister María de Jesús Castro (b. 1826), brothers Manuel (b. 1830) and Francisco (b. 1833); church records show four children, not the baby he described (Handbook, "Alamo Noncombatants", Bill Groneman).
- Gregorio (José María) Esparza: born 25 Feb 1802 in San Antonio (so 34 during the siege); joined Juan Seguín's Tejano company Oct 1835; family entered at twilight on 23 Feb through a small window of the church; tended a cannon; killed 6 March (Handbook, "Esparza, José María").
- His brother Francisco, by his own account in the local Mexican presidial company until Cos surrendered in Dec 1835, got permission to find Gregorio's body and buried it in the Campo Santo: the only defender given a Christian burial (Handbook). The 1902 reporter calls the uncle "an assailant"; that he fought in the assault is UNVERIFIED.
- Other Tejano defenders killed: José Toribio Losoya (born 1808 in the Alamo barrio; his mother, brother and sister survived inside), Juan Abamillo, Juan Antonio Badillo, Antonio Fuentes, Andrés Nava (Handbook entries). Seguín left as a courier, leaving seven of his men (Handbook, "Losoya").
- Travis, 26 (born 1809), regular officer; Bowie (born prob. 1796), elected by the volunteers; joint command from 13 Feb; Bowie collapsed 24 Feb (pneumonia, typhoid or probably advanced tuberculosis) and lay on a cot (Handbook, "Bowie"). Crockett (born 1786, so 49) arrived about 8 Feb with twelve men (same).
- Susanna Dickinson, about 22; Almeron Dickinson, ex-US Army artilleryman, captain of the Alamo's artillery; Angelina born 14 Dec 1834 (Handbook). Joe born about 1813 (Handbook); Gray: "about 21 or 22".
Siege
- 23 Feb: Santa Anna's army reached Béxar; Travis answered the demand to surrender at discretion with a shot from the 18-pounder; a red flag (no quarter) flew from San Fernando church (Alamo site; Caro). Siege lasted 13 days (Handbook).
- Garrison about 150 at the start (Travis, 23 Feb; Alamo site); 32 Gonzales men got in on 1 March, the only relief (Alamo site; Handbook; Travis entry says 35); Bonham returned 3 March saying help was coming (Alamo site). Independence was declared 2 March; the garrison probably never knew (Alamo site).
- Artillery: 18 serviceable guns (Alamo site; Dickinson) to about 21 (Handbook; Santa Anna).
The Mexican plan
- Santa Anna announced the assault on 5 March; his officers were stunned and objected (Hardin). Caro (1837): the fort would likely have surrendered in a few more days.
- General order of 5 March (translation on the Sons of DeWitt Colony site): four columns under Cos, Duque, Romero and Morales; reserve (Sappers and five grenadier companies) under Santa Anna; troops to move at midnight, columns in place by 4 a.m.; bugle signal from the northern battery; 28 ladders (10, 10, 6, 2), crowbars and axes; no overcoats or blankets; untrained recruits left behind; cavalry to stop escapes. Directions: Cos west, Duque north, Romero east, Morales the entrance on the south (de la Peña, via The Alamo Reader).
- Assault force (disputed): 1,400 infantry (Santa Anna's report; Caro); about 1,800 (Handbook; PBS). Per-column figures often quoted (Cos ~300, Duque ~400, Romero ~300, Morales ~100, reserve ~400) are modern reconstructions, UNVERIFIED.
Weapons and why they mattered
- Mexican infantry: British-made India Pattern muskets (a cheaper "Brown Bess"), .75 smoothbore, 17-inch socket bayonet (Ed Dubravsky, Alamo de Parras site, from archaeology). Brown Bess bayonets, trigger plate, buttplate and a Baker rifle barrel were excavated at the La Villita earthworks (Handbook, "La Villita Earthworks").
- Musket: accurate under about 70 yards (John Bryant, Alamo de Parras); a volley weapon, fire often held to about 40 yards; "buck and ball" loads, which Joe's buckshot wound fits (Dubravsky). Some light troops carried Baker rifles.
- Long rifle: "A well-tuned long rifle could hit a target at 200 yards" (Doug Wicklund, NRA Family, 2017). Most defenders had shotguns (Travis a double-barrelled one), fowlers and muskets captured from Cos (Bryant; Wicklund).
- Why it mattered: in the dark, with columns massed under the walls, range counted for little; canister from the defenders' guns did the damage (Manifesto: artillery fire threw the columns into disorder; Caro: "deadly fire of shot and shell"); once over the north wall the attackers turned captured guns on the barricaded rooms (Caro). De la Peña (via The Alamo Reader): few ladders arrived; the north wall's timber reinforcement, 8-9 ft, could be climbed.
The assault
- Times (disputed): "about midnight" (Louisiana Advertiser, 1836); about 2 a.m. (Esparza); "just before day break" (Joe via Gray); began at 5, lasted over 1.5 hours, report at 8 (Santa Anna); attack 5:30 to 6 (Almonte); "around 5:00 A.M.", "no more than ninety minutes", all fighting men dead by 8 (Hardin); "roughly 90 minutes" (Alamo site); accounts range 2-6 a.m., early dawn most likely (Groneman, HistoryNet, 2017).
- Shouts in the ranks ruined the surprise (Manifesto); Adjutant Baugh woke Travis (Joe); three outlying pickets gave no alarm (Gray).
- North wall: Travis among the first to die, on the north bastion (Hardin; Joe; Ruiz found him at the north battery). Duque wounded early, his column wavered, Santa Anna sent the reserve; Amador, Ampudia, Mora and Aguirre gained the north side "where the strife was bitterest" and took its guns; Morales and Miñón took the south entrance (Caro). Two attempts thrown back, the third succeeded (Gray, from Dickinson).
- Defenders fell back to the Long Barrack and church; Bowie was killed in his bed in a south-side room; the church fell last (Hardin; Ruiz). Joe fired from the wall, then from a building where he hid (Gray). A Black woman was killed, Joe thought by a stray shot (Gray; Handbook).
Casualties (ranges with names)
- Defenders: official list 189, research may raise it to 257 (Hardin); "usually 183 to 189" (Britannica); 182-189, one researcher up to 260 (Groneman); 183 (Caro); 182 burned (Ruiz); de la Peña 182 by documents, 253 counted (via The Alamo Reader); "more than six hundred" (Santa Anna's report, disowned by Caro).
- Mexican: ~70 killed, 300 wounded (Santa Anna 1836 and 1837); ~400 dead incl. 100+ wounded who died untreated (Caro); about 600 killed and wounded, "best estimates" (Hardin); 1,600 (Ruiz; Dickinson); 1,000+ (US press 1836).
Crockett's death (CONTESTED)
- Died fighting: Gray's 1836 letter (Joe and Dickinson) says he and a few friends lay together with 24 enemy dead around them (Alexandria Gazette printing; other printings 21); Dickinson saw his body between the church and the barrack; Ruiz found it "toward the west and in the small fort opposite the city"; the Handbook says Dickinson placed his death outside, among the first.
- Captured and executed: US papers within weeks said he "cried for quarter" (Louisiana Advertiser); a 1836 newspaper letter dated 7 Sept credited to a Mexican officer (said to be Almonte) has Castrillón bringing six prisoners incl. Crockett to Santa Anna, who had them killed (Digital History, Univ. of Houston; chain UNVERIFIED); Caro (1837): Castrillón found five men hiding, Santa Anna had them killed, no names; de la Peña's narrative names Crockett, its authenticity long debated and he did not see it himself (Handbook, "Crockett, David", Lofaro/Davis).
- Handbook: "It is almost impossible to determine how Crockett died, though he certainly could have been among those who tried to surrender." Groneman: "The fact is we don't know how Crockett died."
After
- Ruiz was called to identify Travis, Bowie and Crockett; on Santa Anna's order the defenders' bodies were burned on pyres (Ruiz; Alamo site). Mexican dead went to the city cemetery, where there was not room for all (Ruiz).
- Survivors: about 15-20 noncombatants plus Joe (Alamo site; Handbook). Named: Susanna and Angelina Dickinson; Ana Esparza and four children; Juana Navarro Alsbury, baby Alejo and sister Gertrudis; Concepción Losoya, son Juan, daughter Juana Melton; Victoriana de Salinas and three daughters; Petra Gonzales; Joe; Sam, enslaved by Bowie, reportedly survived; Trinidad Saucedo left during the siege (Handbook, "Alamo Noncombatants").
- The women were taken to Ramón Músquiz's house, then before Santa Anna: two dollars and a blanket each (Esparza; Handbook). Mrs. Melton begged Ana Esparza not to mention her American husband (Esparza). Santa Anna wanted to adopt Angelina; her mother refused (Handbook).
- Santa Anna sent Dickinson, with Almonte's servant Ben and a warning letter dated 7 March, to Houston; Joe joined them; they reached Gonzales about 12 March (Handbook). Two ranchers had brought the news there on 11 March (Alamo site, "Early Reports"); Houston retreated and the Runaway Scrape began (Handbook).
- Joe was questioned at Groce's Retreat (20 March), returned to Travis's estate, and escaped on 21 April 1837 with an unnamed Mexican man and two horses; a $50 reward ran three months; last reported in Austin in 1875 (Handbook, "Joe").
- Goliad: Fannin and over 300 men executed, 27 March 1836 (Alamo site). San Jacinto, 21 April 1836: about 900 Texans surprised 1,200-1,300; 18 minutes; Houston's report 630 Mexicans killed, 730 prisoners, 9 Texans killed (Britannica; Handbook); "Remember the Alamo! Remember Goliad!"; Santa Anna captured next day (Alamo site); Seguín's company the only Tejano unit (Handbook).
- The Mexican army tore down the outer walls in May 1836; only church and Long Barrack remain (Alamo site). Seguín later directed burial of the gathered remains (Handbook; 1886 magazine: 25 Feb 1837).
- Enrique farmed in Atascosa County, then truck-farmed in San Antonio; hauled freight by cart to Indianola; married Gertrudes Hernández, seven children; died 20 Dec 1917, buried at Losoya (Handbook).
- Adina De Zavala (granddaughter of Lorenzo de Zavala: Handbook; the 1901 article wrongly says "daughter") brought him forward in 1901; Express interviews 1902, 1904, 1907 (Handbook). The 1902 reporter: he preferred Spanish and "his voice several times choked and he could not proceed for emotion" (Alamo de Parras transcription only).
- Susanna Dickinson remarried several times, settled in Austin as Mrs. Hannig, died 7 Oct 1883 (Handbook).

STORY SHAPE (11 chapters, ~38 min, chronological; Esparza alternating with the wider picture)
1. Cold open (0:00-3:00): the dark room, "Gregorio, the soldiers have jumped the wall" (8), "I never saw him again" (9); the old man in 1907: "I alone live" (1).
2. Béxar, Feb 1836 (3:00-7:00): a Tejano town in Mexico's civil war (1824 constitution; slavery in the background); Gregorio in Seguín's company; John W. Smith's warning; drums and salute at sundown (2, 3); Mrs. Melton's umbrella (4).
3. The fort (7:00-11:00): ~150 men (Tejanos, Americans, Europeans, enslaved men), 18-21 guns; Travis and Bowie; Crockett; Bowie's collapse; the cannon-shot answer, red flag, Travis's letter.
4. Thirteen days through a child's eyes (11:00-15:00): a shot every fifteen minutes (5); water cut; the soldier reading bugle calls; drums and flute; Crockett at the fire (6), his fiddle; the 32 from Gonzales; his mother's refusal (7).
5. The other army (15:00-19:00): Santa Anna's reasons and admiration for the defenders, the stunned officers, Caro's "Why didn't we wait?"; the order of 5 March: 28 ladders, no overcoats, recruits left behind, cavalry to cut off escape.
6. Weapons explained (19:00-22:00): India Pattern/Brown Bess (70 yards, volleys, bayonet, buck and ball) vs long rifle (200 yards) vs the shotguns and captured muskets most defenders had; canister; why darkness and walls cancelled the rifle's edge.
7. Midnight to the first shout (22:00-25:00): columns lying in the cold; an exhausted garrison asleep; the "imprudent huzzas"; Baugh's cry; Travis on the wall (17), shot early; the time dispute.
8. The north wall (25:00-29:00): canister, Duque down, columns crowding north, the reserve, two attempts thrown back, the third "like sheep"; Morales at the south gate; Joe in hiding; "stubborn resistance... more than one hour and a half".
9. The dark rooms (29:00-32:30): fall back to Long Barrack and church; captured guns turned inward; the Esparzas huddled (10, 11); Almeron's last words (15); Bowie in his bed (one plain line); the church last.
10. Daybreak (32:30-35:30): shadows at the window (12); the bayonet and the officer (13); "Are you Mrs. Dickinson?" (16); "Yes here's one" (18); the Crockett versions with names; "Victory belongs to the Army" (19) and Caro's confession (20); the casualty ranges.
11. Aftermath and a long life (35:30-38:00): Ruiz and the identification; the pyres (one sentence); Gregorio's Christian burial by his brother; silver and blankets; Dickinson, Ben and Joe to Gonzales; Runaway Scrape, Goliad, San Jacinto in 18 minutes; Joe's escape in 1837; Enrique the farmer and carter, found in 1901, dead in 1917; close on quote 1 or quote 11.

RULES
- Quote only from VERIFIED QUOTES and BACKUP LINES, naming year and paper or book. No invented dialogue; lines the witnesses report from others are "as they remembered".
- Esparza spoke 66-71 years later, through reporters, in Spanish rendered into English: attribute ("he told the Express in 1902"). His "about two o'clock" conflicts with almost every other account (about 5-5:30 a.m.): say so or say "in the dark before dawn". Age: "about eight" / "seven or eight".
- "Don Benito": in 1902 he says the Mexicans called Crockett "Don Benito" and that this man held daily talks with Santa Anna during a three-day armistice; nothing else confirms Crockett negotiating: never narrate it as fact. The armistice and the uncle as "assailant" are UNVERIFIED elsewhere.
- The two 1902 transcriptions differ slightly ("two o'clock"/"2 o'clock"; "anyone/anyhow"/"any one/any how"; Crockett speaking "the Mexican language"/"the Spanish language"; "Corpus Christi... Goliad"/"Goliad"): use the Gale wording; avoid the language line.
- Only quote 1 comes from 1907, credited "as quoted in the Handbook of Texas".
- MYTH, the line in the sand: first printed in 1873 by William P. Zuber in the Texas Almanac, from what he said Louis "Moses" Rose told his family (Handbook, "Zuber"; "Rose, Louis (Moses)"); Zuber later admitted adding an invented paragraph, and no 1836 letter or report mentions it (Groneman). Esparza's 1907 interview (via the Handbook's Rose entry) and the 1881 Express visit with Dickinson repeat it decades after Zuber, and Barnes, the 1907 interviewer, told it in his own 1910 book: present as legend, never as Esparza's 1902 testimony (it is absent there).
- Other myths to correct: "no survivors"/"Thermopylae had her messenger of defeat; the Alamo had none" (15-20 noncombatants and Joe lived); the Alamo "bought time" for Houston's army (he was at the convention until 6 March); Houston ordered it abandoned; the "coldest winter"; the men were free to leave (they had sworn an oath); Travis was disliked (all: Alamo site, "Myths and Legends"). Early false reports: Travis killed himself, Almeron Dickinson leapt from the church with his son (Houston's 11 March letter, Alamo site); Crockett "cried for quarter". Later legends: Crockett swinging his empty rifle to the last, Bowie fighting from his cot (Barnes 1910): legend, not testimony.
- Crockett: always the versions with who says each; never settle it. De la Peña: summarize only (debated, copyrighted translation).
- Numbers only as ranges with names: defenders ~182-189 (list 189, maybe up to ~257-260); assault 1,400-1,800; Mexican losses 70 dead/300 wounded (Santa Anna) to ~400 dead (Caro) to ~600 killed and wounded (Hardin); never "1,600 killed" as fact; Santa Anna's "600" rebel dead is false by his secretary's own admission. Times: "before dawn", "about 5 a.m.", "about ninety minutes".
- Respect every side: Santa Anna's soldiers are soldiers, not "hordes"; give their officers' own words (praise of the defenders' courage, Caro's grief and his condemnation of the prisoner killings). Tejanos fought on both sides: avoid "Mexicans vs Americans"; say "Santa Anna's army" and "the defenders (Texans, Tejanos, Europeans, enslaved men)". Never repeat the sources' slurs or labels ("hordes", "savage", "dastardly", "for one of his class", "negroes"); Joe is "Joe, a man enslaved by Travis".
- Joe's account reached us only through Gray's recollection: "according to William Fairfax Gray, who heard Joe questioned". No invented feelings or words.
- No gore: one plain sentence each for the pyres, Bowie's death, the boy in the Esparzas' room, the Black woman, the killing of prisoners, and the men Dickinson saw killed in the church. Never describe wounds or what was done to bodies (several sources do: leave it out).
- Names as spelled here (Gregorio Esparza, Ana Salazar de Esparza, Juana Melton, Concepción Losoya, Juana Navarro Alsbury, Susanna/Almeron/Angelina Dickinson, Joe, Ben, Francisco Antonio Ruiz, Ramón Martínez Caro). Unnamed people stay unnamed (the officer who saved the Esparzas, the boy, the Black woman). Sources misspell Bonham ("Benham") and Dickinson ("Dickenson").
- Images: the 1836 compound: church roofless with an earth ramp inside for guns (Esparza 1902), Long Barrack, low barrack with the south gate, low stockade in front of the church (Handbook, Crockett), north wall shored with timber (de la Peña); stone and adobe houses; Mexican infantry in caps with chin straps (general order), muskets and bayonets; defenders in civilian clothes with mixed guns, not a buckskin army; no gore.
- NOT VERIFIED: the 1907 Express text itself; Dickinson's 1876 statement; per-column numbers; the informant chain of the 1836 "Almonte" Crockett letter; whether Francisco Esparza fought in the assault; how many ladders reached the walls.
