NOTES: "How 28 Buffalo Hunters Held Off 700 Warriors at Adobe Walls"
Second Battle of Adobe Walls, 27 June 1874, Texas Panhandle (Hutchinson County).
Primary source: "Life and Adventures of 'Billy' Dixon of Adobe Walls, Texas Panhandle" (1914),
dictated by Billy Dixon in 1912-13 and set down by his wife Olive K. Dixon, nearly 40 years after the fight.
Spelling of quotes kept exactly as printed (including "--" dashes and the book's inner quote marks).
Outer wrappers on quotes are curly “ ”; everything inside them is verbatim.

VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim from Dixon's memoir; never rephrase)
- “That the Indian should have resisted with relentless and increasing ferocity every effort to drive him from this paradise was natural and justifiable from his point of view.” — context: Chapter I, the book's introduction on the Plains tribes and their homeland (narrator's voice, probably the compiler's; credit it to "Dixon's memoir", not to Dixon speaking).
- “The hunting of buffaloes for their hides began in the spring of 1870. That was also the beginning of the destruction of the buffalo.” — context: Kansas, 1870, start of the commercial hide trade (Chapter IV).
- “If the killing of the buffaloes should continue unabated, the Indians would soon be facing starvation; at least, their old freedom would be at an end” — context: 1872, south of the Arkansas, Dixon explaining why the tribes grew hostile to hide hunters.
- “Myers & Leonard built a picket house twenty by sixty feet in size. James Hanrahan put up a sod house, twenty-five by sixty, in which he opened a saloon.” — context: spring 1874, building the new post on East Adobe Walls Creek.
- “This was deadly business, without sentiment; it was dollars against tenderheartedness, and dollars won.” — context: June 1874, Dixon's camp on Dixon Creek once the herds arrived.
- “All of us felt that these murders had been perpetrated as a warning to the buffalo-hunters to leave the country--to go north of the "dead line."” — context: June 1874, after four hunters were killed near the Walls.
- “In all that vast wilderness, ours were the only lights save the stars that glittered above us.” — context: the night of 26 June 1874, hunters celebrating with doors open.
- “Twenty-eight men and one woman would have been slaughtered if the ridge pole in Hanrahan's saloon had not cracked like a rifle shot.” — context: about 2 a.m., 27 June 1874; the cracking ridgepole woke the camp.
- “The black body of moving object suddenly spread out like a fan, and from it went up one single, solid yell--a warwhoop that seemed to shake the very air of the early morning.” — context: dawn, 27 June, Dixon by his wagon watching the horses by the creek.
- “Hundreds of warriors, the flower of the fighting men of the southwestern Plains tribes, mounted upon their finest horses, armed with guns and lances, and carrying heavy shields of thick buffalo hide, were coming like the wind.” — context: dawn charge, 27 June.
- “Some of the men were still undressed, but nobody wasted any time hunting their clothes, and many of them fought for their lives all that summer day barefoot and in their night clothes.” — context: first minutes of the attack, men split between three buildings.
- “For the first half hour the Indians were reckless and daring enough to ride up and strike the doors with the butts of their guns.” — context: first half hour of the fight, early morning 27 June.
- “they had killed only two of our men, the Shadler brothers who were caught asleep in their wagon. Both were scalped.” — context: opening of the attack, the freighters' wagon outside the buildings.
- “An Indian in those days would quickly endanger his own life to carry a dead or helpless comrade beyond reach of the enemy.” — context: morning of 27 June, warriors recovering their fallen under fire.
- “Their "medicine" man had told them that all they would have to do would be to come to Adobe Walls and knock us on the head with sticks” — context: Dixon on the medicine man's promise (sentence continues: their bullets would not break an Indian's skin).
- “Just as Tyler was entering the door of the adobe store, he turned to fire, and was struck by a bullet that penetrated his lungs.” — context: early in the fight, Billy Tyler retreating from the stockade into Myers & Leonard's store.
- “Even those that were at a considerable distance from the buildings when they received their wounds came to us, as if seeking our help and sympathy.” — context: 27 June, wounded horses gathering by the buildings.
- “I have no idea how many guns were cracking away at us, but I do know that bullets rattled round us like hail.” — context: about noon, Dixon and Hanrahan running from the saloon to Rath's store for ammunition.
- “But all that first day, when the hand of death seemed to be reaching from every direction, this pioneer woman was cool and composed and lent a helping hand in every emergency.” — context: Mrs. Olds, the only woman, inside Rath & Wright's store on 27 June.
- “we blanketed the bodies of Tyler and the Shadlers and dug a single grave near the north side of the corral. There they lie to this day, without a stone to mark the spot.” — context: evening of 27 June, first burial.
- “The distance was not far from three-fourths of a mile. A number of exaggerated accounts have been written about this incident. I took careful aim and pulled the trigger. We saw an Indian fall from his horse.” — context: the third day, about fifteen warriors on the bluff east of Adobe Walls Creek; the long shot.
- “They had risked their lives, as we had frequently observed, to rescue a comrade who might be not only wounded but dead. I was admittedly a good marksman, yet this was what might be called a "scratch" shot.” — context: right after the long shot, two warriors ran out on foot to carry off the fallen man.
- “Gladly would I have faced all the Indians from the Cimarron to Red River, rather than have witnessed this terrible scene.” — context: fifth day, William Olds killed by his own gun going off on the lookout ladder, in front of his wife.
- “The fight at Adobe Walls broke up buffalo-hunting in that section just as the Indians had planned. This was the last buffalo-hunting I ever did as a business.” — context: the days after the siege (Chapter X).
- “When I told him that I was one of the men that had fought against him, he leaned over on his horse and shook my hand. We became good friends.” — context: 1877, Dixon meets Quanah while scouting with troops near the Palo Duro.

VERIFIED FACTS (from the memoir; one line each)
- Billy Dixon was born 25 September 1850 in Ohio County, West Virginia; at Adobe Walls he was "in my twenty-fourth year" (23); "Bat" Masterson was the youngest of the party and Jim Hanrahan the oldest.
- Hide hunting began in spring 1870, started by agents of eastern hide buyers at Hays City; first prices $1 per cow hide and $2 per bull hide, rising to $4 per bull hide by fall 1872 as buffalo grew scarce.
- A capable skinner could skin 50 buffalo a day and was paid about $50 a month; Dixon paid up to 25 cents a hide; he did his own shooting and usually kept two skinners.
- As a 17-year-old teamster Dixon was at the Medicine Lodge treaty council (October 1867); its main point, he says, was that the Indians keep south of the Arkansas, which hunters then called the "dead line".
- By spring 1874 the hunters at Dodge City agreed the herds would never again come that far north "because of the enormous slaughter" of 1872-73, and decided to go south to the Canadian River.
- Dodge merchant A. C. Myers agreed to move his store south; the expedition left Dodge with about 50 men and 30 wagons; the new post was about 150 miles from Dodge City.
- Site: East Adobe Walls Creek, in a broad valley about a mile and a quarter north of the ruins of the original Adobe Walls (a trading post probably built by William Bent's company in the 1840s).
- Buildings: Myers & Leonard picket house 20x60 ft with a stockade corral of cottonwood logs; Hanrahan's sod saloon 25x60 ft; O'Keefe's picket blacksmith shop 15 ft square; Wright & Langton's (Rath & Wright's) sod store 16x20 ft, run by James Langton; a mess house in the stockade and a well.
- Layout, north to south, all facing east: Myers & Leonard's store, then the blacksmith shop, Hanrahan's saloon about 50 yards south, then Rath & Wright's store with a great pile of buffalo hides behind it (Mrs. Olds' restaurant at its rear); east across Adobe Walls Creek a butte-like hill stood "about twelve hundred yards" from Myers & Leonard's.
- Dixon says the main buildings' walls were "about two feet thick"; roofs were sod on a big cottonwood ridge log; no port-holes had been cut, so the men fired from windows and transoms.
- In June 1874 four hunters were killed near the Walls: Dudley and Wallace on Chicken Creek, "Cheyenne Jack" (John Jones) and "Blue Billy" on the Salt Fork of Red River.
- Dixon lost his big "50" and his mule Joe when his wagon overturned crossing the flooded Canadian; on the evening of 26 June he got a new "44" Sharp's (priced $80) from Langton but left its case of ammunition at Rath's store.
- Dixon had just partnered with Hanrahan (who usually ran seven skinners) for half the profits; their wagons were loaded to leave on the morning of 27 June 1874.
- Night of 26 June: 28 men and one woman (the wife of William Olds) at the Walls; only 8 or 9 men lived there, the rest were hunters passing through; most slept outside with doors open.
- About 2 a.m. Shepherd and Mike Welch, asleep in Hanrahan's saloon, heard a crack like a rifle from the ridgepole; about fifteen men threw dirt off the roof and cut a prop at the creek; later no break was ever found in the log.
- At first light Hanrahan sent Billy Ogg for the horses, grazing a quarter mile southeast along the creek; Dixon saw the riders at the timber edge, tied his horse, fired one shot and ran; Ogg reached Hanrahan's door just behind him.
- Hanrahan's saloon (9): Hanrahan, Bat Masterson, Mike Welch, Shepherd, Hiram Watson, Billy Ogg, James McKinley, "Bermuda" Carlisle, Dixon.
- Myers & Leonard's store (11): Fred Leonard, James Campbell, Edward Trevor, Frank Brown, Harry Armitage, "Dutch Henry", Billy Tyler, Old Man Keeler, Mike McCabe, Henry Lease, "Frenchy".
- Rath & Wright's store (7): James Langton (printed "Longton" in this list), George Eddy, Thomas O'Keefe, William Olds and his wife, Sam Smith, Andy Johnson; with the two Shadler brothers in their wagon, 28 men.
- Attackers: Dixon says estimates ran from 700 to 1,000 and "700 would be a safe guess"; mostly Kiowas, Cheyennes and Comanches; Masterson's letter in the book says "fully one thousand" and adds the Arapahoe.
- Barricades were sacks of flour and grain; the warriors charged to bugle calls that ex-soldiers in Hanrahan's understood; Masterson says Harry Armitage killed the bugler late on day one with a 50-calibre Sharp's.
- Dead on 27 June: the Shadler brothers (freighters), killed asleep in their wagon at the start, and their Newfoundland dog; Billy Tyler, shot through the lungs at the door of Myers & Leonard's, lived about half an hour.
- About 10 a.m. Dixon shot through a corner of the hide stack behind Rath's at a warrior sheltering there; by then he had given his "44" to Hanrahan and taken Hanrahan's big "50" from the excited bartender Shepherd.
- About noon the charges stopped; low on ammunition, Dixon and Hanrahan ran to Rath's store ("thousands of rounds"); Hanrahan ran back with a sack of cartridges; Dixon stayed at Rath's, the building with the fewest men and with Mrs. Olds.
- At Rath's the recoil of the "50" knocked Dixon off the flour-sack barricade with a crash of tubs and pans; the men thought he was shot; his third shot hit the man crawling in the grass.
- By 2 p.m. the warriors had pulled back to the foot of the hills, split east and west of the buildings; about 4 p.m. "Bermuda" Carlisle went outside, others followed, and only then did they learn Tyler was dead.
- Horses: all killed or run off; 56 dead horses counted near the buildings (10 belonged to the hunters) plus the Shadlers' 28 oxen; Dixon's own saddle horse was among the first shot.
- Thirteen dead warriors were left on the ground near the buildings; the defenders buried Tyler and the Shadlers in one grave north of the corral at dusk on 27 June; no attack came that night.
- Day 2 (28 June): bodies and horses dragged away on buffalo hides; 12 horses buried in a pit; George Bellfield's wagon and Jim and Bob Cator came in; Henry Lease rode for Dodge City after dark on Bellfield's horse.
- Day 3: about fifteen warriors on the bluff east of the creek; Dixon's long shot, which he puts at "not far from three-fourths of a mile" (about 1,300 yards); the book's chapter heading and photo caption say 1,200 yards.
- Day 5: lookouts of sod built on Rath's and Myers' roofs; William Olds was killed when his own gun went off as he came down the lookout ladder; buried about 60 feet southeast of Rath's store.
- By the sixth day "fully a hundred men" were at the Walls, which Dixon says led to later claims that all of them had been in the fight.
- About a week after the fight some 25 men under Hanrahan rode to Dodge, finding Henry Lease's partner Charley Sharp killed in camp on the way; a 40-man relief party under Tom Nixon went down and brought Mrs. Olds and most of the men back.
- Dixon became a scout for General Nelson A. Miles (6 August 1874 to 10 February 1883); returning with Lt. Frank D. Baldwin, he saw George Huffman lanced near the Walls, the fifth grave; in October 1874 the abandoned post had been burned and "the walls were still smoking."
- Later life per the book: Medal of Honor for the Buffalo Wallow fight (12 September 1874); homesteaded the original Adobe Walls site in 1883 and was postmaster there nearly 20 years; died of pneumonia on 9 March 1913, buried at Texline. [Not in the memoir, well documented: the Army struck his medal from the roll in 1916-17 because he was a civilian scout and restored it in 1989; his remains were moved to the Adobe Walls battle site in 1929.]

THE OTHER SIDE (well-documented facts about the Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne side; mark disputed numbers as ranges with who claims them)
- Why they came: once eastern tanneries could make leather from buffalo hides (early 1870s) and the railroad reached Dodge City (1872), hide hunters killed buffalo by the millions; William Hornaday (1889) estimated more than three million killed by hide hunters on the southern plains in 1872-74 alone. For the Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne the herds were food, shelter, clothing and trade.
- Medicine Lodge (October 1867): Comanche, Kiowa and Plains Apache, then Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho, accepted reservations in Indian Territory, keeping the right to hunt south of the Arkansas "so long as the buffalo may range thereon in such numbers as to justify the chase." The tribes saw the hunters' move into the Panhandle as a breach that the army did not stop. Quanah's Quahadi band never signed.
- Other grievances in 1873-74 (well documented): short and late rations at the agencies, whiskey traders, and white horse thieves raiding Native herds.
- Isatai (Isa-tai), a young Quahadi Comanche medicine man, won a following in spring 1874 by predicting a comet's disappearance and a drought; in May 1874 he called a Sun Dance, the first the Comanche held as a whole people (a report quoted in Dixon's book: near the mouth of the Sweetwater, a Red River tributary), where war on the hunters was planned with Cheyenne and some Kiowa joining.
- Isatai's promise: the hunters would be killed asleep and his paint would make warriors bulletproof (Dixon: "our bullets would not be strong enough to break an Indian's skin"). Later Native accounts say he watched from a rise, his horse was shot under him, and he blamed the failure on a Cheyenne who had killed a skunk; his standing collapsed after the battle.
- Quanah: Quahadi Comanche war leader, son of Peta Nocona and Cynthia Ann Parker (taken captive in Texas in 1836), then in his twenties (birth year uncertain, about 1845-1852); his horse was shot under him and he was wounded in the shoulder (his own account, reported in Dixon's memoir).
- Who fought: mostly Comanche and Southern Cheyenne, with smaller numbers of Kiowa and Arapaho. Dixon names Big Bow and Lone Wolf as leaders, and also Little Robe and White Shield, but his own book shows those two Cheyennes talking to the agent at the Cheyenne Agency that September; treat his list of leaders as unconfirmed.
- Number of attackers (disputed): Dixon 700 ("safe guess", others said up to 1,000); Masterson "fully one thousand"; modern historians, drawing partly on Native testimony, usually give a smaller force, commonly 250 to 700. Say "hundreds of warriors" or give the range with who claims each end.
- Native losses (disputed): Dixon counted 13 bodies left near the buildings; others were carried away during the fight; totals usually given run from about 15 to 30 killed, plus many wounded; Native accounts gathered later give lower figures than the hunters'.
- Same ground, ten years earlier: on 25 November 1864 (First Battle of Adobe Walls) Kiowa, Comanche and Plains Apache warriors forced Kit Carson's U.S. column to withdraw from the ruins of Bent's old post.
- What followed: war parties raided across Texas, Kansas, Colorado and New Mexico through summer 1874, and the U.S. Army answered with the Red River War (1874-75): five columns converging on the Panhandle, including Miles from Fort Dodge (with Dixon as scout) and Mackenzie from Fort Concho.
- Palo Duro Canyon, 28 September 1874: Mackenzie's 4th Cavalry surprised the winter camps, burned lodges and food stores and shot more than 1,000 captured horses; few people died, but the loss of horses and supplies forced surrender over the winter.
- The end: bands came in through the winter and spring of 1875 (Southern Cheyenne under Stone Calf in March); Quanah led the Quahadi into Fort Sill on 2 June 1875, among the last to surrender; dozens of men named as war leaders were imprisoned at Fort Marion, Florida. By the end of the 1870s the southern buffalo herd was gone.
- Afterwards Quanah became the leading Comanche spokesman on the reservation; Dixon records that the two old enemies shook hands in 1877 and became friends.

STORY SHAPE (suggested chronological chapter plan for 38 minutes, 8-10 chapters, one line each)
1. Cold open (0:00-2:30): about 2 a.m., 27 June 1874, a crack like a rifle shot in Hanrahan's saloon wakes the men; outside, hundreds of riders gather in the dark timber.
2. The hide boom (2:30-7:00): young Dixon on the Kansas range, $1 and $2 a hide, 50 skinned a day, "dollars won"; the herds thin north of the Arkansas; the Medicine Lodge promise and the "dead line".
3. Into the forbidden country (7:00-11:00): Myers' plan, 50 men and 30 wagons leave Dodge in spring 1874; a sod-and-picket town rises on Adobe Walls Creek beside the ruins of Bent's post.
4. The other camp (11:00-15:30): what the buffalo meant to the Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne; ration and horse-thief grievances; Isatai's prophecies, the May 1874 Sun Dance, Quanah, the promise of bulletproof paint.
5. Warnings (15:30-19:00): four hunters killed, the herds finally arrive, Dixon's rifle lost in the Canadian, the new "44", the last night with doors open under the stars.
6. Dawn (19:00-23:30): the ridgepole, Billy Ogg sent for the horses, the line that "spread out like a fan", the race for the doors, 28 men split into three buildings, the Shadler brothers killed in their wagon.
7. The longest morning (23:30-28:00): warriors striking doors with gun butts, the bugle calls, Billy Tyler shot, the warrior behind the hide stack, warriors carrying off their fallen, the wounded horses coming to the buildings.
8. Noon to nightfall (28:00-31:30): the ammunition run to Rath's, Mrs. Olds, the 2 p.m. pullback, men stepping outside at 4 p.m., 13 bodies on the field, one grave by the corral.
9. The siege days (31:30-35:30): day 2 Bellfield's wagon and Henry Lease riding north alone; day 3 the long shot "not far from three-fourths of a mile"; day 5 Olds' death; a hundred men by day 6.
10. Aftermath (35:30-38:00): the post burned and abandoned, the Red River War, Palo Duro Canyon, Quanah's surrender in June 1875, the buffalo gone; Dixon and Quanah shake hands; the ridge log no one could find a crack in.

RULES
- Quote Dixon verbatim only from VERIFIED QUOTES; never invent dialogue. Lines from Bat Masterson or other people printed in the book must be credited to them, not to Dixon.
- Attribute Dixon's numbers to him ("Dixon reckoned...", "Dixon counted..."): he dictated the book about 38 years after the fight, and some details differ from other accounts.
- Disputed numbers always given as ranges with who claims them: attackers (Dixon 700, Masterson 1,000, many modern historians 250-700); Native dead (Dixon: 13 bodies left on the field; usual estimates 15-30).
- The long shot: say what Dixon says ("not far from three-fourths of a mile", on the third day, a "scratch" shot with a big .50). The often-repeated 1,538 yards (said to be a later army survey), the "second day" and the exact cartridge (".50-90") are not in his memoir; if used, present them as later claims.
- Dixon calls the buildings "adobe" but his own list says picket (Myers & Leonard) and sod (Hanrahan's, Rath's): say "sod and picket walls" or "thick sod walls".
- Spellings vary in the book: Langton/Longton, Myers & Leonard/Leonard & Myers, Rath & Wright/Wright & Langton; later sources also spell the Shadlers "Scheidler". Use Langton, Myers & Leonard, Rath & Wright, Shadler.
- Respectful of the Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne fighters, who are not villains; their reasons (the slaughter of the buffalo that fed them, broken promises) are stated plainly and early. Call them warriors, fighters or by tribe; do not repeat Dixon's period words ("savage", "barbaric", "bucks", "squaws") in narration, and never repeat the racial slur he uses for the army cook in Chapter X.
- Graphic details (scalping, the stake on Chicken Creek, Olds' death, the dead horses) stated once and briefly, never relished.
- No invented scenes inside Native camps beyond the documented facts in THE OTHER SIDE (Isatai's prophecies, the Sun Dance, Quanah's wound, the surrender).
