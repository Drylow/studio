# How 28 Buffalo Hunters Held Off 700 Warriors at Adobe Walls

It's about two o'clock in the morning on the 27th of June, 1874, on a creek in the Texas Panhandle, when the roof of a sod saloon cracks like a rifle shot over two sleeping men. And you're about to find out how 28 buffalo hunters held off hundreds of warriors at a place called Adobe Walls, which is nothing like the legend. If you're one of those hunters, you're asleep on the ground outside, 150 miles from Dodge City, and every door in the place is standing open. You've probably heard the story. Crack shots behind adobe walls, 700 warriors, and one rifle shot so long that it ended the battle. That's not how it went. The walls weren't even adobe. That number was one man's guess. The famous shot came two days after the main fight was over. And the men who lived through that morning were alive because a cottonwood log in a roof woke them up. Dawn is a few hours away. Somewhere out past the horses, along the creek timber, hundreds of riders are waiting for it.

## Two in the morning

The two men are Shepherd, the bartender, and Mike Welch, and they jump up in the dark of Hanrahan's saloon sure that somebody has fired a gun. Nobody has. The sound came from the ridgepole, the big cottonwood log that runs along the top of the building and carries the roof. And the roof is sod, packed earth laid across that one log. If the log gives way, the whole thing comes down on whoever is sleeping under it.

So they start waking people up, and soon about fifteen men are working in the dark. If you're asleep outside, it's the noise of men climbing onto a roof and dirt coming down off it that wakes you. If you're one of them, you're up on that roof throwing the dirt off to take the weight off the log, while others go down to the creek to cut a prop to hold it up.

Almost 40 years later, one of the hunters at the Walls that night, Billy Dixon, dictated his story to his wife, Olive, and she wrote it down. It came out in 1914 as a book called Life and Adventures of Billy Dixon of Adobe Walls. On this point he doesn't hedge at all. In his own words, "Twenty-eight men and one woman would have been slaughtered if the ridge pole in Hanrahan's saloon had not cracked like a rifle shot."

Which raises a question the legend skips. What are these men doing here at all? They're camped on the wrong side of a river the hunters themselves called the dead line, in country where a treaty had promised the hunting to the Comanche, the Kiowa and the Cheyenne. And they know it.

It starts four years earlier, with a price.

## A dollar a hide

Hays City, Kansas, in the spring of 1870. Agents of eastern hide buyers arrive in town and start paying cash for buffalo hides, one dollar for a cow and two dollars for a bull. The reason is back east. Tanneries have worked out how to turn buffalo hide into leather, and every animal on the plains now has a price on its back.

Billy Dixon is still a teenager that spring. He was born in Ohio County, West Virginia, on the 25th of September 1850, and he's already been working on the plains for a few years. Now he's a hunter. He does his own shooting and usually keeps two skinners. A capable one can take the hides off 50 buffalo in a day, and the going wage is about $50 a month. Dixon says he paid as much as 25 cents a hide.

Then the price starts to climb, and it climbs for the worst possible reason. By the fall of 1872, a bull hide brings $4, because the buffalo are getting scarce. Think about what that does. The scarcer the buffalo get, the more each hide is worth, and the more a hunter will risk to go out and find one. That same year, the railroad reaches Dodge City, and the hides start going east by train.

How many animals are we talking about? In 1889, a naturalist named William Hornaday tried to add it up. His estimate was more than 3 million buffalo killed by hide hunters on the southern plains in 1872, 1873 and 1874 alone. That's an average of more than 2,700 animals a day, every day, for three years.

And there was supposed to be a limit. Dixon had seen it agreed. In October 1867, as a 17-year-old teamster, he was at the treaty council at Medicine Lodge. The Comanche, the Kiowa and the Plains Apache, and then the Southern Cheyenne and the Arapaho, accepted reservations in Indian Territory, and they kept the right to hunt south of the Arkansas River "so long as the buffalo may range thereon in such numbers as to justify the chase." Those are the treaty's own words.

The way Dixon understood it, the main point was simple. The tribes would keep south of the Arkansas, and the hunters would keep north of it. The hunters even had a name for that river. They called it the dead line, and no hunter was supposed to cross it.

By 1872, hunters are already working south of it, and Dixon knew exactly what that meant for the people on the other side. He later wrote: "If the killing of the buffaloes should continue unabated, the Indians would soon be facing starvation; at least, their old freedom would be at an end".

And he kept on hunting. Two summers later, he'd be camped in the middle of the country that treaty was written to protect.

## Across the dead line

By the spring of 1874, the hunters around Dodge City have reached a conclusion they don't like. The herds aren't coming that far north again. Two years of slaughter have seen to that. So they decide to go where the buffalo still are, south to the Canadian River in the Texas Panhandle, a long way past the dead line.

Hunters that far out need somewhere to sell hides and buy cartridges and flour. A Dodge merchant named A. C. Myers agrees to move his store south with them. The expedition leaves Dodge with about 50 men and 30 wagons, bound for a spot about 150 miles away.

They stop in a broad valley on East Adobe Walls Creek, about a mile and a quarter north of a ruin. The ruin is the original Adobe Walls, a trading post probably built by William Bent's company in the 1840s, and it's where the place gets its name.

This valley has seen a fight before. On the 25th of November 1864, Kiowa, Comanche and Plains Apache warriors fought a U.S. Army column under Kit Carson around those same ruins and forced it to withdraw. That was ten years before the hunters rode in.

Now they build, in a row running north to south. At the north end, Myers and Leonard put up a store made of pickets, cottonwood logs set upright side by side, 20 feet by 60, with a stockade corral of cottonwood logs, a mess house inside the stockade, and a well. Next comes Thomas O'Keefe's blacksmith shop, also picket, 15 feet square. About 50 yards south of the store, Jim Hanrahan builds a saloon out of sod, 25 feet by 60. And at the south end is Rath and Wright's store, sod again, 16 feet by 20, run by James Langton. A great pile of buffalo hides is stacked behind it, and at the rear of the store is a restaurant run by Mrs. Olds, the wife of William Olds.

Every one of these buildings faces east, toward the creek. And across the creek, about 1,200 yards east of Myers and Leonard's store by Dixon's estimate, a hill rises out of the valley like a butte. If you're standing in a doorway at the Walls, it's the high ground straight across the creek. Remember that hill.

Now, about that name. The adobe walls were the ruin up the creek. Myers and Leonard's is picket, and Hanrahan's and Rath's are sod, blocks of cut prairie turf, even though Dixon's own book calls them adobe. The walls these men will fight behind are turf and cottonwood.

They're thick, though. Dixon says the walls of the main buildings were about two feet thick, with sod roofs laid over big cottonwood ridge logs. And there isn't a single port-hole cut in any of them. If anyone ever has to defend this place, they'll be shooting out of the windows and through the open transoms over the doors.

If you want the rest of the frontier told like this, from every side and without the Hollywood, subscribe. Because the hunters aren't the only ones making plans that spring.

## The other camp

If you're Comanche, Kiowa or Cheyenne in 1873, the buffalo is the food in your camp. It's the hide cover of your lodge, the clothes you wear, and the goods you trade. So when hide hunters cross the dead line, taking the skins and leaving, and then push on into the Panhandle, the tribes see the treaty broken in front of them, and the army doing nothing to stop it. One Comanche band, the Quahadi, never signed that treaty at all.

And look again at the condition written into it. The right to hunt there lasted as long as there were buffalo enough to justify the chase. Every hide that went east on the railroad brought the end of that right a little closer, and the people living on the hunt could see it coming.

The buffalo isn't the only grievance. The rations at the agencies arrive short and late. Whiskey traders sell to the camps, and white horse thieves raid Native horse herds.

Even the book that carries Dixon's name, the life story of a man who fought them, doesn't pretend the anger came from nowhere. Its opening chapter, on the Plains tribes and their homeland, says: "That the Indian should have resisted with relentless and increasing ferocity every effort to drive him from this paradise was natural and justifiable from his point of view."

That spring, a young Quahadi Comanche medicine man named Isatai is gathering a following. He's predicted that a comet would disappear and that a drought was coming, and people have started to listen. In May, he calls a Sun Dance, the first the Comanche have ever held as a whole people. A report quoted in Dixon's book puts it near the mouth of the Sweetwater, a branch of the Red River. Cheyenne come, and some Kiowa. And there, the war against the hunters is planned.

One of the men who'll lead it is a Quahadi war leader named Quanah. He's in his twenties, though nobody is sure of the year he was born, somewhere between about 1845 and 1852. His father was Peta Nocona. His mother was Cynthia Ann Parker, taken captive in Texas in 1836.

Isatai's promise comes in two parts. The hunters will be killed in their sleep. And his paint will make the warriors bulletproof. When Dixon heard about it later, the version that reached him was that the medicine man had told the warriors they'd only need to ride into Adobe Walls and knock the hunters on the head with sticks, because the hunters' bullets wouldn't be strong enough to break their skin.

Everything in that promise depends on one thing, catching the hunters asleep. And by June, the hunters have had their warnings.

## The warnings

In June, four hunters are killed near the Walls. Dudley and Wallace die on Chicken Creek. A man called Cheyenne Jack, whose real name was John Jones, and another known as Blue Billy are killed on the Salt Fork of the Red River. Dixon says they all took the killings the same way, as a warning to the hunters to clear out and get back north of the dead line.

The hunters stay. That same month, the thing they came for finally shows up. The herds arrive, and the killing starts again in earnest. Dixon is out at his camp on Dixon Creek, and when he describes the work there, he doesn't dress it up. He put it this way: "This was deadly business, without sentiment; it was dollars against tenderheartedness, and dollars won."

But Dixon himself is having a bad month. Crossing the Canadian while it's in flood, his wagon turns over, and he loses his big fifty-caliber rifle and his mule, Joe. So on the evening of the 26th of June, he's at the Walls buying a replacement from James Langton, a forty-four-caliber Sharps priced at $80, more than a month and a half of a skinner's wages. And he makes a small mistake that will matter the next morning. He leaves the case of ammunition for the new rifle sitting in Rath's store.

He's also just made a deal. Dixon is 23, and Jim Hanrahan, the oldest man at the Walls, has taken him on as a partner for half the profits. Hanrahan runs a big outfit that usually keeps seven skinners busy. Their wagons are already loaded. They plan to pull out for the hunting grounds the next morning, the 27th.

So the night of the 26th is a last night in town. Only 8 or 9 of the men at the Walls actually live there. The rest are hunters passing through, like Dixon. The youngest man there is a hunter named Bat Masterson. Most of them spread their blankets on the ground outside, and the doors of the buildings stand open behind them. Dixon remembered that evening like this: "In all that vast wilderness, ours were the only lights save the stars that glittered above us."

Four buildings, a corral, wagons loaded for the morning, and most of the men asleep in the open. For a war party planning to kill men in their sleep, it could hardly look better.

Some time after the lights go out, the ridgepole in Hanrahan's saloon cracks. That's where this story started.

## First light

Back on the roof of Hanrahan's, the prop is in place and the sky is getting light. There's no sense going back to sleep now, with the wagons leaving this morning, so Hanrahan sends a man named Billy Ogg down along the creek to bring in the horses. They're grazing about a quarter of a mile southeast of the buildings.

Dixon goes back to his wagon. And when he looks out toward the horses, he sees something past them, at the edge of the timber. A dark mass, moving, too dim to make out.

Dixon later wrote: "The black body of moving object suddenly spread out like a fan, and from it went up one single, solid yell". He says the war whoop seemed to shake the air. If you're asleep on the ground by the buildings, that yell is the first thing you know about any of this.

What he's looking at is hundreds of riders, coming at a gallop straight for the buildings, and the horse herd is right in their path. Dixon says they carried guns and lances and heavy shields of thick buffalo hide. If you're Billy Ogg, you're on foot out by the horses, with nothing between you and the buildings but open ground, and the charge is coming up behind you.

Dixon ties his saddle horse to his wagon, grabs his rifle, fires one shot, and runs for the nearest door. It's Hanrahan's. He gets inside, and right behind him Billy Ogg comes through the same door, having outrun the charge the whole way back.

Everyone else does the same thing. They run for whatever building is closest, and that's how the defense of Adobe Walls gets organized, by accident. Nine men end up in Hanrahan's saloon. Hanrahan, Dixon, Billy Ogg, Bat Masterson, Hiram Watson, James McKinley, a hunter called Bermuda Carlisle, and Mike Welch and Shepherd, the two who heard the ridgepole crack. Eleven are in Myers and Leonard's store. Fred Leonard, James Campbell, Edward Trevor, Frank Brown, Harry Armitage, Dutch Henry, Billy Tyler, Old Man Keeler, Mike McCabe, Henry Lease, and a man the others called Frenchy. And in Rath's, the smallest building on the row, there are six men and Mrs. Olds. James Langton, George Eddy, Thomas O'Keefe the blacksmith, Sam Smith, Andy Johnson, William Olds, and his wife.

Two men never reach a door. The Shadler brothers are freighters, asleep in their wagon outside. They're killed there in the first rush, and both are scalped. Their big Newfoundland dog is killed with them.

So the real number behind those walls is 26 men and one woman, split three ways, with no way to talk to each other across the open ground. And not all of them were buffalo hunters, either. Besides the Shadlers, the list includes a bartender, a blacksmith, a saloon keeper and the man who runs Rath's store.

Coming at them are mostly Comanche and Southern Cheyenne fighters, with smaller numbers of Kiowa and Arapaho. Quanah is riding with the Comanche. Dixon also names Big Bow and Lone Wolf among the leaders, and two Cheyenne chiefs, Little Robe and White Shield. But his own book shows those two Cheyennes at the Cheyenne Agency that September, talking to the agent, so his list of leaders is best treated as unconfirmed.

And how many are there? Dixon reckoned 700 was a safe guess, and he says the estimates he heard ran as high as 1,000. Bat Masterson, in a letter printed in the same book, says a full thousand, and adds the Arapaho. Modern historians, drawing partly on Native testimony, usually give a smaller force, commonly somewhere between 250 and 700. Take the bottom of that range and it's still about nine attackers for every man at the Walls.

And at first, the warriors ride right up to the doors.

## The longest morning

As Dixon tells it, "For the first half hour the Indians were reckless and daring enough to ride up and strike the doors with the butts of their guns."

If you're inside Hanrahan's, that's what you hear. Rifle butts hammering on the door a few feet from you, while the men beside you drag sacks of flour and grain across the floor and pile them against it. Some of them are still in their night clothes. Dixon says many of them fought the whole day barefoot. And with no port-holes, every shot the hunters fire goes out through a window or a transom, which means every window is a way in for a bullet too.

Then the hunters hear a bugle. The warriors are charging to bugle calls, the way cavalry does. But in Hanrahan's saloon there are men who've served in the army, and they know those calls. When the bugle sounds a charge, the men inside know what's coming.

The guns matter too. These are work rifles, heavy Sharps and big fifties made for killing buffalo at long range, and a good number of the men holding them shoot for a living.

Over at Myers and Leonard's, Billy Tyler goes out into the stockade and has to fall back under fire. Dixon later described how Tyler turned at the door of the store to shoot back and was hit by a bullet that went through his lungs. They get him inside. He lives about half an hour, the only man inside the walls to die that day. Nobody in the other two buildings knows, and it'll be hours before anyone crosses the open ground to find out.

Out on the field, Isatai's promise is coming apart in front of everyone. Quanah's horse is shot from under him, and he's wounded in the shoulder. That comes from his own account, as it was reported in Dixon's book. Later Native accounts say Isatai watched the fight from a rise, and that his horse was shot from under him too. Every man on that field has just watched the paint fail.

The warriors keep riding in close anyway, for a reason the hunters noticed again and again. When a man goes down near the buildings, others ride in under fire to bring him out. Dixon wrote that in those days a warrior would quickly risk his own life to carry a dead or helpless comrade out of the enemy's reach. It's a brave thing to do in front of buffalo guns, and it costs lives. It also means that nobody inside the walls will ever be able to count the dead.

Around ten in the morning, a warrior takes cover behind the big stack of buffalo hides at the back of Rath's store. He's using the hunters' own hides for cover. By now Dixon is carrying a different rifle. The cartridges for his new forty-four are sitting in Rath's, so he's handed the forty-four to Hanrahan and taken Hanrahan's big fifty from Shepherd, the bartender, who's too excited to make much use of it. Dixon puts a bullet through one corner of the hide stack, aimed at the man behind it. He'll still have a big fifty in his hands two days from now, when he fires the shot everyone remembers.

And all morning, the horses die. Dixon's own saddle horse, the one he tied to his wagon at dawn, is one of the first shot. Horses hit far out in the valley come back to the buildings anyway and stand there bleeding, as if, Dixon said, they were coming to the hunters for help.

By noon the charges stop. And in Hanrahan's saloon, the men are almost out of cartridges.

## Noon to nightfall

The warriors are firing from farther out now. In Hanrahan's, the men have been shooting since dawn. Rath's store has thousands of rounds, brought down from Dodge for the hunters. So Dixon and Hanrahan decide to run for it.

If you're watching from the saloon window, you see two men break out and sprint for Rath's door. Dixon later wrote: "I have no idea how many guns were cracking away at us, but I do know that bullets rattled round us like hail."

They both make it. Hanrahan fills a sack with cartridges and runs it back to the saloon, but Dixon stays. Rath's is the building with the fewest men in it, and it's the one with Mrs. Olds inside. Dixon says that all that first day she stayed cool and composed and lent a hand in every emergency.

That afternoon, Dixon climbs up on the flour-sack barricade in Rath's to look out over the grass, spots a man crawling there, and fires the big fifty. The recoil throws him clean off the sacks. He comes down in a crash of tubs and pans, and the men in the store run to him, sure he's been shot. He hasn't been. He climbs back up and keeps firing, and his third shot hits the man in the grass.

By two in the afternoon, the warriors have pulled back to the foot of the hills and split up, some to the east of the buildings and some to the west.

Around four, Bermuda Carlisle steps out of Hanrahan's. Nobody shoots at him. Others follow him out, and for the first time since dawn they can see the whole field. Only then do the men from the saloon find out that Billy Tyler has been dead since the morning.

There's one more death from that day the hunters would tell stories about. According to Masterson, the bugler was killed late on that first day, shot by Harry Armitage with a fifty-caliber Sharps.

Then they count what's left. Fifty-six dead horses lie around the buildings, 10 of them the hunters' own, along with the Shadlers' 28 oxen. Every horse the hunters had is dead or run off, so nobody at Adobe Walls is riding anywhere. Dixon counted 13 dead warriors left on the ground near the buildings, lying too close to the guns for anyone to bring them out.

At dusk they wrap Tyler and the two Shadlers in blankets and bury them together in a single grave on the north side of the corral. Decades later, Dixon said, there was still no stone on it. No attack comes that night.

Isatai's plan needed the hunters asleep, and a cracked ridgepole had fifteen of them up on a roof before first light. Once the surprise was gone, the attackers had to break in, and they hit two feet of sod and stacked flour. Starving them of ammunition was no use either, with the store next door full of cartridges. The paint was meant to protect them, and Quanah and Isatai both had their horses shot from under them. And every charge was announced by a bugle that former soldiers inside could read.

The big attack was over. Nobody inside knew that yet.

## The siege days

On the 28th of June, the second day, the dead are still lying where they fell, and they have to be moved. With no teams left to pull anything, the men roll the bodies of the dead warriors and the carcasses of the horses onto buffalo hides and drag them away. Twelve horses lying together get a pit of their own.

Later that day, a wagon comes up the valley. It belongs to a man named George Bellfield. Jim and Bob Cator come in the same day. And somebody has to get word to Dodge City. Henry Lease, one of the men from Myers and Leonard's, volunteers. After dark on the second day, he rides out alone on Bellfield's horse, about 150 miles through country where the war parties are still out.

On the third day comes the shot. The one everybody has heard about, and most of what gets repeated about it isn't in Dixon's own account.

About fifteen warriors appear on the bluff east of the creek, on that same hill that rises like a butte across the valley. Dixon takes aim with the big fifty. At that distance, a man on horseback is a dot on the hillside. Here it is in the words of the man who fired it: "The distance was not far from three-fourths of a mile. A number of exaggerated accounts have been written about this incident. I took careful aim and pulled the trigger. We saw an Indian fall from his horse."

Then two warriors run out on foot, pick up the fallen man, and carry him to cover. Three days in, they're still going back for their own. Dixon himself called it a scratch shot, a lucky one, and he was a man who knew he could shoot.

Now hold that up against the legend. The number you'll usually hear is 1,538 yards, said to come from an army survey made later. That number isn't in Dixon's book. Neither is the claim that the shot came on the second day, or the fifty-ninety cartridge it's often credited to. The book's own chapter heading and photo caption say 1,200 yards, and three-quarters of a mile is about 1,300. So the man who made the shot gave a shorter distance than the people who tell the story about him, and he still called it luck.

By the fifth day, enough hunters have come in from their camps that the men start fortifying properly. They build small sod lookouts on the roofs of Rath's and Myers and Leonard's, with ladders running up from inside.

William Olds is coming down the ladder at Rath's with his rifle in his hand when it goes off. It kills him on the spot, in front of his wife. Dixon saw it, and he said he'd gladly have faced every warrior from the Cimarron to the Red River rather than watch it happen. They bury Olds about 60 feet southeast of Rath's store. Mrs. Olds, who held steady through the whole of the first day, is now a widow at a post in the middle of a war.

By the sixth day, about a hundred men are at the Walls. Dixon believed that's where a later claim came from, that all of those men had been in the fight. The men who were at the Walls on the 27th numbered 28, and three of them were already in the grave by the corral.

A hundred armed men, plenty of cartridges, and sod walls that had already held once. And about a week after the fight, they start to leave.

## What the record leaves out

Some 25 of them ride north for Dodge under Hanrahan. On the way, they find Henry Lease's partner, Charley Sharp, killed in his camp. A relief party of 40 men under Tom Nixon comes down from Dodge and takes Mrs. Olds and most of the others back with it.

Dixon later wrote: "The fight at Adobe Walls broke up buffalo-hunting in that section just as the Indians had planned. This was the last buffalo-hunting I ever did as a business."

So who won? The hunters kept their buildings. But the warriors went to Adobe Walls to drive the hide hunters out of that country, and by the hunters' own account, that's what happened. It was the second time in ten years that warriors had forced an armed American party out of this valley. In 1864, it had been Kit Carson's column at the old ruins.

None of that helped Isatai. His standing collapsed after the battle. Later Native accounts say he blamed the failure on a Cheyenne who had killed a skunk.

And the war didn't end when the hunters left. All that summer, war parties raided across Texas, Kansas, Colorado and New Mexico. The army answered with what became the Red River War, five columns converging on the Panhandle, Miles coming down from Fort Dodge and Mackenzie coming up from Fort Concho. Dixon signed on as a scout for General Nelson A. Miles on the 6th of August. Riding back to the Walls with Lieutenant Frank Baldwin's troops, he watched a man named George Huffman lanced to death near the buildings. Huffman was the fifth grave at Adobe Walls.

On the 28th of September, Mackenzie's 4th Cavalry surprised the winter camps in Palo Duro Canyon. Few people died there. But the soldiers burned the lodges and the stored food and shot more than 1,000 captured horses, and without horses or food, the bands had to come in over the winter. By October, the abandoned post at Adobe Walls had been burned too, and when Dixon came by, the walls were still smoking.

The Southern Cheyenne under Stone Calf came in that March. On the 2nd of June 1875, Quanah led the Quahadi into Fort Sill, among the last to surrender. Dozens of men named as war leaders were sent to prison at Fort Marion, in Florida. By the end of the 1870s, the southern buffalo herd was gone, and with it the hunting right the Medicine Lodge treaty had promised.

So what does the record leave out? Start with the dead. At the Walls, the hunters' list is exact. The Shadlers and Tyler died on the first day, Olds on the fifth, Huffman when the troops came. The other side was never counted. Dixon's 13 counts only the bodies that were left behind. Totals usually given run from about 15 to 30 killed, plus many wounded, and the Native accounts gathered later give lower figures than the hunters did. And look at where the story of that morning comes from. Dixon's account was dictated when he was in his sixties. Masterson's is a letter printed in the same book. Even Quanah's own account of his wound reached us secondhand, through that book. Nearly all of it comes from the men inside the walls.

Quanah became the leading Comanche spokesman on the reservation. In 1877, Dixon was out with troops near the Palo Duro, the canyon where the winter camps had lost their horses three years before. That's where he met Quanah, and the talk turned to Adobe Walls. Dixon put it simply: "When I told him that I was one of the men that had fought against him, he leaned over on his horse and shook my hand. We became good friends."

Dixon won the Medal of Honor for a fight at Buffalo Wallow during the Red River War. The army struck it from the roll in 1916 or 1917, because he'd been a civilian scout, and restored it in 1989. He scouted for the army until February 1883, and that same year he homesteaded the site of the old Adobe Walls and ran the post office there for nearly 20 years. He died of pneumonia on the 9th of March 1913, the year before his book came out, and was buried at Texline. In 1929, his remains were moved to the battle site.

Go back to the roof of Hanrahan's saloon, two in the morning, the 27th of June. When the men looked at that cottonwood ridgepole afterward, they never found a break in it, and nobody has ever explained the crack that woke them. Billy Dixon said that sound saved every one of them. He's buried now in the valley where he heard it.
