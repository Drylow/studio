NOTES: "The Fetterman Fight's Last 30 Minutes Were Worse Than You Were Told"
Channel: Frontier Blood. Event: the Fetterman Fight ("Fetterman Massacre"; to the Lakota and Cheyenne the Battle of the Hundred in the Hand / of the Hundred Slain), Friday 21 December 1866, Red Cloud's War.
Place: the ridge (later "Massacre Hill") beyond Lodge Trail Ridge, about 3-5 miles north of Fort Phil Kearny, Dakota Territory (today near Story, Wyoming).
Through-line witness: Frances Grummond, about 21, pregnant, wife of Lt. George W. Grummond, who rode out that day and did not come back.

VERIFIED QUOTES (verbatim; source named; never rephrase)
1. “We came in sight of Pilot Hill and could see the picket guard waving a signal, a double signal, if we could have interpreted it, and it was well that we did not.” — Frances (1910), ch. XI. Context: September 1866, her arrival; the signals meant a wood-train attack and her own party approaching. At the gate a wagon passed carrying a woodcutter just killed (one line, no detail).
2. “That strange feeling of apprehension never left me, enhanced as it was by my delicate condition” — Frances (1910), ch. XI. "Delicate condition": she was pregnant.
3. “"a company of regulars could whip a thousand, and a regiment could whip the whole array of hostile tribes,"” — Margaret Carrington (1868), ch. XXI. Context: Nov 1866, an opinion she says Fetterman "had often given language" to. The earliest printed boast found (see RULES).
4. “Captain Fetterman has been in, and says "he has learned a lesson, and that this Indian war has become a hand-to-hand fight, requiring the utmost caution,"” — Margaret Carrington (1868), journal of 6 Dec 1866, after the fight that killed Lt. Bingham and Sgt. Bowers. The source that blames Fetterman also records him learning caution.
5. “No sleep came to my weary eyes, except fitfully, for many nights, and even then in my dreams I could see him riding madly from me with the Indians in pursuit.” — Frances (1910), ch. XVII. Context: after 6 Dec, when Grummond, cut off with three men, outran pursuers with a spear "at his back" (her words).
6. “saying that it was asking too much of their people—asking all they had—for it would drive away all the game.” — Special Commission report (1867), in Absaraka's Appendix. Context: June 1866 council at Fort Laramie; the Lakota leaders' answer to the demand for forts on the Bozeman road.
7. “December, 21st, 1866, was to us the saddest day of the year.” — Margaret Carrington (1868), ch. XXIV (comma as printed).
8. “" Support the wood-train, relieve it, and report to me. Do not engage or pursue Indians at its expense; under no circumstances pursue over the Ridge, viz.: Lodge trail Ridge, as per map in your possession."” — Col. Carrington's official report (3 Jan 1867), his orders to Fetterman, about 11 a.m., 21 Dec.
9. “I was standing in front of my door next the commanding officer's headquarters and both saw and heard all that transpired. I was filled with dread and horror at the thought that after my husband's hairbreadth escape scarcely three weeks before he could be so eager to fight the Indians again.” — Frances (1910), ch. XIX, late morning 21 Dec.
10. “"Under no circumstances must you cross Lodge Trail Ridge;" and the column moved quickly from sight.” — Frances (1910), ch. XIX: Carrington, on the sentry walk inside the stockade, halts the column and shouts the order again. Can follow: “I stood for a time, moments indeed, almost dazed, my heart filled with strange forebodings, then turned, entered my little house, and closed my door.”
11. “"Answer me quickly," he said, "I have a hundred or more,"” — White Elk's account in Grinnell (1915). Context: the day before the fight; a Lakota holy person (White Elk: "a person, half man and half woman") rode out four times seeking the enemy, returning with "ten men, five in each hand", then twenty, then fifty, all refused by the chiefs; the fourth time fell from the horse with both hands on the ground and said this, and the warriors yelled. Origin of "Hundred in the Hand".
12. “At twelve o'clock firing was heard toward Peno Creek, beyond Lodge Trail Ridge. A few shots were followed by constant shots, not to be counted.” — Carrington's official report (1867).
13. “When the charge was made the sound of many hoofs made a noise like thunder and the soldiers began to fall back.” — White Elk's account in Grinnell (1915). Context: the decoys, across the creek at the end of the ridge, split and crossed paths (the signal); the hidden warriors rose on both sides.
14. “the arrows flew so thickly above the troops that to him they seemed like a lot of grasshoppers flying across each other.” — White Elk's account in Grinnell (1915): the mounted soldiers fighting back up the icy slope.
15. “Then followed a few quick volleys, then scattering shots, and then, dead silence. Less than half an hour had passed, and the silence was dreadful.” — Frances (1910), ch. XIX, at the fort, while Capt. Ten Eyck's relief force was still crossing the creek.
16. “They took our bait exactly as we had hoped! It was a matter of a very few minutes, for every soldier lay dead in a shorter time than it takes to annihilate a small herd of buffalo.” — Rain-in-the-Face, as recorded by Charles Eastman (1918). Plan as he gives it: chase the woodchoppers, retreat slowly, lead any pursuers into the ambush. (His dating "some two years later" and his list of chiefs present are unreliable: use only this.)
17. “"All are dead but the dog; let him carry the news to the fort," but someone else cried out: " No, do not let even a dog get away "” — White Elk's account in Grinnell (1915): the end of the fight, where the monument now stands.
18. “The ladies clustered in Mrs. Wands' cabin as night drew on, all speechless from absolute stagnation and terror. Then the crunching of wagon wheels startled us to our feet. The gates opened.” — Frances (1910): dusk 21 Dec, Ten Eyck's wagons bring 49 bodies and word that probably no one survived.
19. “"Mrs. Grummond, I shall go in person, and will bring back to you the remains of your husband." I could only reply, "They are beyond all suffering now. You must not imperil other precious lives and make other women as miserable as myself."” — Frances (1910), ch. XX: morning 22 Dec, after the officers' council opposed a search.
20. “In one sense, that slowly moving train was a funeral procession, for my husband's remains were placed in one wagon, that they might accompany me to my home in Tennessee for a resting place until the final Roll Call.” — Frances (1910): leaving the fort, 23 January 1867, in a snowstorm.

VERIFIED FACTS (source in each line; disputed numbers as ranges)
The road and the forts
- 1863: John Bozeman blazed the trail through the Powder River Basin, the short road to the Montana gold fields, across Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho hunting grounds (WyoHistory; Grinnell).
- Sand Creek, 29 Nov 1864: Colorado volunteers under Col. Chivington killed about 150 Cheyenne and Arapaho at a peaceful camp; survivors fled north to the Powder River country (WyoHistory).
- June 1866, Fort Laramie: during treaty talks Carrington arrived with about 700 men bound to build forts in that country; Red Cloud and his followers withdrew, refused the presents and went to war (Special Commission 1867; WyoHistory).
- The Commission (1867) found the bands fought "for the purpose of defending their ancient possessions", calling the land, "with propriety", "their last best hunting-grounds".
- Three forts, summer 1866: Reno (old Fort Connor), Phil Kearny (camp 13 July, Grinnell; established 15 July, WyoHistory), C. F. Smith in Montana (August). Phil Kearny: 8-foot log stockade around 17 acres (WyoHistory), about 230-236 miles from Fort Laramie.
- Wood was its lifeline: daily trains to the pinery 5-6 miles west (WyoHistory). 26 July-21 Dec 1866: 51 hostile appearances before the fort, 96 soldiers and 58 civilians killed in the district (Commission's figures, Fetterman's dead included).
- Carrington: Yale-educated lawyer, never in battle in the Civil War (Brady 1904; Smith; State Historic Site).
People
- Frances Courtney of Franklin, Tennessee (born about 1845, genealogical records) married George W. Grummond, a Michigan volunteer officer (lieutenant colonel) she met while he was posted at Franklin (Williamson Scene history column). She reached the fort "in September" 1866 (Carrington 1908), joining four other officers' wives (Frances).
- Capt. (brevet Lt. Col.) William J. Fetterman arrived 3 Nov 1866 (WyoHistory; the State Historic Site says 4 Nov): decorated Civil War officer, due to command the new 27th Infantry from 1 Jan 1867 (Shannon Smith, WyoHistory). Capt. Frederick Brown, quartermaster, already ordered away, wanted "one more chance" (Frances).
- Grummond: historian Shannon Smith (WyoHistory 2014) documents reckless conduct, drunkenness and a court-martial in his record.
6 December 1866 (the rehearsal)
- Lt. Bingham and Sgt. Bowers killed, one sergeant and four privates wounded (Cooke, 27 Dec). Fetterman's report: about 14 men held off about 100 warriors after most cavalry fled. State Historic Site: from this fight "the warriors learned that if they could lure a large military command over the ridge they could be wiped out."
The other camp
- Tongue River camps: Oglala and Minneconjou Lakota (some Brulé), Northern Cheyenne, Arapaho (State Historic Site; Grinnell). White Elk: a small party would draw the soldiers out, or else they would attack the post. Ten decoys: two Cheyennes (Little Wolf and Wolf Left Hand named; Little Wolf offered his place to his brother Big Nose), two Arapahos, two from each of three Lakota tribes (Grinnell).
- Leaders: Minneconjou High Backbone (Hump) usually named (State Historic Site; WyoHistory). Cheyenne witnesses told Grinnell the Lakota chiefs were Black Leg and Black Shield and that Red Cloud "was not present—at least under this name"; the State Historic Site: accounts disagree, and Red Cloud later spoke of being there. RED CLOUD'S PRESENCE IS DISPUTED.
- Crazy Horse, documented vs legend: no 1866-67 army record names him. Judge Eli Ricker's interviews (1906-07) mention Crazy Horse and American Horse among the decoys (State Historic Site); Eastman (1918): Crazy Horse "was chosen to lead the attack on the woodchoppers, designed to draw the soldiers out of the fort". White Elk's Cheyenne account does not mention him. State Historic Site: some oral history makes him leader of the decoys, "but there are conflicting accounts." Safe line: "Lakota accounts recorded decades later place the young Oglala Crazy Horse among the ten decoys."
- Warriors (DISPUTED): 600 in ambush (Eastman); 1,500-1,800 (Special Commission); 1,500-3,000 (Gen. Cooke's "judgment of the probabilities"); "nearly three thousand" (Carrington's first telegram); Cheyennes and Arapahos "a good many hundred", Lakota three times as many, more than at the Little Bighorn (White Elk). Modern: "more than 1,000" (WyoHistory), "more than 1,500 may have taken part" (HistoryNet on Monnett 2017). Say "between one and two thousand by most estimates".
Weapons and why they mattered
- Infantry (about 49): single-shot muzzle-loading Springfield rifle-muskets, .58 caliber, 2-4 shots a minute (State Historic Site; NRA Museum; WyoHistory: "three rounds a minute at most").
- Cavalry (27, Co. C, 2nd Cavalry): 7-shot Spencer carbines (State Historic Site; Carrington: "the Cavalry having the new carbine"). Brady (1904) says most of these recruits still had muskets: say "probably Spencers".
- Wheatley and Fisher, civilian employees: Henry repeaters, "sixteen-shot" (Hebard); more than fifty empty shells around one of them (Commission). The Henry dates from 1862: Hebard's "just invented that year" is wrong.
- Ammunition: "not a cartridge box full to every man" (Frances); Carrington (1908): 1,000 rounds at Laramie instead of 20,000 promised. Cooke (27 Dec) urged breech-loading Springfields for all these troops.
- Warriors: mostly bows, lances, war clubs; about one in ten had a firearm (State Historic Site); White Elk: "Most of the Indians were armed with bows. The few who had guns had old smooth-bore flintlocks." Close in, arrows came faster than a muzzle-loader could reload (Margaret). Arrows from both sides of the trap also hit warriors (White Elk; Smith).
21 December: the timeline (army clock times)
- About 10-11 a.m.: the season's last wood train leaves late with about 90 armed men (Margaret; Frances). About 11: Pilot Hill signals "many Indians"; the train corrals about a mile and a half out (Carrington).
- Capt. Powell was offered the command; Fetterman claimed it by seniority; Grummond asked for the cavalry; Brown and the two civilians joined (Carrington; Margaret). Orders given at headquarters, repeated by Lt. Wands, shouted again from the sentry walk (Carrington; Frances; to Grummond: "implicitly obey orders, and not leave him", Carrington). 81 men: 3 officers, 76 enlisted, 2 civilians (State Historic Site; Grinnell).
- A howitzer case shot at the Big Piney crossing flushed nearly thirty hidden warriors (Carrington). Fetterman went north-east toward Lodge Trail Ridge, not west to the train (Smith; Commission). The train broke corral and went on; it never heard the fight (Carrington; Margaret).
- About 11:45: Fetterman's men on the crest as skirmishers, then over it (Special Commission). Smith argues Grummond's cavalry raced ahead and Fetterman followed to support them.
- About 12:00: firing heard. Ten Eyck sent with about 50-76 men and wagons (Carrington 1908; Commission), "hardly twelve minutes" later (Margaret), by a longer route (Commission). About 12:45: from the summit he sees the valley full of warriors challenging him to come down; the last shots fall silent (Commission). Grinnell: the fight "ended about noon".
- HOW LONG (DISPUTED): "little more than half an hour" (Carrington 1867), "little more than thirty minutes" (Margaret), "half an hour or more" (Commission), "less than half an hour" (Frances; Eastman), "inside of twenty-one minutes" (Carrington 1908), "a very few minutes" (Rain-in-the-Face). Say "about half an hour, perhaps forty minutes".
- Positions (State Historic Site; Commission; Carrington): Wheatley, Fisher and a few veterans died among rocks at the far end; Grummond on the road between; most infantry, Fetterman and Brown among rocks at the end nearest the fort. Bugler Adolph Metzger fought to the last; American Horse said the warriors covered him with a buffalo robe to honor him (State Historic Site, from Ricker interviews). Fetterman's death is DISPUTED: Carrington and the Commission concluded he and Brown shot each other; American Horse claimed he killed him (told to Capt. James H. Cook, Hebard 1922).
- The post surgeon judged not more than six were killed by bullets (Carrington). The dead were stripped and mutilated; historians link this to Plains war custom and to revenge for Sand Creek (Smith; State Historic Site). One sentence only.
- Native losses (DISPUTED): Carrington counted 65 pools of blood in an acre and nine or ten dead ponies; White Elk: two Cheyennes killed, Lakota dead in two rows of "perhaps fifty or sixty"; "about a dozen" in some Indian sources (State Historic Site); 13 (Ambrose), 14 (George Bent), 11 Oglala named by Red Cloud (these three via Wikipedia, UNVERIFIED). Say "Native sources usually name between a dozen and twenty dead; one Cheyenne witness remembered far more."
After the fight
- Dusk 21 Dec: Ten Eyck brings 49 bodies. 22 Dec: Carrington with about 80 men recovers the rest, Grummond among them. Before leaving he ordered that, in a last struggle, the women and children be put in the magazine and all destroyed rather than captured (Frances; questioned because Margaret never mentions it, State Historic Site).
- Burial: numbered pine coffins, a pit 50 feet long and 7 deep, frozen ground dug in short shifts, finished Wednesday 26 Dec (Margaret; Frances). Fetterman, Brown and Grummond in one grave (Carrington); Grummond's body later taken home.
- Night of 21 Dec: John "Portugee" Phillips rode for Fort Laramie, about 236 miles (Hebard; Frances 235); the telegram left Horseshoe Station on Christmas Day; he reached Laramie Christmas night during a ball (State Historic Site). Not alone all the way: courier Daniel Dixon also rode to Fort Reno, both paid $300; others joined at Reno (State Historic Site).
- 26 Dec, before any report arrived: Gen. Cooke relieved Carrington, ordering him to Fort Casper (Special Orders No. 126). Gen. Wessells arrived 16 Jan 1867 (State Historic Site) through snow at 25-40 below (Frances).
- Inquiries: the Special Commission blamed lack of troops and supplies; an army court under Gen. John Gibbon followed (Carrington 1908). Carrington's report and the full Commission report were printed only in 1887 (Frances; Carrington 1908).
- 23 Jan 1867: Frances, the Carringtons and Grummond's coffin leave with 40 infantry and 20 cavalry; six miles the first day; the thermometer's mercury froze; frostbite, amputations at Fort Reno (Frances; State Historic Site). Carrington's own revolver went off on the march and wounded him in the leg (Frances). Home in Tennessee in March 1867, "after a journey of seven weeks"; "A month later a son was born." Grummond lies in Rest Haven Cemetery, Franklin, Tennessee (Williamson County history column).
- 1 Aug 1867 Hayfield Fight near Fort C. F. Smith; 2 Aug 1867 Wagon Box Fight: about 32 men behind wagon boxes with new breech-loading Springfield Model 1866 ("Allin") rifles, 15-20 shots a minute, held off hundreds of warriors (WyoHistory). New rifles: "over 80" (State Historic Site) or 700 (WyoHistory); Native dead: six by their own count, 60-1,500 in white claims (WyoHistory).
- Fort Laramie Treaty: signed 29 April 1868 (first signers, Brulé). Article XVI: the posts abandoned and the road closed within 90 days of peace; Red Cloud signed on 6 November 1868, after the forts were abandoned (National Archives text). Last troops left Phil Kearny in late August 1868; it was burned, probably by Cheyennes under Little Wolf (State Historic Site; WyoHistory; Grinnell).
- The dead were exhumed in 1888 and reburied at the Little Bighorn battlefield, later moved into Custer National Cemetery (Sheridan Media on the fort cemetery; True West). Hebard (1922) gives 1896: say "in the late 1880s".
How the accounts reached us
- Army accounts come from the field afterwards and from what the fort heard. Margaret published Absaraka in 1868 to defend her husband; died 1870 (WyoHistory). Frances married Henry Carrington in 1871, lived in Boston (1882) and Hyde Park (1885), returned to Massacre Hill with him on 3 July 1908, published in 1910, died in 1911 (her book; 1911 obituary reprinted by Sheridan Media). Henry died in 1912 (Wikipedia, UNVERIFIED beyond the year).
- The Native side survived in oral tradition and later interviews: Ricker (1906-07), Grinnell with White Elk on the field (about 1914), Eastman (1918). Copyrighted, PARAPHRASE ONLY, book named: White Bull (Minneconjou, 17) came home with a captured carbine, a wound and a coup (Stanley Vestal, "Warpath", 1934; Monnett, "Eyewitness to the Fetterman Fight: Indian Views", 2017); Fire Thunder (Oglala) told Neihardt of the "Battle of the Hundred Slain" ("Black Elk Speaks", 1932), details not checked; American Horse's Ricker interview (ed. R. Jensen, 2005).
- Reassessment: Shannon D. Smith, "Give Me Eighty Men" (U. of Nebraska Press, 2009 per WyoHistory); John H. Monnett, "Where a Hundred Soldiers Were Killed" (2010): the reckless-Fetterman story was largely built by the Carringtons' side.

STORY SHAPE (chronological, about 38 minutes; Frances's view alternating with the wider picture)
1. Cold open (0:00-3:00): 11 a.m., 21 Dec 1866; a pregnant 21-year-old at her door watches her husband ride out; the shouted order (quotes 9, 10); noon firing, then silence (15). No soldier lived to tell the last half hour: we hear it from both sides.
2. The last hunting grounds (3:00-7:00): Montana gold, Bozeman's shortcut, Sand Creek, the June 1866 council and walk-out, "asking all they had" (6); 700 soldiers, three forts, the stockade and the pinery, attacks almost daily.
3. A bride on the Bozeman (7:00-11:00): Frances from Franklin, Tennessee; the double signal (1); the dead woodcutter at the gate; pregnancy (2); the other wives; tents, snow, "All's well".
4. The officers (11:00-15:00): Fetterman arrives; the "company of regulars" line (3) and the later "eighty men" myth (RULES); 6 Dec: Bingham killed, Grummond's ride (5), Fetterman's "lesson" (4); the warriors learn the trap works.
5. The other camp (15:00-19:00): Tongue River camps; White Elk joins the war party; the hundred in the hand (11); ten decoys from every tribe; Big Nose and Little Wolf; Crazy Horse as later Lakota accounts remember him; the Red Cloud question; his war speech as remembered (backup).
6. The morning (19:00-23:00): late wood train, 11 a.m. signal, Fetterman claims command, Grummond the cavalry, Brown and the Henry rifles; the orders (8); weapons mismatch, thin cartridge boxes; howitzer at the crossing.
7. Over the ridge (23:00-27:00): 11:45 on the crest; cavalry ahead after the decoys; Big Nose riding before the guns; the decoys cross paths; thunder of hoofs (13); infantry to the rocks; arrows like grasshoppers (14), friendly fire; Wheatley and Fisher at the far rocks; Grummond falls on the road.
8. The last minutes (27:00-30:00): Ten Eyck's men on the run; at the fort, volleys then silence (15, Margaret backup); the final rush, Metzger's bugle, the dog (17); "a very few minutes" (16); the timing debate told plainly.
9. Night and the dead (30:00-33:00): Ten Eyck sees the valley full of warriors; 49 bodies at dusk, the women in Mrs. Wands' cabin (18); Phillips and the wolf robe; Carrington's promise (19), the magazine order, the lock of hair, burial in frozen ground. The field without close-ups: rocks, empty shells, Native dead carried away.
10. Blame (33:00-35:30): "a fight unexampled"; Carrington relieved before anyone knew the facts; Sherman's "extermination" telegram; Cooke on muzzle-loaders; the Commission's "nutshell"; reports buried until 1887. Frances's winter march home with the coffin (20), frozen mercury, a son born in April.
11. Aftermath (35:30-38:00): Hayfield and Wagon Box with breech-loaders; the 1868 treaty, forts abandoned and burned, Red Cloud signs in November ("we thought the treaty was to remove the forts"); the dead moved to the Little Bighorn; Frances marries Carrington, returns to Massacre Hill in 1908, publishes in 1910; White Elk walks the field with Grinnell; the boast traced and taken apart. Close on "the silence was dreadful" or on the dog.

RULES
- Quote only from VERIFIED QUOTES / BACKUP LINES, source named on screen ("Frances Grummond, 1910", "Margaret Carrington, 1868", "Col. Carrington's report", "White Elk, as told to G. B. Grinnell", "Rain-in-the-Face, as told to Charles Eastman"): Native accounts are always "as told to".
- Both wives wrote to defend Henry Carrington (Frances 42 years later, as his wife): "Frances remembered/wrote"; their picture of Fetterman is not neutral.
- THE BOAST: never present "Give me eighty men and I'll ride through the whole Sioux nation" as Fetterman's words. Paper trail: 1868, Margaret's "a company of regulars could whip a thousand" (no eighty men); 1904, Brady (checked by Carrington): Fetterman and Brown "offered with eighty men to ride through the whole Sioux Nation!"; 1908, Carrington: "I can take eighty men and go to Tongue River."; 1922, Hebard & Brininstool, unsourced. Eighty is also the size of the dead command. Flag as unverified and show how it grew.
- Numbers: 81 killed (not 80, 84 or 94); warriors and Native dead as ranges with who gives each; fight length 20-45 minutes by source. No single Native casualty figure as fact.
- Red Cloud led the war; do not say he led the ambush in person (disputed). Crazy Horse: "among the decoys, according to Lakota accounts recorded decades later"; invent no actions for him and never "Crazy Horse led the attack" as fact.
- The "hundred in the hand" seer: "a holy person" or White Elk's own description; never Grinnell's footnote words. The State Historic Site names Crazy Mule instead: do not name the seer.
- Respect: the Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho defended country a federal commission called theirs; this was a planned victory. No period words (savages, hordes, red men, squaws, braves) in narration; skip Margaret's false "blood-groove" arrow claim.
- No gore: never read Carrington's mutilation list, Bowers' wound, the "105 arrows" or Hebard's descriptions.
