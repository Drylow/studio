# The Fetterman Fight's Last 30 Minutes Were Worse Than You Were Told

You are at Fort Phil Kearny in morning cold on December 21, 1866, as Frances Grummond watches her husband ride toward the forbidden ridge, and you are about to discover why the last 30 minutes were worse than the old story admits. Lieutenant George Grummond is among 81 men in Captain William Fetterman's command, sent to support a wood train 5 or 6 miles out. Carrington's January 1867 report says the order was plain. Support the train. Do not pursue. Do not cross Lodge Trail Ridge. Beyond it waited a trap of decoys, terrain, weapons, separation, and delayed relief.

## The forbidden ridge
At that moment Frances Grummond is pregnant, and standing close to the commanding officer's headquarters, where every sound carries in the cold air.

More than 40 years later, in her 1910 account, she placed herself there with a precision memory sometimes has after disaster. "I was standing in front of my door next the commanding officer's headquarters and both saw and heard all that transpired."

Her husband, Lieutenant George W. Grummond, is joining the mounted detachment. Fetterman has the infantry. Captain Frederick Brown is with him. Two civilians, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher, will go too. In all, 81 men move out under an order that has already been written down.

In Henry Carrington's official report of January 3, 1867, the instruction to Fetterman is plain. "Support the wood-train, relieve it, and report to me. Do not engage or pursue Indians at its expense; under no circumstances pursue over the Ridge, viz.: Lodge trail Ridge, as per map in your possession."

Frances remembered Carrington stopping the column from the sentry walk and calling the warning again. "Under no circumstances must you cross Lodge Trail Ridge;" she wrote, and the command moved quickly from sight.

That is the part the simple version leaves out. The order, the sentry walk, the watching fort, and the ridge line are all part of the record. The order is heard. The forbidden line is visible in the distance.

Frances already has reason to fear it. After Grummond's narrow escape earlier in December, she wrote that she was "filled with dread and horror."

Now the column is gone from view. It has not yet fired a shot. But it is already moving toward the boundary the order was built around.

The story starts in 1863, when John Bozeman and John Jacobs opened a shorter emigrant road to the Montana gold fields, cutting through Powder River hunting country.

## The road north
On a map, the Bozeman Road looks like a saving of miles. On the ground, it turns a shortcut into a war route, because it crosses the hunting country of Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho people, where buffalo herds still made life possible. The 1867 Special Commission later called that country their "last best hunting-grounds."

Another memory fed the resistance to the road.

On November 29, 1864, at Sand Creek in Colorado Territory, volunteer troops under Colonel John Chivington attacked a Cheyenne and Arapaho camp whose leaders had sought peace. The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War reported in 1865 that many of the dead were women and children. Survivors and relatives moved north into the Powder River country, carrying the memory with them.

That memory is part of the reason the forts are there.

By June 1866, treaty talks are underway at Fort Laramie. While those talks are still open, Colonel Henry B. Carrington arrives with about 700 men of the 18th Infantry, ordered to build and garrison forts along the Bozeman Road.

The Special Commission later summarized the Native objection in hard words. The road and forts were "asking too much of their people, asking all they had, for it would drive away all the game."

That same federal commission found that the bands fought "for the purpose of defending their ancient possessions."

Red Cloud and his followers refuse the presents and leave the talks. Red Cloud leads the war that follows, though the evidence does not allow us to say he personally led the Fetterman ambush.

In 1866, three posts are built or occupied along the road. Fort Reno to the south. Fort Phil Kearny in the middle. Fort C. F. Smith farther north. Fort Phil Kearny sits roughly 230 to 236 miles from Fort Laramie.

It is an 8-foot log stockade enclosing about 17 acres. It can hold men, animals, stores, and fear. But it cannot make heat from nothing.

The pinery is 5 or 6 miles away.

By winter, the road problem had become a daily wood problem for the fort.

## A fort under watch
By late summer and fall of 1866, Fort Phil Kearny is still unfinished, still exposed, and still dependent on the country outside its own walls.

The Special Commission counted the pressure afterward. From July 26 to December 21, there were 51 hostile appearances before the fort and in the district. Including the Fetterman dead, 96 soldiers and 58 civilians were killed in that same period.

Those repeated attacks changed daily life at the post.

The stockade can shelter men behind logs 8 feet high. It can hold off a rush if the gates are closed and the firing ports are manned. But it cannot cook, warm barracks, bake bread, finish buildings, or survive a Wyoming winter unless wagons go west to the pinery.

That is the mechanical problem. Every day, the fort has to open itself.

The wood train is slow. Wagons move out with teamsters, infantry guards, and mounted men when the post can spare them. They have to reach the timber 5 or 6 miles away, load, turn, and come back before darkness makes the road worse. A broken wheel or a scattered herd can become a fight.

Carrington commands this problem. He is a Yale-educated lawyer and a capable Civil War administrator, but Cyrus Townsend Brady and the Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site both note the hard limit on his record. In the Civil War, Carrington organized and administered troops. He did not command them in battle. Now he is asked to hold a war zone with a garrison and supply system closer to peacetime than war.

Before Fetterman leaves the gate, the fort's supply problem and manpower problem are already fixed in place.

So the post watches everything. Pilot Hill. Pickets. Blockhouses. Signal flags. Dust. Smoke. Mounted figures at a distance. A mistaken signal can mean nothing, or it can mean a party is already dying.

September 1866. Frances Grummond comes up the Bozeman road.

## Frances arrives
Frances Courtney Grummond is from Franklin, Tennessee. She is about 21 years old, married to Lieutenant George W. Grummond, a former Michigan volunteer officer she met while he was posted in her town.

By September 1866, she is coming into Fort Phil Kearny.

In Frances Grummond's 1910 account, there are already four officers' wives at the post. That matters. The fort is a military enclosure, but people are living inside it. Cabins, tents, trunks, blankets, cooking fires, letters home, and women trying to make a life inside an 8-foot wall in a country where every wagon road can turn into a killing ground.

Frances later remembered the approach in her 1910 account. "We came in sight of Pilot Hill and could see the picket guard waving a signal, a double signal, if we could have interpreted it, and it was well that we did not."

The double signal meant two things at once. One message warned of an attack on a wood train. The other marked the arrival of Frances's party.

The same signal system has to report an arriving officer's wife and a working party under attack.

At the gate, a wagon passes carrying a woodcutter who has just been killed.

Frances writes that from that moment, "That strange feeling of apprehension never left me, enhanced as it was by my delicate condition." The phrase meant she was pregnant.

If you are inside that stockade at night, the walls seem solid. Sentries call "All's well." Snow gathers against cabins and canvas. Men sleep with weapons near them. The cold gets into everything. But the walls only protect the center. They do not protect the wood road. They do not protect the pinery 5 or 6 miles away. They do not protect anyone caught beyond signal range.

Frances wrote more than 40 years later, and by then she was Henry Carrington's wife. Her memory is precious, but it is not neutral.

Still, her 1910 account records what Carrington's official reports do not: how the alarm felt to an officer's wife who reached the post during an attack warning.

She is not on the battlefield. But she will hear the battle end.

The double signal was a warning sign. The post lived by signals, and soon one signal beyond the ridge would arrive too late.

## The officers
Captain William J. Fetterman reaches Fort Phil Kearny in early November 1866.

He is also brevet Lieutenant Colonel Fetterman, a title earned from Civil War service. He is a decorated officer, and he expects to command the new 27th Infantry when the Army reorganization takes effect on January 1, 1867.

This is where the popular version usually begins.

Fetterman is remembered as the man who said he could ride through the whole Sioux nation with 80 men. That line is repeated because it is clean. It gives the disaster a simple cause. Pride speaks, the ridge is crossed, 81 men die.

That is too neat.

The earliest printed form comes from Margaret Carrington's 1868 book, Ab-sa-ra-ka. She writes that Fetterman had often used language expressing the view that "a company of regulars could whip a thousand, and a regiment could whip the whole array of hostile tribes."

That is not the famous 80 men line.

In 1904, Cyrus Townsend Brady, whose account Carrington read and corrected on matters of fact, gives a version where Fetterman and Captain Frederick Brown are tied to the "eighty men" form. In 1908, Henry Carrington gives another version. By 1922, Grace Raymond Hebard and E. A. Brininstool repeat a more developed story.

Later versions make the boast shorter, cleaner, and closer to the number of men killed.

And the number 80 is suspicious. The dead command numbered 81.

That paper trail weakens the boast legend, but it does not clear Fetterman.

Brown is there too, the quartermaster, already ordered away from the post, and Frances later says he wanted one more chance before leaving. Grummond is another hard case. Historian Shannon D. Smith, reading his military record, found reckless conduct, drinking, and a court-martial in his past.

So inside the fort there is more than one pressure. Ambition. Frustration. Recent losses. A belief among some officers that bold action can restore control.

Then December 6 comes.

## The rehearsal
December 6 comes fifteen days before the Fetterman Fight, with fewer men and a shorter fight.

But it teaches the wrong people the right lesson.

Fetterman's own report says about 14 men held off about 100 warriors after most of the cavalry fled. Lieutenant Horatio Bingham dies. Sergeant Bowers dies. One sergeant and 4 privates are wounded, according to General Cooke's December 27 summary.

That is a hard little fight. But compared with December 21, it looks almost like a warning shot.

Margaret Carrington's journal entry for that day complicates the later case against Fetterman. She records that "Captain Fetterman has been in, and says 'he has learned a lesson, and that this Indian war has become a hand-to-hand fight, requiring the utmost caution.'"

That sentence matters.

The same Carrington circle that later blamed Fetterman also preserved him saying he had learned caution.

Grummond nearly dies that day too. Frances later writes that he is cut off with 3 men and rides for his life, with a spear at his back. She is inside the fort, and the image stays with her.

"No sleep came to my weary eyes, except fitfully, for many nights, and even then in my dreams I could see him riding madly from me with the Indians in pursuit."

Frances wasn't recording tactics there. She was describing fear inside the fort after nearly losing her husband.

But outside the fort, another lesson is being learned. The Wyoming State Historic Site summarizes it plainly. Warriors learned that if a large command could be lured over Lodge Trail Ridge, it could be wiped out beyond the fort's view.

Fifteen days later, that lesson will matter more than any boast.

The December 6 fight warned the fort. It frightened Frances. It showed the warriors how the ridge could be used.

The spear in Frances's dream would come back in another form. This time, the riders would not come back over the ridge.

## The other camp
The people waiting beyond the ridge are not a single army gathered from nowhere. They are camps along the Tongue River, including Oglala and Minneconjou Lakota, some Brulé, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho, according to the Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site and George Bird Grinnell.

Their reason for fighting is the one the federal commission later put on paper: the road and the forts ran straight across their last good hunting country.

One witness was White Elk, a Cheyenne who was about 16 to 18 years old in 1866. Nearly 48 years later, Grinnell walked the battlefield with him and recorded what he remembered. Grinnell's account comes from later oral history, memory, and translation, so it has to be weighed differently from army paperwork. Other Native accounts were recorded in similar ways, including those gathered by Charles Eastman.

White Elk remembered a holy person riding out to seek enemies and volunteers. Three times the rider came back and offered enemies in the hand, and three times the chiefs refused. On the fourth return, White Elk said, the rider fell from the horse with both hands on the ground. "Answer me quickly," he said, "I have a hundred or more."

That tradition gave the battle names such as the Battle of the Hundred in the Hand, or Hundred Slain. The sources do not agree on the holy person's name.

The plan was practical. A small party would draw soldiers away from the stockade. If that failed, the camps might attack the post itself. Grinnell describes 10 decoys, including two Cheyennes, two Arapahos, and two men from each of three Lakota tribes.

The Cheyenne decoys included Little Wolf and Wolf Left Hand. Little Wolf reportedly offered his place to his brother, Big Nose. Lakota accounts recorded decades later place the young Oglala Crazy Horse among the decoys, but White Elk does not name him, and no army record from 1866 or 1867 confirms his presence.

Red Cloud led the war, but his presence at this ambush remains disputed. Cheyenne witnesses told Grinnell that Red Cloud was not there under that name. Other accounts placed him at the fight.

Eastman later printed a Lakota memory of Red Cloud's warning: "You have heard the sound of the white soldier's ax upon the Little Piney. His presence here is an insult and a threat." Eastman's printed version is a later record, not a stenographic transcript from the council, and it preserves the position the camps understood.

The plan still needed a target outside the stockade. On the morning of December 21, the target was late.

## The late train
It is between 10 and 11 in the morning on December 21, 1866, and the season's last wood train is leaving Fort Phil Kearny late. Margaret Carrington and Frances Grummond both remembered roughly 90 armed men connected with the train.

The fort needs that wood to cook food, heat quarters, and survive the winter. The pinery is 5 or 6 miles out, and that distance turns every loaded wagon into a slow target long before any relief party can reach it. Every trip sends wagons and guards into country where hostile parties have already appeared 51 times since July 26.

At about 11 o'clock, the signal station on Pilot Hill reports many Indians. Carrington's official report says the wood train forms a corral about a mile and a half from the fort. The wagons stop because the danger is visible now.

Captain James Powell is first offered command of the relief party. Captain William Fetterman claims it by seniority. Lieutenant George Grummond asks for the cavalry. Captain Frederick Brown joins, along with two civilian employees, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher.

The command that leaves contains 81 men. Three officers. 76 enlisted soldiers. Two civilians. Its orders are already written. Support the train, relieve it, report back, do not pursue, and under no circumstances cross Lodge Trail Ridge.

According to Carrington, Lieutenant Wands repeats the order before the column moves. Carrington separately tells Grummond to obey orders and not leave Fetterman. The boundary is not vague. It is the central fact of the mission.

About 49 infantrymen mainly carry single-shot .58 caliber Springfield rifle-muskets. A trained soldier might fire 2 to 4 rounds a minute, with WyoHistory giving 3 rounds a minute at most. Each shot takes a paper cartridge, a ramrod, and time.

Twenty-seven men from Company C, 2nd Cavalry, probably carry seven-shot Spencer carbines, though Cyrus Townsend Brady wrote in 1904 that many of these recruits still had muskets. Wheatley and Fisher carry 16-shot Henry rifles. With cover and ammunition, those weapons can fire far faster than muzzle-loaders.

But ammunition is thin. Frances remembered there was "not a cartridge box full to every man." Carrington wrote in 1908 that he got 1,000 rounds at Laramie instead of the 20,000 promised.

The warriors carry mostly bows, lances, war clubs, shields, and robes. The State Historic Site estimates about 1 in 10 has a firearm. White Elk remembered that most carried bows, while a few had old smooth-bore flintlocks.

Range, reload speed, cover, ammunition, and spacing are about to decide what courage cannot solve by itself.

Near the Big Piney crossing, Carrington says a howitzer fires case shot and flushes nearly 30 hidden warriors from cover. The column has found the enemy. Now it must choose where to go.

## Over Lodge Trail
Shortly before noon, the command is on the crest of Lodge Trail Ridge. The fort is behind you. The wood train lies west. The decoys are ahead and below, moving across ground the fort cannot see.

Fetterman's command goes northeast toward the ridge, not west toward the wagons. That is the reading of the map, Carrington's reports, the Special Commission, and historian Shannon D. Smith.

Smith argues that Grummond's cavalry races ahead, with Fetterman following to support it. The exact decision inside the column cannot be proved. No soldier from the command survives to describe those minutes.

The result is clear. The mission is no longer simply moving toward a wood train. The terrain is pulling the command apart.

At the end of the ridge, the decoys work across the creek. White Elk told Grinnell that they split and crossed paths. That movement becomes the signal.

The charge follows. White Elk remembered, "When the charge was made the sound of many hoofs made a noise like thunder and the soldiers began to fall back."

The ground slopes down, icy and uneven. Another ridge blocks the fort. Warriors are hidden on both sides. There is no direct line from the stockade to the fight, no clear view for officers on the walls, and no quick way to know which men are still together.

Big Nose, the Cheyenne decoy, rides back and forth in front of the soldiers' guns. White Elk's account presents the movement as deliberate, drawing fire while the larger force waits beyond sight.

Mounted men can move faster than infantry. That advantage becomes dangerous once the horsemen pass beyond supporting distance. A rifleman on foot cannot instantly cross a creek, climb an icy slope, and protect cavalry already surrounded.

White Elk remembered arrows flying so thickly above the troops that they seemed "like a lot of grasshoppers flying across each other." Margaret Carrington wrote that arrows taken from a quiver and discharged one after another could produce a faster fire than a revolver.

The trap is dangerous for the attackers too. White Elk said arrows from both sides struck warriors as the lines closed.

By now, the command has separated into positions that cannot support one another quickly. Wheatley and Fisher move toward rocks at the far end. Grummond is on the road between them and the main body. Fetterman and Brown remain with most of the infantry among rocks nearer the fort.

The fort can hear the battle, but it still cannot see the field. That makes the next half hour a race between sound and relief.

## Shots beyond sight
At noon, Carrington hears the battle before he can see it. His official report says, "At twelve o'clock firing was heard toward Peno Creek, beyond Lodge Trail Ridge. A few shots were followed by constant shots, not to be counted."

The fort is only a few miles away, but Lodge Trail Ridge blocks the field. Information reaches Fort Phil Kearny as sound. Somewhere beyond that rise, 81 men are fighting, and nobody inside the stockade knows if it is a skirmish, a retreat, or a command already ringed in.

About 12 minutes after the firing begins, Captain Ten Eyck moves out with a relief force and wagons behind him. The number varies by account, from roughly 50 to 76 men. The Special Commission later said Ten Eyck followed a longer route. Carrington defended the decision. He was not sitting still while Fetterman's command died.

But the clock is already gone. Accounts put the fight itself at about 20 to 45 minutes. If Ten Eyck leaves after 12 minutes, and comes by the longer road, he is riding toward ground that may already be quiet.

Frances Grummond heard the pattern from the fort. In her 1910 account, there are quick volleys, scattered shots, and then silence. Less than half an hour had passed. Margaret Carrington's 1868 account gives the same shape, heavy firing, quick volleys, file firing, scattered shots, and perfect silence in a little more than 30 minutes.

Decoys pull the column forward. Cavalry separates from infantry. Men look for rocks and folds in the ground. Muzzle-loaders slow to two or three shots a minute. Ammunition thins. Mounted warriors close the distance. The relief force moves, but the battle is ending faster than help can arrive.

Near the rocks, Wheatley and Fisher leave the evidence of their 16-shot Henry rifles. Margaret Carrington wrote that they were found surrounded by spent cartridges. Carrington said the shells showed how hard they had fought.

Bugler Adolph Metzger fought to the last. American Horse later said the warriors covered his body with a buffalo robe to honor him.

Fetterman and Brown's final moments remain disputed. Carrington and the Commission said they shot each other. American Horse later claimed he killed Fetterman, in an account given to Captain James H. Cook and printed by Grace Raymond Hebard in 1922.

White Elk remembered someone saying, "All are dead but the dog; let him carry the news to the fort." Another voice answered, "No, do not let even a dog get away."

When the shooting stops, the fort still doesn't know whether anyone is alive.

## The empty field
Around 12:45 p.m., according to the Special Commission's timing, Ten Eyck reaches the summit. Below him, the valley is occupied by Native warriors calling for the relief force to come down.

The last shots have stopped. The fight is already over before the wagons reach the dead ground.

The timing is disputed, and every version changes the shape of the disaster. Grinnell placed the end near noon. Carrington's 1908 account reduced it to less than 21 minutes. Frances and Eastman remembered less than half an hour. Margaret Carrington and Carrington's 1867 report put it at a little more than 30 minutes. The Commission allowed half an hour or more. The safest conclusion is roughly 30 minutes, perhaps 40 or 45.

That matters because Ten Eyck's relief force is small, wagon-bound, and looking down at hundreds, perhaps more than a thousand, of the warriors who have just destroyed Fetterman's command. A charge downhill now would not recover the dead. It could add another command to them. So he waits on the summit until the warriors withdraw far enough for the bodies to be reached.

Their number is uncertain. Eastman gave 600 warriors in the ambush. The Special Commission estimated 1,500 to 1,800. General Cooke judged 1,500 to 3,000 probable, while Carrington's first telegram said nearly 3,000. Most modern estimates fall between one and two thousand.

Below the ridge lie all 81 men from Fetterman's command. Three officers, 76 enlisted soldiers, and two civilians are dead. The field holds rocks, spent cartridge shells, abandoned positions, dead ponies, snow, and bare ground. Wagons wait for men who cannot walk back.

The dead are stripped and mutilated. Historian Shannon D. Smith and the State Historic Site link that both to Plains war custom and to revenge remembered after Sand Creek.

Native losses cannot be fixed. Carrington counted 65 pools of blood in about an acre and nine or ten dead ponies, but he could not know the final number. Native sources usually name between a dozen and twenty dead. White Elk, a Cheyenne witness, remembered far more: two Cheyennes, and Lakota dead laid in two rows of perhaps fifty or sixty.

At the fort, the battle is still rumor until the wagons arrive at dusk. Ten Eyck has found the command, but he has not saved it.

## Wagons at dusk
By dusk on December 21, the women gather inside Mrs. Wands' cabin. Frances later wrote, "The ladies clustered in Mrs. Wands' cabin as night drew on, all speechless from absolute stagnation and terror. Then the crunching of wagon wheels startled us to our feet. The gates opened."

Ten Eyck's wagons bring back 49 bodies and the news that probably no one has survived. Identification is slow and uncertain. Carrington's telegram that evening reports, "I venture as much as any one can, but I have had to-day a fight unexampled in Indian warfare. My loss is ninety-four killed."

That number is wrong. Frances later corrected the loss to 81. The army's final count is Captain Fetterman, Captain Brown, Lieutenant Grummond, 49 enlisted infantrymen, 27 cavalrymen, and 2 civilian employees, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher.

John "Portugee" Phillips arrives that night and volunteers to carry dispatches to Fort Laramie. Frances remembered him saying he would go as a special messenger, even if it cost his life. He gives her his wolf robe before leaving.

The ride through the blizzard is real, and extraordinary. Fort Laramie is roughly 235 to 236 miles away by the winter route. But Phillips was not necessarily alone for the entire distance. Daniel Dixon also rode toward Fort Reno, and other riders joined at Reno, according to the Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site. The later legend makes one man cross the whole frozen country by himself. The record is harder, colder, and more practical.

The telegram leaves Horseshoe Station on Christmas Day. Phillips reaches Fort Laramie on Christmas night, while a dance is underway.

Inside the fort, Frances still doesn't have her husband's body. She has only the silence, the wagons, and the knowledge that George Grummond is probably among the dead.

For a few hours, the fort can only wait. That doesn't last.

## The second morning
Morning comes on December 22, and the fort is still under arms.

The snow and bare ground beyond the gate are no safer than they were at dusk. According to Frances Grummond's 1910 account, the officers meet in council and oppose another search, because the danger is immediate. The same force that destroyed 81 men may still be close enough to draw out another party.

Carrington decides to go anyway.

Frances remembered him coming to her with a plain promise: "Mrs. Grummond, I shall go in person, and will bring back to you the remains of your husband." Frances gives him a harder answer. "They are beyond all suffering now. You must not imperil other precious lives and make other women as miserable as myself." She is still waiting for George's body, but she asks Carrington not to risk more lives to get it.

Carrington goes out with about 80 men. This time the command reaches the field and recovers the remaining bodies, including George Grummond.

Frances later made one darker claim. She wrote that Carrington ordered that, if the post faced a last struggle, women and children should be put in the magazine and destroyed rather than captured. That appears in Frances's memory, but Margaret Carrington does not mention it, and the Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site treats the story cautiously.

What Frances does record with certainty is the packet returned to her. It held a lock of George's hair. She wrote, "He had redeemed his pledge!"

The burial takes days. Margaret and Frances describe numbered pine coffins, frozen ground, and a trench about 50 feet long and 7 feet deep, dug in short shifts and finished Wednesday, December 26. Carrington wrote that Fetterman, Brown, and Grummond were buried in one grave. Grummond's remains would later be taken home.

The fort is physically changed now. Eighty-one dead in one fight. Women widowed. Officers stunned. Winter tightening around the stockade.

Then Carrington was relieved, before the full facts could even arrive.

## Blame and paper
On December 26, before all the bodies have been buried and before any detailed report from Fort Phil Kearny can reach higher command, General Philip St. George Cooke relieves Carrington under Special Orders No. 126 and orders him to Fort Casper.

General Henry Wessells reaches the post on January 16, 1867, through snow and cold that Frances later described as 25 to 40 below zero. Cooke, writing on December 27, has already identified the weapons problem. Men who had used Spencer arms, he writes, "have since felt the inferiority of the muzzle-loading arms."

The 1867 Special Commission states the larger failure plainly: "The difficulty, 'in a nutshell,' was that the commanding officer of the district was furnished no more troops or supplies for this state of war than had been provided and furnished him for a state of profound peace."

Then comes the army's anger. On December 28, Sherman telegraphs Grant, "We must act with vindictive earnestness against the Sioux, even to their extermination, men, women and children." It's a brutal document of immediate reaction, not a verdict to admire.

The sources are loaded. Margaret Carrington's 1868 Ab-sa-ra-ka defended Henry Carrington and printed Commission material. Frances wrote in 1910, after marrying Carrington. Henry's January 3, 1867 report was withheld, then printed by the Senate in 1887.

Modern historians Shannon D. Smith and John H. Monnett argue that the reckless Fetterman story was shaped heavily by the Carrington side of the paper trail.

Fetterman crossed the forbidden ridge. But the fort was undermanned, undersupplied, dependent on wood trains, and armed largely with slow muzzle-loaders.

For Frances, blame is not the final scene. The final scene is a coffin leaving in a snowstorm.

## The coffin road
On January 23, 1867, Frances Grummond leaves Fort Phil Kearny with the Carringtons, 40 infantry, 20 cavalry, and her husband's coffin.

The road out is the same kind of road that made the fort necessary. Long miles. Bad weather. Animals breaking down. Men watching the ridges because the war has not stopped for mourning.

Frances later wrote, "In one sense, that slowly moving train was a funeral procession, for my husband's remains were placed in one wagon, that they might accompany me to my home in Tennessee for a resting place until the final Roll Call."

The first day, they make about 6 miles, according to Frances.

The first 6 miles show how slowly men, animals, and wagons moved in the January cold. Frances and the Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site describe mercury freezing, frostbite, and amputations later at Fort Reno. Carrington himself is wounded on the march when his revolver accidentally discharges and strikes him in the leg, according to Frances.

The war story has left the ridge now. But it has not ended.

It moves east in a wagon, inside a coffin, beside a pregnant widow who had watched her husband ride out on December 21 and had heard the battle end without seeing it. Frances reaches Tennessee in March 1867 after a journey of 7 weeks. A month later, a son is born. George Grummond is brought home to Tennessee to rest.

That is the return leg of the fight, a slow coffin road from the Powder River country to Tennessee, carrying one officer home and leaving 80 others behind.

But the army will soon learn the weapons lesson at two more fights.

In 8 months, soldiers behind wagon boxes would understand exactly what breech-loaders meant.

## What changed
It is August 1, 1867, near Fort C. F. Smith, and the same war has moved into another field. The Hayfield Fight begins there, with soldiers and civilians defending a cutting party north of the Bighorn.

One day later, on August 2, it comes back near Fort Phil Kearny.

This time, the position is a corral of wagon boxes. About 32 men are inside it, under Captain James Powell, facing hundreds of Lakota and Cheyenne warriors on open ground. But the weapons have changed.

Powell's men have Springfield Model 1866 Allin conversion rifles. Breech-loaders. A soldier opens the trapdoor at the breech, pushes in a cartridge, closes it, fires, and loads again without standing up to ram powder and ball down the muzzle.

That changes the mechanics of the fight.

At Fetterman's fight, many infantrymen had carried .58 caliber muzzle-loading Springfields. They were solid weapons, but slow under mounted pressure. Two to 4 shots a minute was practical. WyoHistory gives about 3 at most. The Fort Phil Kearny State Historic Site says the new breech-loaders let Powell's men fire much faster from cover.

Behind wagon boxes, a man with a breech-loader can stay low, open the trapdoor, load from cover, and fire again. He doesn't have to stand up to ram a muzzle-loader while mounted men are closing the distance.

The State Historic Site says more than 80 new rifles had reached the post. WyoHistory gives 700, so the number is contested. The effect is not. At the Wagon Box Fight, the position holds.

Even the dead are disputed. Native accounts counted 6 killed. Some white claims ran from 60 to 1,500, according to WyoHistory. That is why ranges matter. Smoke, distance, and conflicting reports left room for inflated casualty claims.

After the 1868 treaty requires the Bozeman Road closed, the forts are abandoned.

The Fort Laramie Treaty is first signed April 29, 1868. Article 16 requires the forts abandoned and the Bozeman Road closed within 90 days of peace. Red Cloud signs on November 6, after the posts are gone.

The last troops leave Fort Phil Kearny in late August 1868. The fort is burned, probably by Cheyennes under Little Wolf, according to the State Historic Site, WyoHistory, and George Bird Grinnell.

After the forts were abandoned, the fight continued in reports, memoirs, and later histories.

## The silence remains
The surviving record starts with a missing source, because no soldier from Fetterman's command lived to give the army side from inside the fight.

The army side comes from the fort, from sounds heard beyond a ridge, from the field afterward, from official reports, and from later defenses written by people who had something at stake.

Margaret Carrington published Ab-sa-ra-ka in 1868 to defend Henry Carrington. She died in 1870. Frances Grummond married Henry Carrington in 1871, lived later in Boston and Hyde Park, returned with him to Massacre Hill on July 3, 1908, published her account in 1910, and died in 1911.

Native accounts survived another way. They came through oral history and later interviews. Ricker took testimony in 1906 and 1907. Grinnell walked the field with White Elk about 1914. Eastman published accounts in 1918. Later works preserved memories from men named White Bull, Fire Thunder, American Horse, and others.

All of those surviving accounts are later, partial, or carried through someone who was not inside Fetterman's command. The one undisputed fact is that no soldier from that command survived to describe the last half hour.

The later legend made Fetterman a man who boasted, disobeyed, charged, and was destroyed. The record adds the harder machinery. A vulnerable fort, a contested road, a learned ambush, 10 decoys, a forbidden ridge, separated cavalry and infantry, slow-loading arms, thin ammunition, and delayed relief all converged in the same piece of ground.

So return to Frances at her door in the cold light. She cannot see the valley. She hears volleys. Then scattering shots. Then nothing.

Beyond Lodge Trail Ridge, every army witness who could explain the last minutes was killed.

Frances put it in one line: "Less than half an hour had passed, and the silence was dreadful."
