# The Economics of Dying in America

In one typical American case, dying leaves the family a $66,988 bill, and it looks like a funeral bill until you put the medical statements in order. In 2023, the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics recorded 3,090,964 U.S. deaths. The National Funeral Directors Association's 2023 median for a funeral with viewing and burial was $8,300. So why does the smaller bill get the fear? Because the larger bill arrives slowly. Mount Sinai found $38,688 in average out-of-pocket spending in the last five years of life; KFF found traditional Medicare's average last-year spending was $34,529. The cemetery is where the receipt gets stapled.

## The typical case
$66,988 is one constructed case, not a national average.

Follow a widowed retiree in Pennsylvania, late 70s, two adult children, living mostly on Social Security. There is a paid-off house worth about $400,000. There is a five-year health decline. The death happens in hospice. The funeral has a viewing and burial. The house passes through probate.

Put three objects on the table. First, Medicare statements and pharmacy receipts on a kitchen table. Second, a funeral home price list in a quiet office. Third, a courthouse file for the house.

This is general information, not personal legal, tax, medical, or financial advice. We are counting the family-facing tab. We will also show the public bill Medicare pays, and the nursing-home branch that can make the math explode.

The blank ledger has four lines: medical decline, hospice, funeral, probate. The first envelopes arrive years before the funeral home folder.

## Five slow years
Five years before the death certificate, the first bills are ordinary enough to miss.

Prescription receipts. Specialist copays. Dental work Medicare usually doesn't cover. Eyeglasses. A shower chair. A walker. Rides to appointments. A few paid hours of help at home when family can't cover Tuesday afternoon.

Amy Kelley and colleagues at Mount Sinai, writing in the Journal of General Internal Medicine, studied Health and Retirement Study data on 3,209 Medicare beneficiaries from 2002 to 2008. They found out-of-pocket health spending in the last five years of life averaged $38,688 per person.

Call that about $7,700 a year. About $640 a month. Before the funeral home ever opens a folder.

The tail is worse. A quarter of households spent an average of $101,791, and a quarter spent more than their total household assets. Dementia cost the most, more than twice the average for cancer or gastrointestinal disease.

Last five years of out-of-pocket health care, $38,688. Running total: $38,688.

Pay attention to who pays this next bill, because it explains why the family can feel broke even when Medicare is spending heavily.

## Medicare's invisible bill
Now draw two columns.

On the left is what the family pays. On the right is what Medicare pays. The Medicare column is real spending. The family column is the one that usually lands on the kitchen table.

KFF analyzed 2014 traditional Medicare data and found Medicare spent $34,529 on average for each traditional Medicare beneficiary in the last year of life. For beneficiaries who survived the year, the average was $9,121. So the last-year number was nearly four times as high.

KFF also found the 4% of beneficiaries who died accounted for 13.5% of traditional Medicare spending. That is a lot of hospital rooms, doctors, labs, drugs, imaging, and claims passing through the system.

The family often never sees that full number. It arrives as explanations of benefits, adjustments, denied lines, approved lines, and small balances that somehow still need stamps.

This $34,529 is real spending, but it is not added to our family-facing tab. Medicare can pay $34,529 in claims while a separate walker bill still lands on the kitchen table.

So far, the kitchen table has $38,688 on it. Running total: $38,688.

The next bill comes from daily care, and it can pull the house into the math.

## The care gap
Custodial care means help with the basic day. Bathing. Dressing. Eating. Toileting. Getting from the bed to the chair without falling.

Here is the rule people usually learn too late. Medicare does not pay for long-term custodial care in a nursing home. Medicare can cover short skilled-nursing stays after a qualifying hospital stay, under specific rules. That is different.

Now open the dementia branch, but don't add it to our running tab. CareScout and the Genworth Cost of Care Survey put the 2025 national median cost of a private nursing-home room at $129,575 a year.

One year is more than three times the $38,688 five-year out-of-pocket medical line. One year.

Our typical case dies in hospice without a full private-pay nursing-home year, so we don't add it. But if Medicaid enters later, the house can become part of the recovery math instead of simple inheritance.

If this is already changing how you see the death bill, subscribe, because the next bill is worse.

And that is when families hear the asset question.

## Medicaid and the house
Medicaid, in this context, is the joint federal and state program that can pay for long-term care, but only after the person meets the financial rules.

Before Medicaid pays, the person usually has to spend down assets under state rules. Medicaid.gov describes a 5-year, or 60-month, look-back on gifts and transfers, with state rules still controlling the details. Giving the house away right before care is not a magic eraser. The look-back rule is why the transfer date matters.

After death, states must try to recover what Medicaid paid for long-term care from the estate. That can include the house.

So in our Pennsylvania case, the paid-off $400,000 house can be part of the estate Medicaid looks to for recovery.

We are not adding the dementia branch or Medicaid branch to the official tab. But the alternate path is vivid: $129,575 for one private nursing-home year before Medicaid even starts, then possible recovery after death. A paid-off house can still be part of estate recovery or probate costs.

Our case has not added nursing-home care. Running total: $38,688.

Once doctors certify the final stage, a different clock starts, and this one changes who pays.

## Hospice twist
Hospice means care focused on comfort when curing the illness is no longer the goal under Medicare's hospice rules. The center of the bill changes. Hospice usually shifts the bill toward comfort care, pain control, nursing visits, equipment, and caregiver support.

Under Medicare hospice, most people pay nothing for hospice care itself. The small charges are narrow. Medicare says there can be about a $5 copay for each prescription used for pain and symptom control. There can also be up to 5% coinsurance for inpatient respite care, which is short-term care meant to give the caregiver a break.

For our Pennsylvania widow, hospice is the final setting. So for this constructed family-facing tab, the hospice line is $0.

That is the quiet surprise. Hospice can lower the final medical bill for the family, but it doesn't erase the five years of earlier out-of-pocket costs.

Hospice, $0. Running total: $38,688.

After hospice, the medical statements slow down. But the funeral home price list comes out.

## The visible funeral
Now the folder moves from the hospice room to the funeral home desk. This is the bill people recognize. A director, a casket room, a printed price list, and a family choosing while tired.

The National Funeral Directors Association put the 2023 median cost of a funeral with viewing and burial at $8,300. Write this one down, because that median does not include the cemetery plot, opening and closing the grave, the vault, the headstone or marker, or cash advance items paid to outside vendors.

Funeral with viewing and burial, $8,300. Running total: $46,988.

Cremation changes the math for many families. The NFDA 2025 Cremation and Burial Report projects cremation at 63.4% of U.S. deaths in 2025, compared with 31.6% for burial. In 2023, the NFDA median for a funeral with viewing and cremation was $6,280.

That is a $2,020 difference before cemetery extras. Our case still uses burial.

This is the bill everyone can picture, which is partly why it gets blamed.

## Price list rules
The detail people miss is that the funeral home has rules, and some of them actually protect the buyer.

The FTC Funeral Rule says a funeral home must give you an itemized general price list. It must show casket prices before showing caskets. It must also accept a casket bought somewhere else without charging a handling fee.

The rules mainly help the family read the bill before signing it. That matters, because the casket room is one of the few places where the price tag has to appear before the sales tour does its work.

Cremation is also a legal choice many families use. Again, the NFDA projects cremation at 63.4% of U.S. deaths in 2025. But we are not applying that saving here, because our typical case uses the NFDA median burial package.

The medical decline is $38,688, hospice is $0, funeral is $8,300, and the tab is still $46,988.

The funeral receipt is not the last paper in the folder. County courthouse, probate file.

## Probate eats value
Probate is the court process that settles an estate, pays debts, transfers property, and gives legal authority to the right person. It is paperwork with a judge nearby. That tends to affect the price.

In our Pennsylvania case, the $400,000 paid-off house goes through probate. Estate lawyers commonly put probate at 3% to 7% of the estate in court, attorney and executor fees, and it varies by state.

For this case, we use 5% of the $400,000 house. That is $20,000.

Probate on the $400,000 house at 5%, $20,000. Running total: $66,988.

For this case, probate cost matters far more than federal estate tax. The IRS says the federal estate tax basic exclusion amount is $15 million per person in 2026, and the Tax Policy Center estimates about 0.1% of deaths, roughly 1 in 1,000, produce any federal estate tax.

For ordinary families, the problem is usually court cost, delay, debt, and paperwork. The $400,000 house now sits inside the probate file, where creditors make claims, court fees come off the top, and heirs wait for what's left.

The final number has arrived. But two myths still decide who carries it.

## The final ledger
The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says family members, including spouses, are generally not personally responsible for a dead person's debts unless they co-signed, held a joint account, or a state rule applies. The catch is that debts are paid from the estate first. That shrinks what heirs receive.

Life insurance can change the cash problem. LIMRA's 2025 Insurance Barometer says 51% of U.S. adults say they own a life insurance policy. When it exists and is payable to beneficiaries, it can create cash without forcing the family to sell the house. But not everyone has it, and the amount varies.

Who benefits is reasonably clear: hospitals, doctors, drug providers, labs, nursing facilities in the branch case, funeral homes, cemeteries, attorneys, courts, executors, and sometimes state Medicaid programs through estate recovery.

Who carries the risk is just as clear: the dying person first, then the estate, then heirs through reduced inheritance, and taxpayers through Medicare and Medicaid spending.
