# The Economics of a Nursing Home

A nursing home bill can hit $233,856 before Medicaid asks about the house. That's a two-year tab for an 82-year-old widow: a $1,736 hospital deductible from CMS, ten copay days at $217 each, and two years in a semi-private room at CareScout's 2025 median price, $315 a day. About 1.2 million live in nursing homes, according to KFF's 2025 analysis. Families see Medicare as the safety net. But Medicare covers rehab, not long-term help. That switch is the machine. Five stops: hospital, rehab, private pay, Medicaid, estate recovery. Each handoff adds a line. Somebody is already adding it up.

## The case file
Put one case on the board. An unnamed 82-year-old widow, traditional Medicare, no supplemental coverage, a paid-off house, $194,000 in savings, and Social Security of $1,919 a month. This is a constructed example using real public figures. State rules vary. This is general information, not legal or financial advice. KFF reported in 2023 that 13% of people in traditional Medicare, about 3.5 million people, had no supplemental coverage. So she is not rare. First objects: a hospital bracelet, a rehab calendar, a bank statement showing $194,000, and a house key. The CDC says more than 1 in 4 adults 65 and older fall each year, and nearly 319,000 older people are hospitalized for hip fractures, mostly after falls. She falls, breaks a hip, spends 4 days admitted, and moves to rehab. The bank statement looks strong on day one. By month 25, that bank statement is the number Medicaid checks.

## The hospital entry
The first switch happens at the hospital door. Medicare Part A is hospital insurance, but insurance does not mean free. CMS sets the 2026 inpatient hospital deductible at $1,736 for each benefit period. That period uses Medicare's clock, separate from the calendar. A benefit period starts when she's admitted and ends after 60 days out of inpatient care. She was admitted for 4 days, and that word matters. Medicare.gov says a skilled nursing facility stay usually requires at least 3 inpatient hospital days in a row. Time under observation, or time in the ER, does not count toward those 3 days. The family sees a hospital bed, a wristband, a nurse, and a tray of mystery soup, and assumes it counts. It may not, unless the status says inpatient. The question to ask is simple: "Am I an inpatient or under observation?" We will come back to that. For this case, she clears the 3-day door. Hospital stay, Medicare Part A deductible, CMS 2026: $1,736. Running total: $1,736.

## The rehab window
Now she enters a skilled nursing facility. Skilled nursing facility care means daily skilled care after a qualifying hospital stay, such as physical therapy and nursing care after a hip fracture. Medicare.gov says she must enter the facility within about 30 days and need daily skilled care. Under CMS 2026 cost-sharing, days 1 through 20 cost the patient $0. This is where the myth begins. Medicare does not simply cover a nursing home for 100 days. It can cover up to 100 days, only while daily skilled care is needed. MedPAC reported that the average Medicare-covered skilled nursing stay was 30.7 days in 2024. And in 2023 claims, MedPAC found more stays ended on day 20, 3.6%, than on day 19 or day 21, about 2.5%. Right before the copay starts. Rehab days 1 to 20, Medicare pays in full: $0. Running total: $1,736.

## The copay cliff
Move the rehab calendar from day 20 to day 21. She is in the same hallway and the same bed, but the bill has moved to a new column. CMS says that in 2026, skilled nursing facility days 21 through 100 cost $217 a day for the patient. Medicare calls it coinsurance, which is the patient's share after Medicare pays its part. In this constructed case, she stays through day 30. That creates 10 copay days. Ten days at $217 is $2,170. Write that one down, because a supplement changes it. Medicare.gov says Medigap plans C, D, F, G, M, and N pay the full skilled nursing facility copay. If she had one of those, this $2,170 line would be $0. But she has no supplement, like the 13% of traditional Medicare beneficiaries KFF counted in 2023. The room did not get nicer on day 21. The therapy gym did not add marble floors. The care is the same, but the patient's share starts on day 21. Rehab days 21 to 30, 10 days at $217, CMS 2026 copay: $2,170. Running total: $3,906.

## Custodial care
On day 31, the bigger switch arrives. Rehab stops because she no longer needs daily skilled care, but she also cannot live alone. Custodial care means help bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring, and getting safely through the day. Medicare.gov says Medicare and Medigap do not pay for long-term custodial care when that is all you need. There is one important correction here. CMS made clear after the Jimmo case that the test is the need for skilled care. Improvement is not required under that standard. So the old line, "Medicare stops because you stop improving," is wrong. This problem is different. She no longer qualifies for daily skilled care, so the care has become long-term custodial care. The hospital bracelet comes off. The rehab calendar ends. The private-pay invoice starts. The family is paying because an 82-year-old widow cannot safely go home alone after a hip fracture. Pay attention to this next detail, because it explains everything.

## Private pay begins
The question is simple. What does the nursing home cost once insurance is gone? CareScout, formerly Genworth, reported in its 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released in March 2026, a national median of $315 a day for a semi-private room. That is $114,975 for a year. Prices vary sharply by state, but this tab uses the national median so the arithmetic stays clean.

At $315 a day, 365 days costs $9,581.25 a month. Social Security reported in 2026 that the average benefit for a widow living alone is about $1,919 a month. Medicare Part B usually comes out of that check. The CMS 2026 premium is $202.90, leaving $1,716.10.

So the monthly gap is $9,581.25 minus $1,716.10, or $7,865.15 from savings. Write that number down. $7,865.15 every month. At that pace, savings fall by $7,865.15 every month.

Line item: Day 31, Medicare stops; year one of custodial care, 365 days at $315, CareScout median semi-private: $114,975. Running total: $118,881.

How long can $194,000 survive that burn rate?

## The second year
At the end of the first year, Social Security is still covering part of the room bill, and savings are covering the rest. We aren't adding inflation here. The tab uses today's CareScout median for both years, so you can see the math.

The original $194,000 in savings has already lost $94,381.80 after Social Security pays its share. The second year repeats the burn rate. Another 365 days at $315 is another $114,975.

That moves the case from the $3,906 rehab bill to a much larger number. But the stay itself is not made up. ASPE and HHS reported in 2019 that among people who reach 65, about 28% spend at least 90 days in a nursing home. Half of those long-stay residents spend two years or less there.

Kelly and colleagues, in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society in 2010, found a median stay of 5 months among residents who died. One in four stayed 20 months or more.

Line item: year two, same room at today's price, $114,975. Running total: $233,856.

How does the bill reach $233,856? The next question is why Medicaid begins before the house is sold.

## The spend-down
The savings check is blunt. A month costs $9,581.25. Social Security after the Part B premium covers $1,716.10. Savings cover $7,865.15.

Over 24 months, the math is $194,000 minus $3,906 minus $188,763.60, which equals $1,330.40. That is under $2,000, so in most states Medicaid can start in month 25. KFF reported in April 2026 that most states cap a nursing home applicant's countable savings at $2,000. California is different: DHCS says its Medi-Cal asset limit ended January 1, 2024.

Once Medicaid starts, almost all income goes toward care. KFF found a median personal-needs allowance of $70 a month in 2026. Federal rules set a floor of $30. Once Medicaid starts, her monthly check still gets routed toward care, except for the personal-needs allowance.

After 24 months, Social Security paid $41,186.40. Savings paid $192,669.60. The tab still excludes doctors' Part B bills, house costs, and price increases. Running total: $233,856.

Why does Medicaid begin only after savings are nearly gone? The house key comes back now.

## The house question
Medicaid usually doesn't take the house on the day someone enrolls. CMS says in 2026 the home is generally excluded when its equity is under $752,000. States may allow up to $1,130,000. If a spouse still lives there, CMS says that spouse may keep $32,532 to $162,660 in assets, depending on the state.

The later risk is estate recovery. Medicaid.gov says states must try to recover nursing home costs from the estate of enrollees age 55 and older. That can include the house. Recovery generally can't move forward while a spouse, a child under 21, or a disabled child survives. Hardship waivers may apply. Lien rules vary by state.

Then comes the five-year look-back. Under CMS rules tied to the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, Medicaid can review 60 months of gifts and transfers. A gift can create a penalty period. That's why elder-law attorneys exist, but this isn't legal advice.

Medicaid enrollment usually doesn't take the house right away, but estate recovery can bring the house back into the bill later. If Medicaid pays for most residents, why are private bills so high? The payer mix explains the building.

## Who gets paid
Look at the building as a whole. KFF reported in July 2025 that 1.2 million people lived in nursing homes. Medicaid was the main payer for 63%, Medicare for 14%, and private or other payers for 23%. KFF found that 73% of nursing homes were for-profit.

The incentives differ by payer. Medicare rehab is short, technical, and pays well. Medicaid long-term care is the largest payer, but its rates are lower. Private payers face the posted room price. One bed, three price logics.

MedPAC reported in March 2026 that nursing homes earned a 24.4% margin on Medicare patients in 2024. That margin stayed above 10% for 25 years. On everything else, MedPAC reported a negative 2.3% margin. MACPAC found that median Medicaid payment covered 86% of nursing home costs in 2019.

The system creates the spread. The room has one mattress and several business models. So why does the private payer get hit with the posted price? Because somebody has to cover the gap. And somehow, the alternatives aren't cheap either.

## The other options
Assisted living sounds like the escape hatch. CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey puts the national median at $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year. That's cheaper than the nursing home, but Medicare still doesn't pay for long-term custodial care because the lobby has nicer chairs.

Home care runs into the same wall. CareScout puts a home caregiver at $35 an hour. At 44 hours a week, that's $80,080 a year. And that still isn't 24-hour care.

A private nursing home room is worse. CareScout's 2025 median is $355 a day, or $129,575 a year. Two years is $259,150. That's $29,200 more than our semi-private room.

Insurance can help, if you bought it early enough. AALTCI's 2026 comparison says a 55-year-old woman pays about $1,500 a year for a flat $165,000 benefit. That covers about 524 days at $315 a day, roughly 17 months. Milliman and NAIC data show about 5.8 million people had stand-alone coverage at the end of 2024.

Running total stays $233,856. If that sounds bad, keep watching, because the useful part is smaller but real.

## The fewer-bad moves
These are general legal moves people use to reduce surprises. They are not personal financial or legal advice.

First, in the hospital, ask, "Am I an inpatient or under observation?" Medicare.gov's 3-day rule requires 3 inpatient days before skilled nursing coverage. The word inpatient on the chart is what matters for the three-day rule.

Second, check the Medigap policy. If our widow had plan G, or C, D, F, M, or N, Medicare.gov says the $2,170 skilled nursing copay would have been $0.

Third, use SHIP, the free Medicare counseling network from the federal Administration for Community Living at shiphelp.org.

Fourth, don't sign as a personal guarantor. Federal rule 42 CFR 483.15 says a nursing home may not require a relative to personally guarantee payment.

Fifth, call Medicaid before savings run out. KFF says the 2025 federal budget law cuts retroactive Medicaid for older adults from 3 months before application to 2 months starting January 1, 2027.

Sixth, understand the insurance clock. Long-term care insurance is usually bought decades before need, not from the rehab hallway.

The insurance decision had to be made years earlier. By the rehab hallway, the useful window had mostly closed.

## The final ledger
Short-term Medicare rehab can work. It works when the patient qualifies, needs skilled care, improves or maintains function, and leaves before private pay starts.

The math breaks when care becomes custodial. That is when health insurance stops looking like a long-term safety net.

Here is the final bill. Hospital stay, $1,736. Rehab days 1 to 20, $0. Rehab days 21 to 30, $2,170. Year one custodial care, $114,975. Year two custodial care, $114,975.

Total, $233,856.

That leaves out doctors' Part B bills, house costs, and price increases. So the clean tab is already almost a quarter of a million dollars before the extra envelopes.

Who benefits is clear enough. The hospital, the rehab facility, the nursing home, and later Medicaid, after the savings are almost gone.

Who carries the risk is just as clear. The widow first, then her savings, then Medicaid, then possibly her estate.

The family saw it in pieces, one handoff at a time.
