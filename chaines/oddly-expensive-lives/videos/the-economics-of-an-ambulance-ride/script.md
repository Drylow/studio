# The Economics of an Ambulance Ride

A 5-mile 911 ambulance ride can start as a public rescue and end as a $1,899 patient tab. KFF and Peterson-KFF estimate that ambulances take about 3 million privately insured patients to emergency rooms each year, and about half of emergency ground rides include an out-of-network charge. You picture the fire department, a siren, and a city service. But the bill can behave like an out-of-network medical claim. The price turns on ownership, insurance contracts, deductibles, and state law more than distance. The strange part is that the federal No Surprises Act protects air ambulances, but not ground ambulances. Somebody is already adding it up.

## The 911 call
Take a constructed case, using real rounded figures from the sources on the board. A 40-year-old with employer health insurance gets chest pain at night, turns on the kitchen light, puts 911 on speaker, and rides 5 miles to the emergency room. The insurance card is in a wallet on the counter. Nobody is comparing networks while paramedics are at the door. An emergency ambulance is dispatched by the local 911 system, but the bill can come from a city agency, a fire department, a hospital-linked service, or a private ambulance company. KFF and Peterson-KFF found that local fire and rescue departments and other government agencies run nearly two thirds of privately insured emergency ground ambulance rides. That is the first surprise. Public-looking service does not always mean tax-funded service at the point of use. In a real emergency, you call 911 first and fight the bill later. On the board, keep three things: the siren, the insurance card, and the state map.

## The base rate
The first number is the base rate. That means the starting charge, or allowed payment, for showing up with a staffed ambulance, equipment, medical supplies, and emergency transport. Health Care Cost Institute data from 2022 says that for employer-sponsored insurance, a typical commercial ground ambulance trip paid a $718 base rate. Ambulance base rate at the typical commercial price, HCCI: $718. Running total: $718. Before mileage, before the ER, before anyone knows the diagnosis, the tab is already close to a month of groceries for many households. The money goes to the ambulance provider. That could be a city department, a fire-rescue agency, a hospital-affiliated ambulance, or a private company. The quiet twist is that the payment is attached to the transport, not to whether the chest pain becomes a heart attack. The meter starts before anyone knows if the emergency is medical catastrophe or very expensive indigestion. Keep $718 on the board, because Medicare prices the same ride very differently.

## The mileage line
Ambulance mileage is the per-mile amount charged for the transport distance, usually from pickup to the hospital. Health Care Cost Institute data from 2022 says the average employer-insurance ambulance trip is about 5 miles. The same HCCI data puts typical commercial ambulance mileage at about $17 per mile. For this case, 5 miles at $17 a mile is $85. Mileage, 5 miles at $17 a mile, HCCI: $85. Running total: $803. That $85 is the small line on the invoice, but it is also the only part that looks like a taxi meter. The strange lesson is that distance is not the main reason the bill is large. The base rate and the network label matter more. Medicare pays about $8 a mile, according to HCCI, less than half the commercial mileage rate. And because this patient has not met the deductible yet, the allowed amount can land on the patient before insurance feels useful.

## The network trap
In network means the provider has a contract with the insurer for a negotiated price. Out of network means the provider does not have that contract, so the insurer may pay less, and the provider may bill the patient for the difference where allowed. KFF and Peterson-KFF estimate that about half of emergency ground ambulance rides for privately insured people involve an out-of-network charge. FAIR Health private claims data found that in 2022, 62.0% of emergency ground ambulance services were out of network, down from 68.3% in 2018. Health Affairs researchers, Chhabra and colleagues, looked at about 1.5 million ambulance transports in 2020 and found that 71% involved a potential surprise bill. The median potential surprise bill for a ground ambulance was $450. Put our patient in a state with no ground ambulance surprise-billing law. That means the out-of-network balance bill is generally still legal. Out-of-network balance bill, median potential surprise bill, Health Affairs: $450. Running total: $1,253. ## The law gap
The No Surprises Act is the federal law that, since 2022, limits many surprise out-of-network bills in emergencies. The exception is ground ambulances. The federal No Surprises Act protects patients from surprise bills for air ambulances, but not for ground ambulances. For ground ambulances, Congress created a federal advisory committee to study the billing problem. That is government for "we see the fire, and we're making a chart." Commonwealth Fund and Georgetown CHIR reported in 2026 that about 22 to 24 states have some protection from surprise ground ambulance bills. In the other states, out-of-network ground ambulance balance bills are generally still legal. Our constructed patient is on the wrong side of the map: no state protection, out-of-network ambulance, deductible not met. So far: the base rate, the mileage, and the out-of-network balance bill. Running total: $1,253. The siren sounds the same on both sides of a state line, but the bill does not. And now the stretcher reaches a second billing system.

## The ER bill
The stretcher rolls from the ambulance bay to emergency registration. A wristband goes on, a monitor starts beeping, and an intake form asks the questions again. The insurance card leaves the wallet and gets scanned again.

The ambulance company and the emergency department can bill separately. Peterson-KFF's 2019 analysis of large-employer claims found that patients paid an average of $646 out of pocket for an emergency department visit.

The ER visit out of pocket, Peterson-KFF average: $646. Running total: $1,899.

That's the grand number for our constructed case, a five-mile emergency ride and ER visit built from real rounded averages and medians. Write down the deductible. KFF's 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey put the average single-coverage deductible at $1,886. KFF also reported that 34 percent of covered workers had a deductible of $2,000 or more.

A deductible is the amount you usually pay before insurance starts paying many covered costs. Because this patient's deductible isn't met, the allowed ambulance amounts and the ER average can land on the patient. The bill isn't large because the hospital is five miles away. It's large because four systems touched the same night. Now put the same rescue under a different price schedule.

## Three price worlds
Under Medicare, the same five-mile ground trip gets a different number. HCCI reported in 2022 that Medicare paid about $365 for the base rate, plus roughly $8 per mile. Five miles adds about $40, for a total near $405.

That comparison isn't added to our patient tab. It's the same ride under a different price schedule. Our commercial ambulance portion was $803 before the balance bill, about twice the Medicare-style amount.

Now move the ambulance into the air. The GAO found that the 2017 median price was $36,400 for a helicopter and $40,600 for a fixed-wing plane. The helicopter median had risen more than 60 percent from $22,100 in 2012. More than two-thirds of air ambulance transports for privately insured patients were out of network, according to the GAO. Health Affairs reported a median potential surprise bill of $21,698.

The No Surprises Act now protects air ambulance patients from surprise bills, but the ground ambulance gap remains. Apparently, the bill becomes more federally protected once it leaves the road. Same patient, same five miles, three prices on the board. So who benefits from keeping the ground system fragmented?

## Who gets paid
First, city and county fire-rescue departments and other government agencies run nearly two-thirds of privately insured emergency ground rides, according to KFF and Peterson-KFF. Public agencies may bill insurance because taxes don't cover every ambulance cost.

Second, private ambulance companies get paid for crews, vehicles, equipment, dispatch contracts, and transport volume. That includes companies owned by private-equity firms. Third, insurers manage networks and allowed amounts. A narrow network can leave the patient exposed.

The incentive is simple. The patient isn't shopping, the dispatcher isn't checking benefits, and the ambulance may not accept every insurer's contract rate. KFF, Peterson-KFF, and FAIR Health put out-of-network emergency ground rides somewhere around half to 62 percent. That's not a rare edge case.

## Fight the paper
This is general information, not personal financial, medical, or legal advice. Ask the ambulance provider and ER for itemized bills showing rates, mileage, insurance payments, responsibility, and balance bills.

Then check your state's ground ambulance protections through its insurance department or a trusted law resource. Commonwealth Fund and Georgetown CHIR reported in 2026 that about 22 to 24 states have some protection, so the map matters.

Appeal with the insurer after a true 911 emergency when no in-network ambulance was available. Ask the provider about hardship help, payment plans, or accepting the allowed amount in full. None guarantees savings.

Ambulance agencies need crews, vehicles, fuel, equipment, and readiness at 2 a.m. The math breaks because the patient can't choose the provider, check the network, or learn state law first.
