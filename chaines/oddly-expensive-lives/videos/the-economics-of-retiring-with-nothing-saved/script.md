# The Economics of Retiring With Nothing Saved

If your retirement income is mostly Social Security, a basic month for a single older renter costs $2,836, while the average check is $2,071. That's the 2025 Elder Index from UMass Boston against the Social Security Administration's January 2026 retired-worker benefit. It includes nothing for gifts, recreation, savings, or the week the car decides it is a decorative object. People usually notice the empty account first. Then the month drains through a $765 gap that comes back every month. We'll follow one 65-year-old renter through the envelopes. Somebody has to cover the missing $765.

## The case
The first envelope has the constraint that controls the rest. Our case is constructed, not a real person. She just turned 65. She's single, renting a one-bedroom in an average U.S. county, in good health, after 40 years of work in jobs with no retirement plan. Her savings balance is $0. She signs up for Medicare at 65 and claims Social Security now, two years before her full retirement age. Full retirement age means the age when Social Security pays the full calculated benefit. For people born in 1960 or later, the Social Security Administration says that's 67. Claiming at 65 pays 86.7% of the full benefit for life. SSA actuaries, cited by the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, say Social Security replaces about 39% of past pay for an average earner retiring at 65 in 2024. This is general information, not financial, legal, or tax advice. The amounts change every year. The envelope stack starts now.

How does someone get from four decades of work to a kitchen table with no retirement savings?

## No plan
Saving for retirement in America often starts with one boring question. Did the job offer a plan? The Social Security Administration says 30% of private-sector workers had no pension plan at work. And only 16% of workers without a workplace plan had any retirement savings. Without payroll deductions at work, saving required a separate account, a separate habit, and extra cash each month.

The Government Accountability Office, using the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, found about half of households 55 and older had no retirement savings. No 401(k). No IRA. GAO also said 32% of households 55 and older had neither retirement savings nor a traditional pension.

The Congressional Research Service, using the same survey, found 47% of households 65 and older had a retirement account, and 45% expected an old-style pension. The Federal Reserve's May 2026 household survey said 22% of adults 65 and older had neither one, though 95% had some asset, like a home or bank account.

A bank account, a car, or a house doesn't automatically create a monthly check. The first envelope that needs cash is rent.

## The rent
The Elder Index is a basic-needs budget built by researchers. It is not a poverty line, and it is not what every person pays. The 2025 Elder Index comes from the Gerontology Institute at UMass Boston, and here it uses a national-average county.

First envelope: the apartment. Rent for a one-bedroom, utilities included, from the Elder Index using HUD Fair Market Rents: $1,376. Running total: $1,376.

That one line takes almost two-thirds of the average $2,071 retired-worker check before Medicare, food, or gasoline. This is why renting matters. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies reported that in 2023, older renters were the fragile group, with median net wealth of $10,100. That was only 2% of the median older homeowner. Harvard JCHS also reported that 43% of older renters had less than $1,000 in cash savings, using 2022 Federal Reserve data.

The landlord does not accept future good intentions as a payment method. The next envelope is federal, medical, and partly invisible, and part of it disappears before the check reaches the bank.

## Medicare first
Medicare starts at 65, no matter when Social Security is claimed. Part B covers doctor and outpatient care. CMS says the 2026 Part B premium is $202.90 a month, usually deducted from Social Security before the money arrives. CMS also sets the 2026 Part B deductible at $283, and the hospital deductible at $1,736.

The Elder Index bundles Part B, drug and supplemental coverage or Medicare Advantage, and out-of-pocket costs for someone in good health. Its estimate for health care in good health is $460. Running total: $1,836.

KFF reported in May 2026 that people with Medicare spent an average of $6,459 out of pocket in 2023, including premiums and care. That was 36% of average Social Security income. KFF puts average dental spending at $1,107 a year, and traditional Medicare doesn't cover routine dental care.

## Food at home
After rent and health care, the next envelope lands on the kitchen table. Groceries. The Elder Index food line is based on the USDA low-cost plan, and it assumes every meal is cooked at home.

Groceries, every meal cooked at home, from the USDA low-cost plan in the Elder Index: $328. Running total: $2,164.

That means the basic month is already $93 higher than the average $2,071 retired-worker check. And this is still a very narrow food budget. There is no restaurant meal, birthday dinner, delivery fee, or holiday food upgrade. On a budget this tight, even coffee has to fit inside the grocery line.

So far, the envelopes are rent, health care, and groceries. The tab is already above the average check, and the car has not moved yet.

The next envelope is transportation, because the doctor, grocery store, and pharmacy still require trips.

## The car
In this budget, the car connects her apartment to the doctor, the grocery store, the pharmacy, a family visit, and the basic errands that keep a month from falling apart.

The Elder Index assumes a modest car, priced using the IRS mileage rate. That means gas, wear, maintenance, insurance, and the slow punishment of owning a machine with tires.

Transportation, a modest car priced at the IRS mileage rate in the Elder Index: $312. Running total: $2,476.

Against the average $2,071 Social Security check, she is now $405 short every month before soap, clothes, phone service, or household supplies.

Transit can help in some places. In other places, the bus may run too rarely to replace a car.

A repair bill would sit outside this budget, so one bad week can create a new shortfall.

If this is already changing how you see retiring with nothing saved, subscribe, because the next bill is worse.

The next line hides in the word miscellaneous.

## Everything else
Miscellaneous sounds small because the word is trying very hard not to say toilet paper.

In the Elder Index, this line means the ordinary things that keep a home functioning. Clothes. Soap. Laundry detergent. Toothpaste. Light bulbs. Household items. A basic phone. The kind of purchases that feel tiny until they arrive every week with a receipt.

Everything else, meaning clothes, soap, toilet paper, household items, and a phone, from the Elder Index: $360. Running total: $2,836.

That brings the basic-needs month to $2,836, and it still leaves out recreation, birthday gifts, travel, savings, and emergencies. The Elder Index says it is $34,032 a year.

There is still no line for a car repair, a dental bill, or a week when the heat runs too long.

The final total has appeared, but the real question is who covers the gap.

She has one month before the same envelopes come back. The same rent, health care, food, transportation, and miscellaneous bills come back next month.

## The check
Now move from the bill to the income. Nothing new gets added to the running tab. The question is whether the check can carry it.

The Social Security Administration said the average retired-worker benefit rose from $2,015 to $2,071 a month in January 2026, after the 2.8% cost-of-living raise.

That sounds helpful until it meets the stack.

$2,836 minus $2,071 leaves $765 short every month. That is $9,180 a year.

The SSA Annual Statistical Supplement for 2026 says about half of retired workers got less than $2,000 a month in December 2025. About 1 in 10 got less than $1,000.

SSA also says 61% of retired workers took a reduced early benefit. Those people averaged $1,791 a month. Against $2,836, that is $1,045 short every month.

The average check can hide lower checks. For this case, the budget is still short every month.

The claiming month can lock in a smaller check for decades.

## The claiming machine
Social Security is shaped by two big levers. Your earnings record, and the month you claim.

For her, full retirement age is still 67.

SSA's own example shows the machine. A $1,000 full benefit becomes $700 at 62, $867 at 65, $1,000 at 67, and $1,240 at 70.

Our case claimed at 65. So she gets 86.7% of her full benefit for life.

On that example, waiting from 65 to 70 is worth $373 more every month. But only if someone can cover five years of bills first.

Medicare is separate. It starts at 65 whatever the Social Security claiming age.

Then comes the earnings test. SSA's 2026 rule says that before full retirement age, $1 of benefits is withheld for every $2 earned above $24,480 a year. SSA credits those months back later, but that does not pay this month's rent.

Waiting can raise the check if the person can cover the bills until the later claiming date.

Social Security updates once a year. A lease renewal can still arrive before the next adjustment.

## Rent outruns
Social Security gets one cost-of-living adjustment a year. Rent follows the local market, and the Social Security adjustment only arrives once a year.

The 2026 Social Security raise was 2.8%. It moved the average retired-worker check from $2,015 to $2,071. That is $56 more a month.

A $56 raise does not close a $765 monthly hole.

Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies reported in 2025 that 58% of renters age 65 and older paid more than 30% of their income for housing in 2023. That was 4.5 million older renter households.

The same report said 2.6 million older renter households paid more than half their income for housing.

Federal rental help exists, but the line is narrow. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies said only 36.5% of very-low-income older renters eligible for federal rental assistance received it in 2021. Waiting lists can last years.

Rent stays first in the stack because housing instability raises the cost and difficulty of everything else.

## When it breaks
Change one assumption, and the stack gets heavier.

The Elder Index says the same single renter in poor health needs $3,015 a month, because health care rises to $639. Same apartment. Same groceries. Bigger medical envelope.

Change a different assumption, and it shrinks. A homeowner with no mortgage needs $2,162 a month. Still tight, but not $2,836.

That is the lesson. Renting with nothing saved is the hardest version because housing stays large and the cash cushion stays thin.

The Census Bureau said in September 2026 that 15.4% of people 65 and older were poor in 2025 under the Supplemental Poverty Measure, compared with 9.8% under the official-style measure. The higher number is mainly medical bills.

Social Security still matters. Census said it lifted 20.9 million older people above poverty. SSA data summarized by CBPP says Social Security is about 31% of older people's income.

Among beneficiaries 65 and older, 39% of men and 44% of women get at least half their income from it. About 1 in 7 get 90% or more.

The 2026 Trustees Report projects the retirement trust fund runs dry in late 2032, but incoming taxes would still pay about 78% unless Congress acts.

Now look at where the $2,836 goes each month.

## The legal exits
The landlord takes $1,376, almost half the monthly budget. Medicare takes the $202.90 Part B premium before the check reaches her bank account. Separate bills keep arriving from doctors, pharmacies, groceries, gas, insurance, and utilities.

These programs can lower the bill, but people usually have to apply, qualify, and wait. This is general information.

First, check the SSA statement before claiming. The claiming month can permanently change the check.

Second, Medicare Savings Programs. Medicare.gov says that in 2026, QI can pay Part B for one person under $1,816 in monthly income and $9,950 in resources. QMB starts lower, at $1,350, and can also cover copays. That can protect $202.90 each month.

Third, Extra Help can reduce prescription costs. SSA says that in 2026, income under $23,940 and resources under $18,090 may qualify. That can turn a pharmacy bill into a much smaller bill.

USDA's Food and Nutrition Service says SNAP can deduct medical costs over $35 and certain shelter costs. SSA's 2026 SSI federal benefit rate can add up to $994. SHIPs offer free Medicare counseling. Apply for rental help early, because the wait can be long.

A claiming date or benefit application can move hundreds of dollars a month.

## Final ledger
The upside is real. Social Security is a floor. Medicare lowers medical risk. Benefit programs help people who qualify and apply.

The math breaks when Social Security has to cover rent and medical costs almost by itself.

Here is the final bill. Rent and utilities, $1,376. Health care, $460. Groceries, $328. Transportation, $312. Everything else, $360. Fun, gifts, travel, and savings, zero.

Total basic month, $2,836.

The average retired-worker check is $2,071. Monthly shortfall, $765. Yearly shortfall, $9,180. For comparison, early claimers average $1,791, which is $280 less.

Landlords, Medicare, health plans, doctors, pharmacies, grocers, and retailers get paid first.

The renter absorbs the monthly gap first. Family may cover part of it, if they can. Public programs enter only if she qualifies and gets approved.

The empty account wasn't the problem. That envelope stack returned every month.
