# Recherche et consignes — The Economics of Retiring With Nothing Saved

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded, with the source named in the sentence; do not invent other statistics):
- GAO (Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances): about half of households 55+ had no retirement savings (no 401(k) or IRA); 32% had neither savings nor a traditional pension. Congressional Research Service (same survey): 47% of households 65+ had a retirement account, 45% expected an old-style pension.
- Federal Reserve household survey (May 2026): 22% of adults 65+ had neither a retirement account nor a pension, though 95% had some asset (home, bank account).
- SSA fact sheet (2025): 30% of private-sector workers have no pension plan at work; only 16% of people without a plan at work have any retirement savings.
- Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies (Housing America's Older Adults 2023, 2022 Fed data): the median renter 65+ has $10,100 in net wealth, 2% of the median older homeowner; 43% of older renters had less than $1,000 in cash savings. JCHS (2025): 58% of renters 65+ paid more than 30% of income for housing in 2023 (4.5 million households; 2.6 million paid more than half). Only 36.5% of very-low-income older renters eligible for federal rental help got it (2021); waiting lists can last years.
- SSA: 2.8% raise for 2026; the average retired-worker check went from $2,015 to $2,071 a month (January 2026).
- SSA Annual Statistical Supplement 2026 (December 2025 data, 53.6 million retired workers): about half get less than $2,000 a month, about 1 in 10 less than $1,000; 61% took a reduced early benefit, and their average check is $1,791 versus $2,516 for those with no reduction.
- SSA claiming rules (born 1960 or later): full retirement age is 67. Claiming at 62 pays 70% of the full benefit, at 65 86.7%, and each year past 67 adds 8% until 70 (124%), for life. On SSA's example amount, a $1,000 full benefit becomes $700 at 62, $867 at 65, $1,240 at 70. Medicare starts at 65 whatever the claiming age.
- SSA earnings test 2026: before full retirement age, $1 of benefits is withheld for every $2 earned above $24,480 a year; the withheld months are credited back at full retirement age.
- SSA fact sheet (2015 data, released 2024): Social Security is about 31% of the income of people 65+; among beneficiaries 65+, 39% of men and 44% of women get at least half their income from it, 12% of men and 15% of women get 90% or more. CBPP sums it up as 4 in 10 retirees at least half, 1 in 7 at least 90%.
- Census (September 2026): 15.4% of people 65+ were poor in 2025 under the Supplemental Poverty Measure (9.8% official-style), mainly because of medical bills; Social Security lifted 20.9 million people 65+ above the poverty line.
- Medicare 2026 (CMS): Part B premium $202.90 a month (up from $185), usually taken out of the Social Security check; deductibles $283 (Part B), $1,736 (hospital). KFF 2026: stand-alone drug plans average $36 a month, Medicare Advantage drug coverage $8 (most at $0). KFF: Medigap averaged $217 a month in 2023 (Plan G $164). Drug costs are capped at $2,100 a year in 2026 ($2,400 in 2027, Medicare.gov); traditional Medicare has no cap on other costs.
- KFF (May 2026): people with Medicare spent $6,459 out of pocket on average in 2023 (premiums plus care), 36% of their average Social Security income; dental ($1,107 a year on average) is not covered by traditional Medicare.
- Elder Index (Gerontology Institute, UMass Boston, 2025 data, national average, single person 65+, renter, good health): the monthly cost of basic needs is $1,376 housing (a one-bedroom with utilities, from HUD Fair Market Rents) + $460 health care (Part B premium, drug and supplemental coverage or Medicare Advantage, out-of-pocket costs) + $328 food (USDA low-cost plan, all meals at home) + $312 transportation (a car, IRS mileage rate) + $360 miscellaneous (clothes, household items, hygiene, phone) = $2,836 a month, $34,032 a year. Nothing for recreation, gifts or savings. Poor health: health care $639, total $3,015. A homeowner with no mortgage: $2,162.
- SSA Trustees Report 2026: the retirement trust fund runs dry in late 2032; after that, incoming taxes still pay 78% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts.
- Help (2026 unless noted): Medicare Savings Programs (state Medicaid; Medicare.gov) pay the Part B premium for one person with monthly income under $1,816 (QI; $1,350 for QMB, which also covers copays) and resources under $9,950; 18 states go further (KFF). Extra Help for drugs: income under $23,940, resources under $18,090, automatic with a Savings Program; in 2027, $0 premium and at most $5.80 a generic, $14.40 a brand drug. SNAP (USDA, from Oct. 1, 2026): at 60+ only the net-income test applies, medical costs over $35 a month and all excess shelter costs are deducted, assets up to $4,750 (home and most retirement plans not counted), up to $306 a month for one person. Since the July 2025 law, SNAP's 80-hours-a-month work rule applies up to 64 (with exemptions), not at 65. SSI (SSA): 65+ with very low income, up to $994 a month, resources under $2,000; the first $20 of other income is ignored (a $300 Social Security check gets $714 of SSI). SHIPs give free Medicare counseling.
ANGLE: Social Security was built as a floor, not a full retirement: for an average earner retiring at 65 in 2024 it replaces about 39% of past pay (SSA actuaries, cited by CBPP). The hidden machinery: the check is fixed for life by your earnings record and the month you claim; Medicare takes its premium before the money arrives; rent follows the market while the check moves only with the yearly raise; and most programs that close part of the gap pay only if you apply. Follow ONE typical case (a constructed example, say so): a woman who just turned 65, single, renting a one-bedroom in an average US county, 40 years of work in jobs with no retirement plan, $0 saved, good health. She signs up for Medicare and claims Social Security now, two years before her full retirement age of 67, so she gets 86.7% of her full benefit for life. The bill is one month of basic needs, priced by the Elder Index.
