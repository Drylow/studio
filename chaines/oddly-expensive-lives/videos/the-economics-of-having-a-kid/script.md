# The Economics of Having a Kid

When you're pricing a baby's first year, a typical North Carolina couple is $16,426 behind before daycare starts. CDC's National Center for Health Statistics counted 3,628,934 U.S. births in 2024. Peterson-KFF found that with a large-employer health plan, pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum care average $2,854 out of pocket, before newborn care. FMLA offers eligible workers up to 12 unpaid weeks, the Department of Labor says. At the Bureau of Labor Statistics median for full-time women, $1,131 a week, that's $13,572 missing. People price cribs, strollers, and diapers. But the first invoice is time. Somebody is already adding it up.

## The case
The first planning usually happens at the kitchen table, with a benefits packet, a hospital estimate, and a calendar with the unpaid leave period circled.

Here is the case on the board. A married couple in North Carolina. Early 30s. Both working full time. Employer health insurance. First baby. Average birth out-of-pocket cost. No state paid leave.

That state matters because, for this case, no state paid family leave check gets added to the federal baseline. So the leave math starts with FMLA and whatever is sitting in savings.

FMLA means the Family and Medical Leave Act. It can protect your job for up to 12 weeks. It does not replace your paycheck.

And even that protection has a lock on it. The Department of Labor's 2018 surveys found only 56% of employees were eligible for FMLA. So almost half of workers don't even get the locked door. They get the hallway.

This is one first-year running tab, not every possible family. It's general information, not personal tax, legal, or financial advice.

Five lines go on the tab: birth, unpaid leave, diapers, daycare, and the Child Tax Credit.

North Carolina, first year. The benefits packet is open, and the first line is already waiting.

## The birth bill
Start with the hospital invoice, because this is the bill everyone expects and still manages to underestimate.

Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker looked at claims from 2018 to 2020 for families with large-employer health plans. Pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum care added $18,865 in average health costs.

That is the big system number. The hospital, doctors, labs, and insurer all see that larger river of money.

The family does not usually pay all of it. In this case, the family pays the Peterson-KFF average out of pocket: $2,854.

The birth bill, Peterson-KFF average. Running total: $2,854.

Write down the annoying detail. Newborn care is not included in that Peterson-KFF birth number. The baby can have a separate medical file, separate claims, and separate charges.

We are not adding newborn care to this tab, because this constructed case uses only that verified family share.

Who gets paid? The hospital. The obstetric practice. Anesthesiology, if used. Lab work. Insurer network administration. Billing departments.

Even before the car seat is clicked in, the paperwork has its own nursery.

And the bigger first-year charge may never arrive as an invoice at all.

## The unpaid calendar
Now move from the hospital envelope to the calendar on the fridge. Twelve weeks are marked off. Each one is worth one median week of pay in this case.

The Department of Labor says FMLA gives eligible workers up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave. The key word is unpaid. FMLA itself doesn't replace your paycheck.

Eligibility is a filter, too. The Department of Labor's 2018 surveys found only 56% of employees were eligible for FMLA.

For the paycheck number, use the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In the second quarter of 2026, median weekly earnings for women working full time were $1,131.

Now do the plain arithmetic. Twelve weeks times $1,131 is $13,572.

12 weeks of unpaid leave at the women's median weekly pay, $13,572. Running total: $16,426.

That is a used-car-sized hole before daycare, diapers, formula, clothes, or the first pediatric visit outside the birth episode.

So who gets paid here? Usually, nobody pays the mother unless an employer, savings, short-term disability, vacation time, or a state program covers part of it. In this North Carolina case, no state paid leave fills it.

At this point, the family is already $16,426 behind.

## The cheap visible stuff
Now we can walk back into the baby aisle. Diapers, wipes, onesies, bottles, and the small objects that make the cost feel manageable.

The National Diaper Bank Network says disposable diapers cost about $70 to $100 a month per baby. A newborn can need up to 12 diapers a day, which sounds small until you live beside the trash can.

This case uses $80 a month. Over 12 months, that adds $960.

Diapers for one year at $80 a month, $960. Running total: $17,386.

Here is the twist. Diapers feel expensive because parents buy them constantly. They're scanned at the register, they sit in the cart, and they take up half the trunk.

But in this tab, a full year of diapers costs less than one week of unpaid leave.

A diaper box feels expensive because it's real, scanned, and repeated. The unpaid leave costs more, but it usually shows up as a smaller bank balance instead of a receipt.

If this is already changing how you see having a kid, subscribe, because the next bill is worse.

The diaper line is real money, but it's less than one median week of pay in this tab.

## The second rent
The daycare tour starts with cubbies, tiny chairs, a waitlist form, and a price sheet that looks like a lease.

Child Care Aware of America's 2024 Price and Supply report put the national average price of child care at $13,128 a year in 2024.

That number moved fast. The same report said the average was $11,582 in 2023. One year later, it had climbed by about $1,500.

Translate that into a monthly bill. It is about $1,094 a month, which is why parents call child care a second rent. That invoice buys a staffed room, snacks, insurance, and enough open hours to cover a workday.

The report also says child care takes about 10% of a married couple's median income.

One year of daycare at the national average price, $13,128. Running total: $30,514.

Who gets paid? The landlord or lender. Licensing compliance. Insurance. Utilities. Food. Supplies. Payroll. And the staff doing work parents can't leave undone.

That's the uncomfortable part. Parents can be charged a rent-sized price while many child care workers still aren't getting rich. The money is spread across a labor-heavy service with strict ratios and long hours.

Pay attention to the next line, because it is the only one on the tab that moves backward.

## The tax credit
Now the daycare invoice moves to the tax return. The same baby who created the bills becomes a line on a form.

For 2026, the IRS lists the Child Tax Credit at up to $2,200 per child under 17. The IRS says up to $1,700 can be refundable, meaning part can come back even when the credit exceeds income tax owed, subject to the rules.

Keep the case simple. Our North Carolina couple qualifies for the full credit.

Child Tax Credit back, minus $2,200. Running total: $28,314.

That feels like relief, because it is. A minus sign finally appears. This is the first line on the tab that reduces the total.

But the credit doesn't erase a $13,128 daycare bill or replace 12 weeks of missing pay. It softens the hit.

Write this number down. The first-year total isn't the lifetime cost of a child. It's the first-year cash hit in this constructed case. Child care changes fast by ZIP code. Comment later with what it costs where you live.

After the first birthday, the bill keeps changing shape.

## The long bill
The running tab is finished. No new numbers are being added to the $28,314 first-year total.

Now zoom out.

The USDA estimated that a middle-income married couple would spend $233,610 to raise a child born in 2015 through age 17. Brookings Institution updated that estimate in 2022 for higher inflation and put the figure at $310,605. That is about $18,270 a year.

That does not mean every family writes one neat $18,270 check. Nobody mails you one envelope labeled childhood. It means the household gets heavier. Housing, food, child care, transportation, clothing, health care, and other categories all get bigger because another person lives there.

The USDA found housing is the largest share, about 29% to 30%. After that come child care and education, then food.

So the kitchen table has fewer dramatic envelopes now. But the grocery receipt, the rent payment, and the larger apartment search keep repeating.

Children do not send invoices, which is inconsiderate from an accounting standpoint.

Many parents do not hear the next part when the baby is born, because it is 18 years away and already priced separately.

## The missing college line
Now the same child is on a campus tour. The chairs are bigger, the brochures are glossy, and the price sheet is no longer about daycare.

College is not included in the USDA or Brookings child-rearing totals.

College Board's Trends in College Pricing 2025 puts the average 2025 to 2026 budget at a public four-year college for in-state students at $30,990 a year. The visible tuition and fees part is $11,950, according to College Board.

That larger budget includes tuition and fees, housing, food, books and supplies, transportation, and personal expenses. The tuition number is the number people argue about. For families, the full budget matters because it includes the costs paid outside the tuition bill.

Do not add this to the first-year tab. It is a separate future bill, not part of the $28,314 thumbnail number.

But the pattern is the same. The first-year tab hides costs outside the baby aisle. College does the same outside tuition.

And there is still one bill that can outgrow the receipts, because it changes a parent's earnings path.

In the years after the first child, many families understand exactly what the missing paycheck really meant.

## The paycheck scar
Now leave the nursery and go to work. There is a desk calendar, a performance review, a promotion cycle, and a parent leaving at 5:15 because daycare closes.

Kleven, Landais, Posch, Steinhauer, and Zweimuller, in AEA Papers and Proceedings in 2019, estimated the long-run child penalty for women in the United States at about 31%. Their country charts show men's earnings staying close to the old path.

That gap is called the child penalty. It means one parent's earnings path bends after children, while the other parent's path mostly keeps going.

The study measures earnings patterns; it does not tell you what any one mother wanted.

The mechanisms are ordinary. Unpaid leave. Reduced hours. Missed promotions. Child care emergencies. Employers rewarding workers who can stay late with no notice.

Final ledger: birth out of pocket, $2,854; unpaid leave, $13,572; diapers, $960; daycare, $13,128; Child Tax Credit back, minus $2,200. Final first-year total, $28,314.
