# Recherche et consignes — The Economics of Having a Kid

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded, with the source named in the sentence; do not invent other statistics):
- 3,628,934 babies were born in the US in 2024 (CDC, National Center for Health Statistics, final data).
- Pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum care add $18,865 in health costs on average, of which $2,854 is paid out of pocket by families with large-employer health plans; $3,214 out of pocket for a C-section, $2,655 for a vaginal birth (Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, claims 2018-2020). Newborn care is not included.
- FMLA gives up to 12 weeks of UNPAID job-protected leave; only 56% of employees are eligible (US Department of Labor, 2018 surveys). There is no federal paid parental leave (a few states have their own programs).
- Median weekly earnings of women working full time: $1,131 in the second quarter of 2026 (Bureau of Labor Statistics).
- Disposable diapers cost about $70 to $100 a month per baby, and a newborn can need up to 12 diapers a day (National Diaper Bank Network).
- Child care: the national average price was $13,128 a year in 2024, up from $11,582 in 2023; it takes about 10% of a married couple's median income (Child Care Aware of America, 2024 Price & Supply report).
- Child Tax Credit: up to $2,200 per child under 17 for 2026, of which up to $1,700 is refundable (IRS).
- Raising a child born in 2015 to age 17: USDA estimated $233,610 for a middle-income married couple; the Brookings Institution's 2022 update for higher inflation put it at $310,605, about $18,270 a year. Housing is the single largest share (about 29%-30%), then child care and education, then food (USDA).
- College is NOT included in those numbers: the average 2025-26 budget at a public four-year college for in-state students is $30,990 a year (tuition and fees $11,950) (College Board, Trends in College Pricing 2025).
- The child penalty: in the US, mothers' earnings fall by about 31% in the long run after the first child, while fathers' earnings are basically unaffected (Kleven, Landais, Posch, Steinhauer and Zweimüller, "Child Penalties across Countries", AEA Papers and Proceedings 2019).
ANGLE: everybody prices the baby in diapers and cribs, but those are the cheap, visible part. The real bill is time: unpaid leave, daycare that costs like a second rent, and the mother's paycheck that never fully comes back. Follow ONE typical case (a constructed example, say so): a married couple in North Carolina, early 30s, both working full time, employer health insurance, first baby, the average birth out-of-pocket cost ($2,854). The mother takes the full 12 weeks of unpaid FMLA leave (no state paid leave in North Carolina in this case), then the baby goes to daycare.
RUNNING TAB for the FIRST YEAR (every line must be said aloud with this exact amount, then the running total):
- Birth out of pocket (Peterson-KFF average): $2,854 -> total $2,854
- 12 weeks of unpaid leave at the women's median weekly pay of $1,131: $13,572 -> total $16,426
- Diapers for one year at $80 a month: $960 -> total $17,386
- One year of daycare at the national average price: $13,128 -> total $30,514
- Child Tax Credit back: minus $2,200 -> total $28,314 (the only line that pays YOU)
The running tab must end on one final first-year total, $28,314, that becomes the thumbnail number. Then zoom out WITHOUT adding to the tab: $310,605 to age 17 (Brookings), housing as the biggest share, college at $30,990 a year on top, and the hidden bill: the 31% child penalty on the mother's earnings. Do not give financial advice; say once that this is general information. Warm, not anti-kids: the point is to see the bill clearly.
