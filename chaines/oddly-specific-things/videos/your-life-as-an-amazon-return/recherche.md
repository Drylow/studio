# Recherche et consignes — Your Life as an Amazon Return

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded; name the source lightly inside the sentence for big facts; do not invent other statistics or sources):
- How much comes back (retailer survey estimates, always say who): the National Retail Federation and Happy Returns (Oct. 2025) estimate Americans will return about $850 billion of merchandise in 2025, about 15.8% of sales, and about 19.3% of online sales; in 2024 it was about $890 billion, 16.9% (NRF and Happy Returns, Dec. 2024). For 2023, NRF and Appriss Retail said $743 billion, 17.6% of online sales against about 10% in stores. In the 2025 survey 82% of shoppers said free returns weigh on where they buy.
- "Keep it" refunds: CBS MoneyWatch (Jan. 2021) reported Amazon refunds some cheap or bulky items without taking them back (a yoga mat, a bag of dog food); Walmart uses a formula (purchase history, item value, cost of processing). Amazon's "Returnless Resolutions" for its sellers (Aug. 2024, Supply Chain Dive) excludes items with an average price above $75, heavy and bulky items and dangerous goods. A $100 air fryer does not qualify: it has to travel.
- How an Amazon return works (Amazon's returns vice president, Dec. 2023; Amazon, 2026): more than 10,000 US drop-off points, and four out of five customers live within 5 miles of one; no box or label needed for eligible items, just a QR code; Whole Foods kiosks take a return "in 60 seconds or less"; the "vast majority of eligible refunds" are issued within five hours. Returns are consolidated and sent to an Amazon return center; return centers are dedicated to types of products ("clothing, electronics, or furniture and appliances"). Inspectors check all sides of the box for damage or broken seals, then the item for "signs of use" and damage. Paths: resold as new, sold used through Amazon Resale (graded "Like New", "Very Good", "Good" or "Acceptable"), sent back to the seller, liquidated, or donated (through the nonprofit Good360 to more than 700 US nonprofits); recycling when none of these works.
- What liquidation pays (Amazon's own figure): Amazon tells its third-party sellers that with FBA Liquidations "we send your inventory to accredited, wholesale liquidators" and they "typically recover about 5% to 10% of an item's average selling price" (Amazon Seller Central announcement). Optoro, a returns-software company, says processing a return can cost a retailer up to 66% of the item's original price (CNBC, Feb. 2022). Retail analyst Jason Goldberg told CBC (2020): "You're lucky if half of all returns can still be sold as new."
- The pallet market: Amazon sells bulk lots on its official storefront on B-Stock, "Amazon Liquidation Auctions", pallets and truckloads for registered business buyers only (a resale certificate is required, no personal use). Real listing format (seen Oct. 2026): "Est. 1 Pallet of ... & More, 11 Units, Like New Condition, Ext. Retail $9,690, Salem, OR", with an "Avg. Cost Per Unit".
- Bin stores (Houston Chronicle, Dec. 26, 2025): "contract holders" buy truckloads straight from Amazon and other retailers, sometimes in pallets labelled only "electronics", "clothes" or "home goods". One southeast Houston store pays "$16,000 and up" per load with only a vague idea of what's inside; its owner once lost $70,000 in a month on "electronics" that turned out to be Christmas lights. Upwards of two dozen bin stores around Houston (an industry estimate). Friday is restock day: everything $10, then one or two dollars less each day, down to $1 on Wednesday. The line forms two hours before 9 a.m.; long tables under thick gray tarps; a siren on a bullhorn, the tarps come off, wooden bins of sealed brown boxes; some shoppers pull on disposable gloves; an "unboxing station" by the register lets them check an item works; resellers look up values on their phones and flip what they find. The Reverse Logistics Association's director told CNBC "90% of the time, there's nothing wrong with it" (his view).
- The other endings: ITV News (June 2021) filmed inside Amazon's warehouse in Dunfermline, Scotland: a leaked document showed more than 124,000 items marked "destroy" in one week of April 2021 against 28,000 marked "donate" (smart TVs, laptops, headphones; unsold stock and returns). Amazon said: "No items are sent to landfill in the UK", with "energy recovery" as a last resort. In August 2021 it launched resale and liquidation programs for sellers in the UK; Scotland's environment agency later found no evidence of non-compliance (cited by Amazon to the UK Parliament, 2022). CBC Marketplace (Oct. 2020, not 2021) hid GPS trackers in 12 items returned to Amazon Canada, a coffee maker among them: by publication only 4 had been resold; a brand-new backpack went from Amazon's Mississauga warehouse to a waste facility in Etobicoke within three weeks (Amazon said it arrived damaged); toy blocks travelled more than 950 km to a new owner in Quebec. CBC Toronto (Dec. 2023) found 36 returned packages in liquidation bins with the first buyers' names and addresses still on the labels; Amazon said its liquidators must remove them.
- Landfill and carbon (estimates, always "Optoro estimates"): Optoro estimated US returns create about 9.6 billion pounds of landfill waste a year and more than 27 million metric tons of CO2, like 5.9 million cars driving for a year (Retail Brew, Dec. 2022); its earlier figure was 5.8 billion pounds (CNBC, 2022).
- Places and distances: the Whole Foods at 214 3rd Street in Gowanus, Brooklyn (near the Gowanus Canal, rooftop greenhouse) has an Amazon counter and a returns kiosk, 8 a.m. to 10 p.m. (Whole Foods). Park Slope to Houston is about 1,420 miles in a straight line (computed great-circle, 2,280 km). Pasadena, Texas: about 152,000 people (2020 census), southeast of downtown Houston, the Houston Ship Channel on its northern edge, refineries, a Strawberry Festival.
