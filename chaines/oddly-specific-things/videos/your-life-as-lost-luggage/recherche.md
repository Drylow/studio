# Recherche et consignes — Your Life as Lost Luggage

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded; name the source lightly inside the sentence for big facts; do not invent other statistics or sources):
- How often bags go wrong, worldwide (SITA Baggage IT Insights 2026, 20th edition, published June 29, 2026, data for 2025): about 24 million bags mishandled in 2025, 4.9 per 1,000 passengers, out of about 5 billion passengers; the rate fell 23% in a year and is now below pre-pandemic levels (the 2007 peak was 47 million bags: SITA). More than three-quarters were delayed or misrouted, 21% damaged, 4% permanently lost (about 1 million bags: computed, 4% of 24 million). Transfers are the biggest cause: 39% of cases (41% in 2024). SITA puts the cost at $6.3 billion a year, about $635 per lost bag. Don't mix with the 2025 edition (33.4 million bags in 2024).
- The US (DOT Air Travel Consumer Report, Feb. 2026): in 2025, US airlines reported about 2.43 million mishandled bags out of about 471 million checked, 0.52 per 100 bags. DOT counts per 100 bags, SITA per 1,000 passengers: do not compare the two numbers.
- How a bag misses its plane: the paper tag carries a 10-digit number and a barcode and the destination code in big letters. Automatic readers fail when a tag is crumpled, wet or bent; a "no-read" bag is pushed off to a manual station where a worker reads it (Vanderlande, a baggage-system maker). If the tag is torn off, the airport may not know where to send the bag. Newer RFID tags read 99-100% of the time (IATA, 2019).
- The hub: Atlanta is the world's busiest airport, 106.3 million passengers in 2025 (ACI World, July 2026). NPR (May 2026): on a busy day the biggest airline there handles more than 100,000 bags in Atlanta, three-quarters of them connecting; on average nine employees handle each bag; short connections are "hot bags", driven by tug from plane to plane; layovers over two hours are "cold bags" fed into a maze of belts under the concourses. The airline in our story is never named.
- Finding bags: WorldTracer (run by SITA since 1991, 2,800+ airports) matches found bags with missing-bag reports using tag number, name, route, bag type and contents (SITA; Wikipedia). Agents use the IATA Baggage Identification Chart: photos of about 30 bag types and color samples. About two-thirds of mishandled bags are back within 48 hours (SITA, Dec. 2025). SITA says 29 airlines now accept a passenger's tracker location (Dec. 2025; no brand on screen).
- Timeline and money, US domestic flight: after about 5 days the file goes to a central baggage office (American Airlines' website); a bag not found within 21 days is "likely lost" (Delta's website, 2026). Airlines keep searching about 90 days before a bag is declared orphaned (Unclaimed Baggage, March 2026; NPR 2023: "up to three months"). The legal cap is $4,700 per passenger since Jan. 22, 2025 (DOT; it was $3,800). Airlines pay the depreciated value, can ask for receipts and may exclude jewelry, electronics, cash and other valuables (DOT guidance). Emergency toiletries and basic clothes are reimbursed with receipts (Delta). The checked-bag fee is refunded if the bag isn't delivered within 12 hours of a domestic arrival (DOT rule). For international trips (Montreal Convention) the cap is 1,519 SDR since Dec. 28, 2024 (was 1,288): about $2,060 at the IMF rate of Oct. 2, 2026 (computed), and after 21 days a passenger can treat the bag as lost.
- Unclaimed Baggage: founded in 1970 in Scottsboro, Alabama, by Doyle Owens, with a borrowed pickup truck and a $300 loan; his first load came from the Trailways bus line in Washington, D.C. One store, 50,000 sq ft, on West Willow Street in a city of about 16,000 on the Tennessee River (Boston Globe, March 2026); about 1 million visitors a year; about 7,000 new items a day; an online store that ships to 102 countries. Several major US airlines sell to it; some (Alaska) handle lost items themselves (Boston Globe).
- The sale: bags are bought in bulk, sight unseen, at a price kept secret by contract; a JetBlue spokesman told AP in 2011: "It's probably less than what you paid for lunch." Unclaimed Baggage says airlines don't profit and only get rid of bags after claims are paid; items arrive with no identifying information. It says less than 0.03% of checked bags become orphans after the three-month search.
- Inside: 18-wheelers bring the loads to a 100,000-sq-ft sorting building closed to the public, where "bag openers" in latex gloves unpack (Boston Globe 2026). About one-third of items is sold, one-third donated, one-third recycled or thrown away (Sonni Hood, company spokesperson, Boston Globe 2026). Clothes go through its own laundry. Electronics are tested and erased to official NIST standards (company FAQ); locked devices are recycled, only unlocked ones are wiped and sold (Boston Globe). Personal papers are shredded. No used underwear or toothbrushes are sold. Prices: 20 to 80% of suggested retail. Eyeglasses go to Lions Clubs.
- The suitcase itself is usually not sold: "the one thing you won't find at Unclaimed Baggage: luggage" (Boston Globe, March 2026). Hard-sided suitcases are painted by staff and volunteers for Love Luggage and given to foster children, many of whom move their things in trash bags (Unclaimed Baggage). Our check of the online store on Oct. 3, 2026: its luggage section is almost all backpacks and duffels, 2 suitcases out of 210 items. Love Luggage (since at least 2019) "has served thousands of children"; suitcases get "bright happy messages, pictures and quotes" (Alabama Public Radio, 2023); each one is given with the book Tuesday's Bear, a teddy bear and a "first night" bag of toiletries; in May 2026 (National Foster Care Month) more than 250 suitcases were to be painted and donated at the Scottsboro store (Unclaimed Baggage, April 2026). About 329,000 children were in US foster care on Sept. 30, 2024 (federal Children's Bureau).
- Places and distances (computed, straight line): Pittsburgh to Atlanta about 527 miles; Atlanta to San Diego about 1,888 miles; Atlanta to Scottsboro about 116 miles (about 150 by road: NPR). Thanksgiving 2025 was Thursday Nov. 27; the Saturday before is Nov. 22.
STORY SHAPE (constructed example, typical, unnamed people): "you" are a medium black hard-shell suitcase on four spinner wheels (no brand: "the suitcase", "you"), $180 new, owned by a retired school librarian, 64, in Pittsburgh, flying to her son's in San Diego for Thanksgiving week. Packed (constructed, about $1,400 with you): a navy rain jacket ($120), walking shoes, two pairs of jeans, sweaters, a hair dryer, a toiletry bag, spare reading glasses, a tablet locked with her passcode ($330), and a sweater she knitted for her 6-year-old grandson (she can only price the yarn: $38). The trace: a yellow ribbon she ties around your handle Friday night "so I'll spot you on the belt"; it stays on you to the end. Hands, in this real order: Hand 1 the owner (packing Friday night, the ribbon; Saturday Nov. 22, 5:10 a.m. check-in, she pays the $35 bag fee, the agent prints your tag with SAN in big letters, routed through ATL) -> Hand 2 the belts (Pittsburgh to Atlanta, 527 miles; a 41-minute connection, you're a "hot bag"; somewhere between the belly of the plane and the tug, your paper tag tears off; the reader finds nothing, you're pushed aside, the San Diego plane leaves without you) -> Hand 3 the lost-bag room in Atlanta (no tag, no name outside: you're logged as a found bag, "black, hard-shell, spinner", one of many black suitcases; five days on the floor among strangers' bags) -> Hand 4 the airline's central baggage warehouse (location not in our sources: keep it unnamed; you're opened, your contents typed into the file to help a match; nothing inside says her name; about 90 days on a shelf; meanwhile she is paid) -> Hand 5 the bag opener in Scottsboro (March; an 18-wheeler; latex gloves; your contents split three ways: the rain jacket washed and hung on a rack, the reading glasses to the eyeglass charity, the knitted sweater to a donation box, the locked tablet to recycling, her printed itinerary shredded; you, a hard-shell, go to the Love Luggage pile) -> Hand 6 the painter (a volunteer at the May painting day at the store: a yellow sun and "This one is yours" across your lid; inside, a teddy bear, a book about a bear left behind, a toothbrush) -> Hand 7 a foster child (a 9-year-old boy in north Alabama; a caseworker hands you over; his clothes come out of a black trash bag and into you; he keeps the yellow ribbon, thinking it came with the gift). Side cutaway at Hand 5: a shopper buys the navy rain jacket off the rack for $40. Value thread, same labels every time: "$1,400 packed" -> "$900 paid" (her claim: depreciated value, tablet not covered; constructed) -> "a secret price" (what the airline gets from Unclaimed Baggage; never published) -> "$0, a gift". Jacket labels: "$120 new" -> "$40 on the rack" (constructed, inside the store's 20-80% range).
OWNER CUTAWAYS: San Diego, Saturday late morning: the carousel stops, the yellow ribbon never comes around; at the baggage office she points at a photo on the laminated chart of bag types, gets a file number starting with SAN; most bags show up within 48 hours (SITA), so she buys a toothbrush and keeps the receipt; her bag fee comes back automatically (12-hour rule). Thanksgiving Day (day 5): she wears her son's sweatshirt; the claim moves to a central office; she types the contents from memory, prices the sweater at the cost of the yarn. Months later, spring in Pittsburgh: the airline paid about $900 in January (under the $4,700 cap, depreciated); she buys a new suitcase, ties a new yellow ribbon and drops a small tracker inside; she never learns where you went.
