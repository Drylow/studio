# Recherche et consignes — Your Life as a Stolen Bike

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded; name the source lightly inside the sentence for big facts; do not invent other statistics or sources):
- How many bikes are stolen (estimates, always say who estimates): Project 529 (529 Garage, a bike registration program, 2019 study) estimated more than 2 million bikes stolen in North America every year, about one every 30 seconds; only about 1 in 5 thefts reported to police; fewer than 20% of owners know their bike's serial number; only about 5% of stolen bikes ever returned to their owners (CapoVelo and Cycling Industry News, Aug. 2019). A 2025 study in the journal Transport Findings (Agarwal, Fitch-Polse, Nelson, Herr; YouGov survey of March 2024) estimated about 2.4 million adult bicycles stolen in the US every year, worth about $1.4 billion.
- Recovered is not returned: Project 529 (blog, Aug. 2024) says only about 2% of bikes recovered by police get back to their owners, mostly because nobody reported a serial number; most bicycles have their serial number engraved beneath the bottom bracket (Bike Index). Describe only that the number is there, never anything about altering it.
- Bike Index is a free nonprofit registry where owners register bikes and report them stolen; it was started in 2013 and says it has helped recover more than 14,000 bikes (OPB, June 2024). Its co-founder Bryan Hance (Portland) said the average stolen bike reported to Bike Index was worth "right about $1,000" in 2013 and "$1,785" now (June 2024), "almost due completely to E-bikes" (OPB, June 2024). He described crews "rolling into apartment buildings at three o'clock in the morning... carrying power tools" (OPB, 2024). Use only at that level, never any method.
- What the thief gets (estimate): Bryan Hance said he is "used to seeing a $5,000 bike stolen and resold for $250" in the US (Bicycle Retailer, Jan. 2022), about 5%. At that rate an $8,000 bike goes for about $400 (say it as an estimate "at that rate"). A bike thief interviewed for the US Justice Department's Center for Problem-Oriented Policing guide "Bicycle Theft" (Johnson, Sidebottom, Thorpe, 2008) said he got about 10% of the list price for a whole bike (backup source, do not stack both).
- The real pipeline, Bay Area to Jalisco (federal indictment, US Attorney's Office, Northern District of California, filed Jan. 2024, unsealed Feb. 2024; these are allegations, never say anyone was convicted; never name the accused): high-end bicycles were taken in nighttime burglaries of homes in San Francisco and Redwood City (April 2020 to April 2021; nine bikes worth $3,000 to $9,000 each, among them a titanium road bike, a carbon road bike and an e-bike); they were brought to an automotive shop in San Jose, photographed, taken apart, packaged and transported to a partner in Jalisco, Mexico; the photos were sent ahead and used for Facebook sales ads; prosecutors say the Facebook page was set so only people in Mexico could see it; the partner kept records of the profits and the shop owner got a share. San Francisco police searched the San Jose shop in April 2021 and found a stolen bike already taken apart and ready for transport to Jalisco (SFPD, Feb. 2024).
- The seller in Jalisco (Bike Index investigation 2020-2024, four years of monitoring, led by Bryan Hance; Wired, June 2024, Christopher Solomon; also SF Standard, Axios, OPB, NPR, GCN): a seller in La Barca, Jalisco, near Guadalajara, sold bikes stolen mostly in the San Francisco Bay Area (later also San Diego, Oregon, Colorado) through Facebook and Instagram pages. Bike Index counted 2,802 ads and 654 different bikes, roughly $2.1 million in sales. Some months, about $60,000 to $80,000 worth of bikes posted; Hance said the seller "had a real affinity for the high end stuff" (Hance, OPB, June 2024). Bikes stolen in San Francisco were listed online in Jalisco by the next morning (Bike Index); since the photos were sent ahead from San Jose, the listing went up before the physical bike could have covered the distance (inference from the indictment + Bike Index, say it as "your photo is for sale before the rest of you gets there"). The stolen bikes were offered at about one-third of their original prices (Wired, reported by Axios and Jalopnik). The first one Hance spotted, in 2020: an $8,000 bike stolen from an apartment in Mountain View, for sale at a steep discount in Jalisco (Wired via Axios).
- How the shop was matched: the listing photos showed the same shop wall with transmission parts behind bike after bike; Hance: "Same wall, same door, same color" (Bay Area News Group, quoted by SFist, June 2024).
- Insurance: a stolen bike is usually covered under the personal property part of a renters or homeowners policy, minus the deductible; some insurers cap bikes at a lower limit; the Insurance Information Institute suggests an add-on for an expensive bike (WCPO / Triple-I).
- Places and distances: San Francisco to San Jose is about 50 miles by road (US 101). San Francisco to La Barca is about 1,700 miles in a straight line (computed great-circle, 2,730 km). La Barca: town on the north bank of the Lerma River, on the Jalisco-Michoacan line, about 38,800 people in the town (2020 census, Wikipedia), about 57 miles in a straight line from Guadalajara. Guadalajara's Via RecreActiva: every Sunday from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m., main avenues such as Avenida Chapultepec and Avenida Vallarta are closed to cars for walkers, skaters and bikes (official site viagdl.com); it began in 2004.
- The Inner Sunset is a San Francisco neighborhood just south of Golden Gate Park, served by the N Judah streetcar; foggy, with steep hills.
