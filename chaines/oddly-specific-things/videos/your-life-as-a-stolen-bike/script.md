# Your Life as a Stolen Bike

At 3:10 on a foggy Tuesday morning, in a garage in San Francisco's Inner Sunset, you're at 100% battery when two flashlights slide in under the door. You're an $8,000 electric bike, matte gray, with a small dented brass bell on your handlebar. Upstairs, the man who owns you is asleep. He did everything bike owners are told to do. A heavy lock, a locked garage, and a photo of your serial number saved on his phone. But by sunrise, you're in an auto shop about 50 miles south. By the next morning, your photo is for sale in Jalisco, Mexico, before the rest of you even gets there. Six hands and about 1,700 miles later, a stranger pays about $2,700 for you and rings that bell on a Sunday morning in Guadalajara. And the serial number he saved stays on you the whole way.

## Hand 1: The owner
The evening before, at 7:40, you roll down the ramp into the garage under his apartment building, damp from the fog. Your battery reads 31%. The hills did that.

He walks you to your spot by the back wall, between a storage cage and somebody's surfboard. He plugs your charger into the outlet by the water heater. A small green light comes on. You start filling up.

You know his week better than he does. Down Irving Street in the morning, past Golden Gate Park, into downtown traffic. Back up the hills at night, where you do most of the work and he takes most of the credit.

Before you, he rode the N Judah streetcar and spent a lot of time waiting for it. Then he gave up his car and paid $8,000 for you. That's serious money for a bike. In this city, it's also a car he never has to park.

He added one thing himself. The brass bell, bolted to the left side of your handlebar. It has a dent from the day he dropped you on Ninth Avenue, so it rings a little flat. He never replaced it, because he likes it flat.

He hangs his helmet on the hook above you. He wraps a heavy lock through your frame and around a steel pipe. Months ago, he crouched down and took a photo of the serial number stamped under your frame. Then he forgot he had it.

The timer clicks the garage lights off.

You sit in the dark, charging for a morning ride you won't take with him.

## Hand 2: The night crew
At 3:10 a.m., the garage door rises a couple of feet and stops. Two figures duck under it. Hoods up. Headlamps aimed at the floor.

They walk past the cars without looking. They're here for the back wall.

There's a reason for that. Bike Index co-founder Bryan Hance says the average stolen bike reported to his registry was worth about $1,000 in 2013. By 2024, it was about $1,785, and he says that's almost completely because of e-bikes. Bikes like you.

He has also described crews rolling into apartment buildings at three in the morning, carrying power tools. Federal prosecutors described the same kind of night in a Bay Area case. High-end bikes, taken in nighttime burglaries of homes in San Francisco and Redwood City.

A short whine fills the garage, then stops. Upstairs, nobody wakes up. Your lock ends up on the concrete in two pieces.

One of them pulls your charger cable out of your side. It drops and swings against the wall, still plugged in. Your display goes dark. You're at 100%, fully charged for somebody else.

They lift you into a van with two other bikes. Your bell taps a wheel that isn't yours.

To the man upstairs, you're $8,000. To these two, you're a quick sale. Bryan Hance has said he's used to seeing a $5,000 bike stolen and resold for $250. At that rate, you go for about $400, and you haven't even left the garage.

The van turns onto 101 and heads south while the fog is still sitting on the city. The people waiting for you there want something before they want you. Your photo.

## The empty garage
At 7:05 a.m., he comes down the stairs with a travel mug and his keys. He looks at the back wall and keeps looking.

The helmet is still on its hook. The charger is still in the wall, green light on, its cable lying on the concrete where your side used to be. The lock is on the floor in two pieces. Two other spots on the wall are empty too.

He walks the whole garage anyway. Behind the cars. Behind the storage cages. As if an electric bike might have wandered off and gotten stuck behind a minivan.

He unplugs the charger out of habit. Then he plugs it back in, as if that might help.

A neighbor comes down for her car and stops next to him. Her bike was one of the other two. They stand there for a minute, two adults staring at a wall.

Then the practical part starts. He's late. The N Judah is packed. He stands the whole way downtown, holding a pole, his phone open to the photo of your serial number.

It's the one useful thing he has left, and he's hoping it's enough.

## Hand 3: The auto shop in San Jose
Before sunrise, you're about 50 miles south, inside an auto shop in San Jose.

It smells like gear oil and old rags. Transmissions sit on metal shelves. A radio plays low, and nobody here fixes bikes.

The man who runs the place doesn't ride you. He leans you against one wall, under the same work light every time, and takes photos. Front. Side. Close on your motor and your display. Behind you, there are transmission parts on a shelf. That wall will matter later.

A 2024 federal indictment described a shop like this one. Prosecutors say stolen bikes were brought to an auto shop in San Jose and photographed. Then, they say, the bikes were taken apart, packed up, and sent to a partner in Jalisco, Mexico. The photos were sent to that partner, who used them to advertise the bikes on social media. When San Francisco police searched that shop, they found a stolen bike already in pieces, ready for Jalisco. That's what the charges say.

Your photos leave in seconds, through a phone.

Then the wrenches come out. Wheels off. Handlebar off. Your battery slides out of the frame and goes into its own box. The bell stays on the bar, wrapped in cardboard with it.

Bike Index says bikes stolen in San Francisco were listed online in Jalisco by the next morning. So while your frame is getting taped up in San Jose, your photo is already for sale in another country.

Here, you're boxes and a photo. Prosecutors say the man in the shop gets a share of the profits when you sell. So the price that matters now is the one waiting on the other end.

The tape gun screams. The boxes get stacked by the door.

## The report
That night, at a kitchen table in the Inner Sunset, he fills out a police report online. Color. Value. Then the serial number, copied from the photo on his phone.

That already puts him ahead. Project 529, a bike registration company, estimated in 2019 that only about one theft in five gets reported to police. It also found fewer than 20% of owners even know their serial number. Its estimate was more than 2 million bikes stolen a year in North America. A 2025 study in the journal Transport Findings put the US alone at about 2.4 million adult bikes a year.

He marks you stolen on Bike Index, the free registry. It says it has helped recover more than 14,000 bikes. He posts in a neighborhood group. He checks used-bike listings every night for a week.

His renters insurance may cover you as personal property, minus his deductible. It doesn't cover the hills.

Project 529 also estimated that only about 5% of stolen bikes ever get back to their owners. Even bikes the police do recover mostly stay unclaimed, because nobody reported a serial number.

His report, with your serial number, goes into a police database in San Francisco. By then, your boxes are already loaded for the trip south.

## Hand 4: The drive south
Inside the cardboard, it's dark and it smells like tape.

You hear an engine, then highway. Long stretches of hum, then stops, doors, and somebody else's music. Every hour gets a little warmer.

Your battery rides in its own box. Your wheels ride in another. For the first time since the bike shop, you're not one object.

Somewhere along the way, the box with your handlebar takes a hard bump. The bell gives one small, flat ring inside the cardboard. Nobody hears it.

Then a long stop. A line of engines idling. Voices in English, then in Spanish. When the hum starts again, you're in Mexico, still in pieces.

From San Francisco to La Barca, in Jalisco, is about 1,700 miles in a straight line. You cover all of it without turning a pedal. Your motor, built for San Francisco hills, doesn't run once.

Your price has already traveled ahead of you. The listing with your San Jose photo shows you at about $2,700. That's a big jump from about $400 in the van.

Back in San Francisco, he's on his third night of checking used-bike listings. None of them are you.

At the other end, someone has to put all of this back together, bell included.

Near the end, the ride gets bumpy and slow. Then the engine stops for good.

## Hand 5: The seller in La Barca
The boxes open into bright sun in La Barca. It's a town of about 38,800 people on the north bank of the Lerma River, southeast of Guadalajara.

On a back patio, a man puts you back together. Wheels on. Bar on. Battery back in the frame. He wipes the garage dust off your top tube and tightens the bell without looking at it.

His tools are a phone, a pump, and a list.

Prosecutors say a partner in Jalisco posted the photos from San Jose on a sales page and kept the books. They say the page was set so only people in Mexico could see it.

Bike Index watched a seller like this for four years. It counted 2,802 ads and 654 different bikes, roughly $2.1 million in sales. Bryan Hance told Oregon Public Broadcasting that in some months, the seller posted $60,000 to $80,000 worth of bikes. Hance said he "had a real affinity for the high end stuff."

You fit right in.

Most of the bikes came from the Bay Area. Later, Bike Index says, the seller added bikes stolen in San Diego, Oregon, and Colorado.

Your listing sits among the others. Road bikes, mountain bikes, e-bikes, most of them shot against the same wall in San Jose.

Wired reported the bikes went for about a third of their original price. For you, that's about $2,700. To a buyer scrolling in Guadalajara, it looks like a very good deal on a very good bike.

The messages come in, in Spanish. Is it still available?

It is. For about one more day.

## The wall in the photos
Months later, in San Francisco, a friend sends him a news story.

It's about Bike Index and Bryan Hance, who spent four years tracking stolen Bay Area bikes to a seller in Jalisco. In 2020, Hance spotted an $8,000 bike, stolen from an apartment in Mountain View, for sale in Jalisco at a steep discount. One detail tied the listings together. The same shop wall showed up behind bike after bike. Hance said it was "same wall, same door, same color."

He reads it twice. Then he opens the screenshots.

There's a gray e-bike leaning against a wall with transmission parts on the shelf. The photo is small, and the handlebar is cropped. He zooms in until it's just squares.

He can't open the page himself. It was set so people in the US couldn't see it.

He sends the link to the officer on his case. He adds a note to your Bike Index page. He doesn't hear much back.

He rides a cheaper used bike now. He keeps it in the living room, next to the couch.

The charger from the garage is in a drawer. He still hasn't thrown it out.

## Hand 6: The new owner
On a Saturday afternoon, a woman from Guadalajara meets the seller and checks you over.

She squeezes your brakes. She spins your back wheel. She turns your display on and watches the battery number come up. She pays about $2,700 for an electric bike that would cost far more new.

The listing didn't come with a charger. Yours is in a drawer in San Francisco. She buys a new one on the way home.

In her apartment, she plugs it into the wall by the door. A small green light comes on. You start filling up again.

She doesn't know about the Inner Sunset garage, the 3:10 a.m. door, the shop in San Jose, the boxes, or the man who still checks your Bike Index page.

She's planning the ride to work on Monday.

On Sunday morning, Avenida Chapultepec is closed to cars for the Via RecreActiva, like every Sunday from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Families on bikes, kids on scooters, and someone selling fresh juice from a cart.

She rides you down the middle of the avenue, in no hurry.

You started at $8,000 new, in a foggy garage in San Francisco. You went for about $400 at 3 a.m. Now you're about $2,700 of somebody's savings in Guadalajara.

A kid drifts into her lane. Her thumb finds the bell.

It rings a little flat.

Under your frame, the serial number he photographed faces the street, where nobody is going to look.
