# Your Life as a Totaled Car

At 5:50 p.m. on Little Patuxent Parkway in Columbia, Maryland, you're an $11,000 sedan, radiator warm, one headlight at the curb, when the crash turns you into an 82 percent repair estimate in a 75 percent state. A minute ago, you carried a teacher's gym bag, a booster seat, and iced coffee. Her loan is paid, but Monday still needs a car. To the insurer, you're finished. You pass through seven hands, from a tow truck to a Copart yard, a Baltimore car ship, and a Cotonou lot. About 5,800 sea miles later, you sell for about $8,800 again. Just not to her.

## Hand 1: The librarian
Before anyone sweeps glass from the floor mat, your useful life turns into a number on a claim screen. In February 2026, your estimated value is about $11,000, based on age, mileage, and comparable cars. After the red light, that number stops being trivia. It becomes the ceiling on what she can get back. In March 2017, you leave a dealership in Columbia, Maryland, new and worth about $25,000, close to Kelley Blue Book's $25,109 early 2017 midsize average. Your first owner is a school librarian in her fifties. She uses you for school runs, grocery bags, library books, booster seats, and rainy afternoons. During your first week, her son gives her a small blue crab sticker. She presses it onto the left corner of your trunk lid and leaves it there through roughly 112,000 miles. The loan is paid off in 2022. That matters later. No lender is waiting for a balance. For nine years, you are simply useful.

## The crash
At 5:50 p.m. on Tuesday in February 2026, you sit on Little Patuxent Parkway with rain beading on the hood and traffic lights reflected across the road. Another car slides through the red light and strikes your front corner. Airbags pop. White dust fills the cabin. The radiator hisses, and your damaged headlight points at the curb. Both drivers reach the sidewalk and exchange insurance information. Nobody is injured. No ambulance arrives. At 6:05, your hazard lights blink through the rain while airbag dust settles on her coat. She calls her son and says you may be finished. Later, at the body shop or yard, she empties the glove box. The registration card comes out. So does a tire gauge and a library bookmark. The crab will not peel off, so it stays. Before the crash, you are worth about $11,000. Once the damage estimate begins, the same car becomes a possible total loss. Nobody is hurt, but the repair estimate may end your Maryland life. Now the question belongs to the insurer. A tow truck arrives, and its hook takes your weight.

## Hand 2: The tow driver
Later that evening, at a Maryland body shop lot, white security lights shine on rainwater running off the flatbed. You settle onto the pavement with your front corner crushed and your airbags hanging inside. The tow driver is working from the insurer's assignment. Copart says assigned vehicles are often picked up within 24 hours and taken to the nearest available yard, so you move about seven miles toward Laurel. Parts of your electrical system still have power, but you aren't driven. The driver uses the equipment needed to move damaged cargo safely. To him, you are an intake number with wheels. His phone records the front corner, deployed airbags, odometer, interior, wheels, and the blue crab sticker. Those images will later let distant bidders see you without touching you. He doesn't decide your final value. An adjuster will compare repair costs, nearby cars, the deductible, rental costs, and salvage value. For now, your actual cash value remains about $11,000, carrying a repair bill that may cross Maryland's line. That value matters, because Maryland only needs the repair calculation to pass roughly three quarters of it.

## The adjuster
An adjuster photographs you under cold lights and compares you with similar midsize sedans sold or advertised nearby. The adjuster uses nearby comparable vehicles. The owner's memory doesn't set the claim value. The repair estimate reaches about $9,000. Against an $11,000 value, that's roughly 82 percent before other claim costs. Maryland permits an insurer to call you a total loss when repairs exceed 75 percent of fair market value. New York also uses 75 percent. Iowa uses 70 percent. Texas uses 100 percent. California and Massachusetts use a total loss formula. Total doesn't mean the same calculation everywhere. The insurer pays about $10,500 after her $500 deductible and takes control of the title. Her loan ended in 2022, so gap insurance would only matter if a lender were still owed money. CCC Intelligent Solutions says 23.1 percent of auto claims were flagged as total losses in 2025, nearly one in four. A letter says total loss. The valuation lists three nearby cars. She signs the title over. You're worth $10,500 to her, while the insurer expects a separate salvage value. Your parts still work, and someone else is about to price them.

## Hand 3: The Laurel salvage yard
In Laurel, Maryland, about seven miles from the body shop, you sit under gray winter light among cars with missing noses, bent doors, and plastic taped over windows. Your front corner is crushed. Your airbags hang open. The blue crab stays fixed to your trunk, because apparently that sticker has more commitment than most trim pieces.

The yard becomes your waiting room while Maryland's paperwork catches up with your metal.

In Maryland, the Motor Vehicle Administration processes the title. Your salvage certificate arrives after the paperwork clears, and only then can buyers trust the file. Maryland records the salvage status, and the title history follows you.

The Justice Department says NMVTIS exists partly to prevent hidden salvage histories and title washing. Your title record follows you now, so the damage history is harder to hide.

The yard photographs every side. Damage, mileage, location, title status, crab. Your value falls to the salvage market, about $2,800 at auction.

Once that certificate arrives, the clock starts.

## The online auction
Your photos appear on an online salvage auction platform, clean and bright in the way bad news often is. Bidders are not standing beside you. They see the same crushed front corner, the odometer, the hanging airbags, the crab, and the documents.

This is not a side market. Copart sold more than 4 million vehicles in fiscal 2025, with 81 percent coming from insurers, to buyers in more than 165 countries. IAA sold about 2.45 million automotive lots in 2025.

The bids stop. A countdown begins. Under the auction's rules, the highest bidder gets the lot.

Copart reported that international bidders took part in more than 90 percent of auctions for cars expected to be rebuilt. Copart said 38.8 percent of its U.S. vehicles went to international buyers by IP address in fiscal 2025.

A dealer in Cotonou bids through a broker. The hammer falls at about $2,800. That is a typical constructed price for this story, not an average.

You were $25,000 new, $11,000 before the crash, $10,500 to your owner, and now $2,800. He is paying for a damaged car whose parts and labor may still leave room for profit.

Now Maryland has to let your body catch up with the sale.

## Hand 4: The Baltimore port
At the Baltimore port, cranes stand over sodium lights while a truck carries you about 20 miles from Laurel to the waterfront. The air smells like diesel, salt, and work that does not pause to be sentimental.

Baltimore was America's top car port for more than a decade until 2024. You enter a place already built to move vehicles in lines, lanes, and labels.

At the terminal, dockworkers sort cars by what they can do. A runner starts and drives. A tow rolls but will not start. A forklift needs lifting equipment. You are a tow. Your wheels still move, even if nobody trusts your engine.

You are pushed, towed, or dragged up the roll-on/roll-off ship's ramp. Then you are positioned and strapped to a deck. No container needed. The ship is basically a floating parking garage with better paperwork.

On this route, ocean freight, terminal fees, and broker charges become another line item, not a clean public number. The dealer has $2,800 in you before shipping, repairs, fees, and local duties.

Someone photographs your trunk again. The crab happens to be in the frame, but the photo goes into the export file.

The ramp rises under your tires, and the United States starts becoming a place behind you.

## The ship
Below deck, you are strapped among rows of vehicles, cooler and darker than the Maryland yard. The ship's vibration travels through your suspension. Nobody drives you now. The straps, the deck, and the ship's vibration do the moving.

NJ.com reported in 2023 that one car carrier's monthly route ran from Baltimore and New Jersey to Dakar, then Benin, Nigeria, Togo and Ghana. Baltimore to Cotonou by sea is roughly 5,800 miles.

The United Nations Environment Programme says about 14 million used light vehicles were exported worldwide from 2015 to 2018. Forty percent went to Africa. The United States supplied about 18 percent.

You remain a salvage-title vehicle during the crossing. You will not become a U.S. rebuilt-salvage car, because your next registration is meant to happen somewhere else.

IAA explains the basic math plainly. Lower parts and labor costs can make U.S. salvage vehicles worth rebuilding in Benin, Togo, Nigeria, and nearby markets.

Your $2,800 auction price is already about $4,100 with transport. Before duties. Before paint. Before a mechanic makes you useful again.

You reach West Africa before anyone spends what a Maryland body shop said repairs would take.

## Hand 5: The Cotonou mechanic
At Cotonou's RoRo terminal, you roll down the ramp into heat, dust, and bright coastal light. Maryland's gray yard is gone.

A mechanic receives you with a salvage title, deployed airbags, damaged front-end parts, and a body shell that still makes economic sense. He studies what can be repaired or replaced. Here, parts and labor matter more.

CNN has reported the practical pattern at Fifa Park. Imported accident cars arrive, get repaired, and return to the market for profit. A replacement bumper goes on. A headlight is fitted. The bent area is straightened, and silver-gray paint is matched by eye beneath workshop lights.

The blue crab stays on the left corner of the trunk. He paints around it, wipes dust from it, and leaves it alone. It has no effect on the repair bill.

With the $2,800 auction price and roughly $1,300 shipping behind you, the constructed landed-and-fixed value reaches about $7,000, before the dealer's margin and local registration costs. You are drivable again. Saleable again.

But the sticker still points to Maryland, unseen here.

## Hand 6: The dealer's lot near Cotonou
At Fifa Park in Cotonou, long rows of cars sit in intense sun. Some shine. Others remain dented. You join them with a blue crab on the trunk.

The dealer has roughly $7,000 in you, landed and fixed. He adds labor, registration, risk, and margin. The American settlement is finished, so the dealer prices you for buyers in Cotonou.

A Cotonou dealer's asking prices put a mid-size sedan around 5 million CFA francs for a 2015 or 2016 model, and 9.8 million for 2018 or 2019. At about 570 CFA francs to the dollar, 5 million is roughly $8,800. Those are asking prices, so they only show what sellers want.

Legal entry costs money too. Swiss public radio reported that registering a used car in Benin costs around 1,500 Swiss francs in duties, about $1,900.

East toward Nigeria, the lots thin into a route built around a bigger appetite. Nigeria is a large used-car market, though Nigeria Customs Service says vehicle imports through land borders have been banned since January 2017. Swiss public radio reported that many cars from these lots still end up there, much of it as contraband.

The dealer expects 5 million CFA. Your value has climbed from the auction floor to about $7,000 repaired, then roughly $8,800.

Now someone must choose you.

## Hand 7: The nurse
In the late afternoon, heat rises from the lot. Zemidjan riders in yellow shirts pass outside. They are Cotonou's moto-taxis; the name means take me quickly in Fon.

The buyer is a nurse who has used zemidjans to reach work for years. She checks the lights, doors, tires, trunk, and interior. Her questions are practical. What was repaired? Which papers come with the car?

The dealer quotes 5 million CFA francs, roughly $8,800, an indicative asking price.

In Maryland, the repair estimate decided your fate. In Cotonou, she thinks about transport costs, cheaper labor, and whether you can work every day.

She pays and receives the keys. You are used, repaired, and affordable. Your salvage history stays in the background. Your next job is ordinary.

The nurse drives away. You are now part of a family's route through southern Benin.

## The Route des Peches
On Sunday, the nurse drives south and west along the Route des Peches, the Atlantic road from Cotonou toward Ouidah. Palms, lagoons, and beach stretch beside the road.

Her mother sits beside her. The air conditioning works unevenly, and the repaired headlight catches the morning glare. A bag rests in the trunk, not library books.

At a stop, a child points at the small crab on your trunk. The nurse doesn't know it came from Maryland, or that her son gave it to the first owner in 2017.

In September, the first owner types the old VIN into a free insurance-industry check. The words total loss appear. An auction photo shows the hanging airbag, front corner, and crab sticker. The status says sold. She never learns about Cotonou.

You cost $25,000 new. In Cotonou, you sold for about $8,800.

Rain once gathered on your hood in Maryland. Now sunlight warms the same silver-gray trunk. The crab stays fixed, carrying people who never knew your first life.
