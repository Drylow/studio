# Recherche et consignes — Your Life as a Fake Designer Bag

Faits vérifiés et règles données au script.

VERIFIED FACTS for this video (use them, rounded; name the source lightly inside the sentence for big facts; do not invent other statistics or sources):
- How big US seizures are (CBP, IPR Seizure Statistics; values are "MSRP", the price if genuine, never what the fakes sold for): fiscal year 2025, about 78 million items seized, worth about $7.4 billion if real. Handbags and wallets: No. 1 by number of items in FY2024 (about 5.1 million), No. 3 by value in FY2025 (about $980 million), after jewelry and watches. China and Hong Kong: about 9 in 10 items seized in FY2024 and almost 93% of the value (USTR, citing CBP); about two-thirds of the items in FY2025. A typical day in FY2025: about $20 million seized (if real). From FY2013 to FY2019, almost 90% of seizures were in mail and express parcels (GAO, 2020); in FY2025, 62% of items were seized in containers, a shift CBP links to the end of duty-free small parcels ("de minimis", May and August 2025).
- The world (OECD and EU Intellectual Property Office, "Mapping Global Trade in Fakes 2025", May 2025; data for 2021): fakes were worth about $467 billion, up to 2.3% of world imports (up to 4.7% of EU imports, about $117 billion). China was the source of 45% of seizures. Clothing, footwear and leather goods are the most seized; almost 60% of seized items came by mail (2020-21). 13% of Europeans say they knowingly bought a fake in the past year, 26% of those aged 15-24 (EUIPO, 2023).
- Where many are made and sold wholesale (Guangzhou, China): Reuters (Oct. 2010) traced a fake bag to back-street workshops in Shiling, Guangzhou's Huadu district, China's biggest leatherworking town (most of its factories make legitimate bags): three-story shop-houses, women sewing leather by the windows, men hammering hardware in the alleys; then to the Baiyun World Leather market in northern Guangzhou, "the epicentre of the world's counterfeit trade", the size of five football fields. The US Trade Representative's 2025 Notorious Markets List (released 2026) still names the Baiyun Leather Trading Center, with "secret showrooms or a second shop upstairs". CCTV (June 2024) filmed showrooms hidden in apartment buildings next to the leather center, near Guangzhou Railway Station, behind heavy curtains. A crackdown there in June 2024: 17 cases, about 7,600 fake items seized; days later, touts still whispered "Buy a bag? High copy, in stock" (Jiupai Finance via Tencent News, June 2024).
- What a copy costs to make (estimates, always say who): a suspect in a Chinese police case told investigators a high-grade copy bag cost "one or two hundred yuan" to make, labor and leather included, about $15 to $30 (Liuzhou Evening News via The Paper, Nov. 2019). Jiupai Finance (June 2024) put the usual cost of high-grade copies at 300 to 500 yuan, about $40 to $70, rarely over 1,000 yuan. No reliable public figure for the wholesale price of a street-grade bag (Reuters' "about $100 each" in 2010 was for top copies): leave that hand without a number.
- Real prices: a New York Times Magazine investigation (2023) found better copies ("superfakes") sold online mostly for $200 to $300, while the real bags in its examples cost $2,200 and $10,200.
- JFK (Business Insider videos; CBP officers as sources only): the JFK International Mail Facility handles about 1 million packages a day, about 60% of international packages entering the US (2019). CBP narrows them down with intelligence, X-ray and dogs, then opens a few by hand. An officer who seizes a fake looks up the real one's price (MSRP), does the paperwork, and seizures go to a storage room; "in the end, all these products will be destroyed... incinerated at a top-secret location" (2021); a few may be donated if the brand agrees (2019; 19 U.S.C. 1526(e)). From seizure to destruction can take more than 120 days: a seizure notice, 30 days for the addressee to answer, a public forfeiture notice (GAO, 2020). Importing fakes for sale can bring a civil fine up to the real price (19 U.S.C. 1526(f)). CBP's New York director said 36% of all fakes seized in the US are seized at JFK and Newark airports (CBS New York, Nov. 2025).
- Where they are sold: Canal Street, Manhattan's Chinatown, thickest around Broadway and Lispenard Street (Tribeca Citizen, 2024). Street prices: a bag bargained from $130 to $70 (Village Voice, 2016); "from over $120 to half price" (The Student Life, July 2026). One street seller told NY1 (July 2026) he makes $25 to $30 a day and pays $600 a month for a room. Raids: Sept. 2023, 18 arrests, about $35 million (if real) on three flatbed trucks (ABC7); May-June 2026, more than 1,000 handbags taken from Canal Street sellers (NY1); a late-June 2026 sweep: about $150 million (if real), 17 people charged with felony trademark counterfeiting (amNY, NY Post); days later the crowds were back (Tribeca Citizen). Nov. 2023: federal prosecutors said a Manhattan storage facility was used "as a distribution center" for about 219,000 fakes, about $1 billion if real, "the largest-ever seizure of counterfeit goods in U.S. history" (US Attorney, SDNY; allegations, never name the accused).
- Who risks what: selling or importing fakes for sale is a federal crime, up to 10 years and $2 million fine for a first offense (18 U.S.C. 2320); buying one for yourself is not a crime under US or New York law (City Council proposals to fine buyers, 2011 and 2013, never passed). France: importing, carrying or owning a fake is a customs offence even for a traveler's own use: confiscation and a fine of one to two times the value of the genuine item (the real one's price); small traveler cases are usually settled on the spot, the fake given up (French customs, May 2026; Cour de cassation, 2019). Italy: the final buyer of a fake can be fined 100 to 7,000 euros (law of 2005, amended 2009), and since Feb. 2022 also for small parcels from outside the EU, fined by customs.
- Distances (computed, great-circle): Shiling to the Baiyun leather center about 22 miles (36 km); Guangzhou to JFK about 8,000 miles (12,860 km); JFK to Canal Street about 13 miles.
