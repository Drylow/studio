VERIFIED FACTS (each one said with its source in the sentence; rounded; estimates said as estimates)
- Home Office analysis: about 78,000 people in England and Wales had a phone or bag snatched on the street in the
  year to March 2024, up 153% on the year before. That is more than 200 a day.
- Metropolitan Police figures: about 70,000 phones were reported stolen in London in 2024.
- England and Wales, year to March 2024: only about 0.8% of "theft from the person" offences led to a charge;
  most were closed with no suspect identified.
- Metropolitan Police, Operation Echosteep: on Christmas Eve 2024 a woman tracked her stolen iPhone to a building
  near Heathrow; inside was a box of nearly 1,000 stolen phones bound for Hong Kong, almost all wrapped in tin foil.
  Police said the gang may have smuggled up to 40,000 stolen phones from the UK to China in a year (around 40% of
  all phones stolen in London). The operation led to 46 arrests.
- Same police source: street thieves got up to £300 per phone; stolen phones could sell for up to £4,000 (about
  $5,000) in China if they could be made usable.
- Thieves and handlers wrap phones in aluminium foil or Faraday bags to block tracking signals (same case).
- A phone still locked to its owner's account cannot just be wiped and resold whole: it is priced as parts,
  roughly 30% of its unlocked value. For a phone first sold for about $1,000 that is about $300.
- Heathrow to Hong Kong is about 6,000 miles, roughly half a day of flying.
- The Financial Times reported wholesalers in an industrial building at Hung To Road, Kwun Tong (Hong Kong),
  listing phones as "iCloud locked": locked is its own category with its own price.
- Invest HK describes Hong Kong as a free port with no customs tariff on imported goods.
- Gangs send fake "lost phone found" texts that imitate Apple's Find My, often to the contact number shown on the
  lost phone's lock screen; Apple's support pages warn that fake Apple messages push people to bogus sign-in
  pages. If the owner enters her password, the account lock can be removed and the phone sold whole.
- The Financial Times described Huaqiangbei, Shenzhen's electronics district, as a common final stop for stolen
  phones; the Feiyang Times building has been nicknamed the "Stolen iPhone Building". In one reported case a London
  entrepreneur tracked his stolen phone from a London repair shop to Hong Kong, then to Huaqiangbei.

STORY SHAPE (second person: YOU are the phone; 8 pairs of hands; part titles "Hand N: ...")
- Hook: 9:17 pm on a Saturday, outside the Screen on the Green cinema on Upper Street, Islington. You are warm in
  her hand, 81% battery; a bike comes from behind. One second. Over the next weeks 8 pairs of hands will price
  you, wrap you, box you and fly you from London through Heathrow and Hong Kong to Shenzhen; at the end, unless
  one fake text works on her, you are worth about $300 in pieces. She paid £999.
- Hand 1: Her (late 20s, coat damp at the cuffs). An hour earlier, face up on a small café table on Upper Street
  beside a paper cup; photos, messages from Mum, a banking app, a takeaway order, a half-written reply she keeps
  not sending; one hairline scratch near the camera; blue dot moving toward Angel station. The snatch.
- Hand 2: The rider. E-bike, side roads of Islington, you in his jacket pocket; distance first, thinking later;
  missed call lights the screen, he doesn't answer. Back on Upper Street she stares at her empty hand, calls you
  from a friend's phone, tracking from an old tablet; the dot becomes unreliable. Home Office numbers.
- Hand 3: The handler. Back room under fluorescent light, foil rolls, pouches, labels, a tub of phones. Checks:
  locked? lost mode? contact number on the lock screen? Powers down, wraps, sorts by model and condition. Foil /
  Faraday bags. Locked vs unlocked, damaged vs clean: locked = parts, about 30%, about $300. The lock-screen
  number gets copied for later. Next morning: her crime reference number, passwords, cards, insurer; 0.8%
  charge rate.
- Hand 4: The exporter. Warehouse in West London near Heathrow: cardboard, pallet wrap, tape gun; the Christmas
  Eve 2024 box of nearly 1,000 foil-wrapped phones bound for Hong Kong; up to 40,000 a year; 46 arrests. The rider
  got up to £300 for you; everyone after him wants more.
- Hand 5: Cargo. Heathrow cargo handling, a box among other phones, a boring label, conveyor hum, no signal,
  about 6,000 miles to Hong Kong. Locked, about $300 in parts; unlocked and whole, up to £4,000 in China.
- Hand 6: The wholesaler. Kwun Tong, Hong Kong: lift doors, fluorescent corridors, small offices, trays, a
  scale; categories (locked, unlocked, cracked, clean). FT "iCloud locked"; free port.
- Twist (still Hand 6 part): weeks later her new phone shows a fake "lost device found" message. She pauses, checks
  separately, deletes it. Your value stays low: the $300 parts lane.
- Hand 7: The parts trader. Huaqiangbei, Shenzhen: white bench lights, trays of screens and camera modules,
  Feiyang Times building. You are split: screen, camera module, chips, battery.
- Hand 8: The student. In another city, a narrow repair stall with a glass counter; he picks up a repaired phone
  that uses your screen. It lights up for a timetable, a chat, a map with a blue dot on a street that never heard
  her name. The faint scratch near the corner catches the light. Ending: £999 phone full of one woman's life →
  about $300 in parts → one part gives another device a second life.

RULES
- Never a how-to: never explain how to bypass a lock, block tracking step by step, or run a scam. Say what happens,
  not how to do it. Never glamorize the thieves; serious about her loss, quiet dry humor about humans.
- Real places and sources named in the sentence; no real private person named; no brand logos in pictures.
- No meta lines ("this story is constructed"); part titles "Hand N: ..." for the 8 hands only.
- The phone never has a face: its mood is what happens to it (screen lit or dark, foil, box, parts tray).
