# Your Life as a Donated T-Shirt

At 8:12 on a Saturday morning, behind a charity thrift store's donation door in South Philadelphia, a gloved hand lifts you from a trash bag, orange cotton first. A minute ago, you were her 2018 Turkey Trot shirt, then her painting shirt, then her sleep shirt. She calls this charity. Soon you're priced at $4.99, miss a buyer, get pressed into a 1,000-pound cube, and fall to about 9 cents. By the end, nine hands carry you more than 5,000 miles to Accra, where someone pays about 25 cedis. On the store system, the donation becomes inventory.

## Hand 1: The runner
Thanksgiving morning, November 22, 2018, you are new along the Schuylkill River, bright orange against cold air and gray water. Boathouse Row slides past in pieces of light. You are cotton-poly, size L, with a cartoon turkey in running shoes across your chest. Very dignified.

Your first owner is a woman in her late thirties from South Philadelphia. She runs her first 5K beside her father, who keeps checking his watch like time can be encouraged. Her bib, number 2147, is pinned through you at four corners. Four tiny holes open in your chest.

The race costs about $30, close to RunSignup's 2024 average 5K entry of $30.48. The shirt feels free, because humans are generous with math. Scripps News later reported about 1.1 million people ran one of 936 turkey trots in 2024.

Afterward, you become souvenir, painting shirt, sleep shirt, moving-day fabric.

The four pinholes stay. Back at the donation door, nobody cares.

## Hand 2: The donation door sorter
April 26, 2025, the donation door again. Fluorescent light sits flat on everything. The back room smells like wet cardboard, dust, and someone else's garage. Hangers scrape metal in short little bursts.

The sorter wears gloves and moves fast. He doesn't know the Schuylkill River, the father, the first 5K, or six years of sleep. You arrive with household overflow. Jeans, a fleece blanket, mismatched shoes, a mug wrapped in newspaper.

Your owner gets a blank receipt marked for records. If she ever uses it, the IRS says donated clothes generally need to be in good used condition or better, valued at thrift-store value. The receipt lands in a kitchen drawer under takeout menus and loose batteries. It will probably live there forever.

The sorter checks for obvious stains and damage. Your print is cracked, but wearable. The pinholes are tiny. You pass.

Your value here is zero to the donor, unknown to the store, and sentimental to nobody in the room. The back room keeps taking bags anyway.

## Hand 3: The thrift-store pricer
You move from a rolling cart to a hanging rack under bright lights. A pricing gun clicks nearby, quick and final.

The pricer has quick wrists, color stickers, a black marker, and a practiced glance for holes, stains, and season. You get a $4.99 tag. That fits the Goodwill of Southern New Jersey and Philadelphia May 2024 guide, where men's short-sleeve shirts are listed at $4.99 and below.

A color sticker marks your week. The Seattle Times described a similar Goodwill system in the Seattle area, where each item had a color tag and roughly a four-week life on the floor.

This is your last normal American chance. Adam Minter told NPR's Fresh Air in 2019 that the average US thrift store sells only about one-third of what ends up on its shelves.

The color sticker gives you about four weeks.

## The shoppers who don't buy you
You hang through four weeks of fluorescent mornings. Cart wheels rattle past. People slide hangers, pause, squint, and keep going.

A shopper touches your sleeve. Another reads TURKEY TROT 5K 2018, smiles once, and pushes you back. Event shirts are strange objects. They prove someone else did cardio six years ago.

Your tag color gets cheaper each week, like the Seattle-area Goodwill pattern. First you are $4.99. Then half price. Then less, at least in the private math of bargain shoppers who still don't choose you.

Your first owner is gone from the room, but her absence matters. Nobody here knows why there are four little holes in your chest. Nobody knows bib 2147. Nobody knows her father slowed near the finish so they could cross together.

The rack doesn't know either. By the end of your tag week, staff need that hanger for newer donations.

When your floor clock runs out, staff pull you. In Seattle Times language, unsold clothes can be ragged off to an outlet, and after that become salvage.

Your value is no longer a retail price.

Now you become pounds.

## Hand 4: The outlet and salvage baler
At the outlet, the room is louder than the sales floor. Carts knock together. Bins fill with clothes. Hands sort by the pound instead of by memory.

Some pieces are sold in outlet stores by weight. Others have already missed their last chance at a rack. Those become salvage, the lowest American stage before export processing.

Seattle Times reporting found textiles pressed into cubes weighing about 1,000 pounds. Dealers paid roughly 27 cents per pound for them.

The Or Foundation estimates about three T-shirts weigh a pound. So at this stage, you are worth around 9 cents.

A $30 race entry became a $4.99 thrift tag. The $4.99 became nothing when nobody bought you. Now the turkey, the pinholes, and six years of laundry are priced like a loose coin under a car seat.

You are compressed with clothes that had owners, drawers, odors, arguments, and laundry rules. The press sorts for material and weight.

A bulk buyer wants light, wearable cotton because it fits export categories.

But who buys a thousand-pound cube of unwanted clothes?

## Hand 5: The northern New Jersey grader
The cube reaches a used-clothing grader in northern New Jersey. Inside, conveyor belts rattle under warehouse lights. Plastic straps snap. Bins carry labels like men's shirts, wiping cloths, winter wear, and damaged goods.

The grader has no reason to pause over your turkey. A sorter gets only a brief glance to judge fabric, condition, gender, and market.

SMART, the trade association for the textile-recycling industry, describes this as a sorting and grading business. Used clothing is assigned a category, then resold, mostly overseas.

Its broad industry split is roughly 45 percent reused as clothing, 30 percent made into wiping cloths, 20 percent turned into fiber, and 5 percent unusable.

Elizabeth Cline wrote that one New Jersey grader sorted close to 17 million pounds a year into about 200 categories. At least half were called the bad and the ugly.

You avoid the wiping-rag bin because you're wearable, bright, and light. The cracked print helps. So do the tiny holes.

You land in a men's T-shirt export bin. Your value is still measured in pennies, but now the category decides which ocean you cross and who carries the risk later.

## The bale
The loose shirts move beneath a bale press. In this load, hundreds of shirts are packed together, including you, until the machine makes one hard sealed export bale.

The pressure removes the air first. Then it removes the space between every shirt. Plastic wrapping goes around the outside. Straps hold everything tight.

The label says men's T-shirts. The buyer still takes the sealed-bale risk.

Greenpeace Unearthed reported bales in Ghana at about $160 to $250 in 2025. Spread across roughly 350 to 400 shirts, your rough share is still under a dollar.

The buyer pays for the sealed bale and takes the risk on whatever is inside.

Donors hand over bags because it feels better than throwing them away. Thrift stores try the sales floor first. Graders make the export categories. By the time the bale is sealed, the market trader is the one carrying the risk.

You are still priced in pennies here.

The retailer who will risk money on you still hasn't seen you.

## The container
By late summer 2025, you are inside a 40-foot container at Port Newark. Bales fill the metal space. Diesel vibration comes through the floor. There is no daylight.

The route to Tema is about 5,140 miles in a straight line. By sea, it takes weeks, depending on the freight route.

Ghana imports some American worn clothing. The bigger suppliers are elsewhere. Bloomberg called Ghana the world's biggest importer of used clothing in 2020.

World Bank WITS data from UN Comtrade shows that Ghana imported about $93 million in worn clothing in 2023. The United Kingdom led with about 35,000 tonnes. China and Canada followed, with the United States at roughly 5,000 tonnes.

In Ghana, secondhand clothes are often called obroni wawu. The Twi phrase is commonly translated as dead white man's clothes. Decades ago, when these garments first arrived, people assumed the former owners had died.

After the crossing, you reach Tema. A truck carries you toward Accra's business district and Kantamanto, just west of Accra Central railway station.

Your value is buried in bale pricing. Your next test begins when the straps come off.

The thrift-store part is over. Kantamanto is a major market, with porters, pressers, tailors, retailers, and repairers moving the weight.

## Hand 6: The head porter
Thursday, September 4, 2025, Kantamanto opens with new bales.

TIME has reported that Thursday is bale day here. The market already sounds awake. Voices, carts, scissors, irons, radios. Traders call across narrow lanes. Tailors sit near machines. Pressers wait for pieces worth saving. Dyers handle color like weather.

The Or Foundation estimates about 15 million garments arrive here every week. More than 30,000 people work around this market. About 25 million pieces a month are recirculated through resale and repair.

Then a young woman reaches for your 55 kg bale.

She is a kayayei. The Or Foundation translates it as she who carries the burden. TIME has reported many kayayei are young women from northern Ghana.

She lifts you onto her head.

New Lines reported bale carries often pay about 7 to 12 cedis. This one earns her about 10.

You feel motion for the first time in weeks.

Your resale price is still unknown.

The paperwork calls the bale clothing stock. In the lane, people make room by inches, because a 55 kilogram bale leaves no extra space.

## Hand 7: The retailer
The bale stops at a stall run by a woman who has traded here for 12 years.

Her old stall burned in January, so she starts again with fresh timber, donated roofing sheets, and a ledger wrapped in plastic. Kantamanto had to rebuild after the fire on Thursday, January 2, 2025.

Ghana National Fire Service said it started at 1:18 a.m. The cause was never established.

The Or Foundation said more than 60% of the retail side was destroyed. At least 10 of 13 sections burned. More than 10,000 people were affected. The foundation committed $1 million at once.

One year on, Ghana News Agency reported trading had returned, but slowly. Everyone was out by 6 p.m. Fire officers worked three shifts.

The Or Foundation's Dead White Man's Clothes report puts many Kantamanto bales in a rough $75 to $500 range, bought sight unseen. If this one lands near the middle and holds about 400 shirts, your rough cost is about 70 cents.

She cuts the straps.

First selection. Second. Third. Under.

The Or Foundation's survey found an average bale is about 18% first, 30% second, 46% third, and 6% under. It also found 46% of retailers did not make back their money on their last bale.

This is the part where the shirt stops being funny, because the wrong bale can cost a trader real money.

## Hand 8: The presser and the waste pile beside you
She lifts you out.

Orange cotton. Cracked turkey. L tag. Four pinholes still on the chest.

She checks the armpits, collar, seams, and print. The holes are tiny. They do not matter.

You make first selection. Clean enough, bright enough, strange enough to catch a buyer's eye.

A stained shirt beside you does not.

The Or Foundation's two-year study put bale waste around 40%, and traders told TIME it can be 30 to 40%, which is a brutal number when every shirt was bought blind. Accra waste chief Solomon Noi told ABC Australia close to 40% can be complete chaff. AFP put the city's bill around $500,000 a year. Business Insider, TIME, AFP and Greenpeace have traced leftovers from the Old Fadama dump to Korle Lagoon, Chorkor beach, and the Densu Delta wetland.

Still, Adam Minter has noted many African buyers prefer secondhand quality. Liz Ricketts told Business Insider the goal was never to cancel the trade, but to make it better.

A presser smooths you with a coal-heated iron.

Here, she writes 25 cedis on your price, about $2.30. No source lists today's price for one shirt; this is an estimate from 2025 bale prices.

The presser improves your presentation, and the unsellable shirts beside you move toward waste.

## Hand 9: The new owner
Saturday, September 13, 2025, a young man who repairs phones stops at the stall.

He notices the orange first. Then the ridiculous turkey.

He laughs at TURKEY TROT 5K 2018 without knowing the Schuylkill River, the father, the $30 entry, or bib number 2147.

He pays 25 cedis.

A $30 race entry. Zero at donation. A $4.99 thrift tag. About 9 cents as salvage. About 70 cents inside the bale. Then 25 cedis in Accra.

On Thanksgiving morning, November 27, 2025, your first owner runs the same race with her father in a new shirt. She wants a then-and-now photo. She digs through drawers, remembers the donation bag, and stops looking.

It is 1 p.m. in Accra. You are drying on a line.

In January 2026, she scrolls past a headline about Kantamanto one year after the fire. She does not stop. She has no idea.

The next Saturday at 6 a.m., he wears you with a neighborhood keep-fit club along the road.

Orange cotton moves with his breathing. The cracked turkey catches the morning light. Four tiny holes still sit where her bib used to be.
