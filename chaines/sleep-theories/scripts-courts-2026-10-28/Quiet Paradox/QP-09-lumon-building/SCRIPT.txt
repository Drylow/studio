For Mark's innie, that same arrangement begins with a job he can't remember accepting. He has no shared married life with Gemma to mourn, yet he receives the hours his outie has chosen to skip. Gabby's childbirth arrangement offers a different kind of relief: the self returning to family life need not remember labor.

At the gala, the senator's wife speaks about using severance for childbirth. Her account presents an appealing outcome for the self who doesn't retain the experience. Unlike Mark's recurring office life, the separated experience here is tied to childbirth. The self who remembers the family afterward is spared the labor that another self lived through.

Gemma's crib test brings a further possibility into view. In the Cold Harbor room, she dismantles a crib without the emotional response that the object is meant to provoke. Her history with Mark includes their efforts to have a child and the pain surrounding that part of their marriage. Lumon's test places an object connected to that history in front of a version of Gemma who can perform the assigned task without carrying the same association into it. Dr. Mauer watches for the response while she continues working.

The proposed suffering-avoidance product would depend on exactly that kind of separation. Mark's office life removes him from remembering a loss during particular hours. One self avoids remembering the childbirth experience. Gemma's test examines whether a painful association can remain unavailable while another self handles the object connected to it. Taken together, these situations suggest a service that could assign difficult experiences to designated selves. That's a commercial hypothesis, rather than a sales plan Lumon has confirmed. Cold Harbor gives it a technical basis within the story.

The crib also reveals a difference in what Lumon might offer. In the childbirth arrangement, an experience still happens to the body although one self does not retain it. Gemma's test concerns an encounter whose pain depends on personal history. Dismantling the crib requires ordinary physical actions, and the Cold Harbor self can carry them out. The difficulty Lumon is testing lies in what those actions might mean to Gemma. A company able to separate that meaning could promise customers access to activities or surroundings they otherwise find unbearable, without resolving the history that makes them painful.

Gemma hasn't asked Lumon to provide that service. She's being held and tested, and her wish to leave has no place in the procedure's definition of success. That matters when considering what a successful demonstration would actually deliver. Mauer can be satisfied with her response to the crib while Gemma's broader circumstances remain intolerable. Mark's innie and Gabby raise the same concern through different arrangements: the self spared an experience can't be the only person whose experience counts.

So the theory fits what Lumon can already do, with a cost its demonstrations tend to place elsewhere. Mark receives hours away from grief. Childbirth remains outside one self's remembered life. The Cold Harbor self works on the crib without Gemma's painful associations. In each case, a person is present during the interval another self doesn't have to bear. Lumon's possible promise of relief would still leave that person inside the room, living through the experience until someone switches them away.

At the severance gala, Jame Eagan speaks to the woman he believes is his daughter about his coming revolving. The word suggests a transition, and for the Eagans, succession is already part of the company's public story. Employees can walk through that history as part of their working lives, inside a company that also gives its founder's writing the force of instruction. Jame's eventual departure would enter a family narrative Lumon has spent considerable effort maintaining.

That's the setting in which the immortality theory takes shape. Perhaps severance research could give an Eagan some continued existence beyond an ordinary lifespan, or prepare a person to carry a founder's identity. Through Cold Harbor, though, no transfer of Jame's mind, clone, preserved Kier body or recovered Kier consciousness appears. Revolving remains unexplained family language. The theory is worth considering because Jame connects his own approaching transition with a project that already changes how a person experiences being alive, and because Lumon makes the preservation of Kier unusually central to its work.

Kier's doctrine reaches employees long before they have much freedom to judge it. His account of the four tempers gives Lumon a vocabulary for describing human nature. His words fill the handbook, and managers appeal to his teachings when they correct behavior. In the Perpetuity Wing, the reconstruction of his home brings the founder's domestic life into the workplace. The outdoor retreat at Woe's Hollow extends that process. Milchick uses the Fourth Appendix and the story of Kier's brother Dieter to frame the employees' experience. A company outing becomes an occasion for entering another piece of the founder's account of himself. The practical consequence is clear enough: even away from their desks, the refiners are expected to understand their surroundings through Kier. If the Eagans want their founder to endure through repeated teaching and imitation, the severed workforce gives them an audience with very little competing personal history available.

But those employees don't develop into interchangeable followers. Helly resists the workplace almost immediately. Dylan finds reasons to act that grow out of his attachments, and Irving's devotion to Lumon doesn't prevent him from challenging the people who run it. These are awkward results for any project that depends on producing a predictable personality. Lumon can limit what its employees remember and surround them with Kier's doctrine. The people inside those arrangements still form loyalties and judgments that management hasn't supplied.

Jame's encounters with Helly in the second season make the family side of this more interesting. A family interested in shaping how its members experience their lives could have reasons to value that capacity, even without any means of extending a body's lifespan.

The simpler explanation also has concrete support in the gala itself. Helena lends her name and body to a public campaign for severance. So a durable Eagan future fits the world we've been shown, with literal immortality remaining the speculative part. The Perpetuity Wing preserves the succession, the handbook carries Kier into daily work, and Helena's campaign seeks a wider public for the technology. Jame's interest in Helly adds a more personal possibility, that severance could help the family search for qualities it wants its descendants to carry. Mark gets Gemma to the exit, and then he turns back toward Helly. By then, he's done the thing his outie needed him to do. Gemma has been found, she's been brought out of Cold Harbor, and there's a route away from the testing floor. Yet the Mark standing inside Lumon doesn't follow her through the door. He remains where his own experience can continue. The rescue brings two people close enough to leave together, while the division inside one of them makes leaving mean something different to each.

For Gemma, Mark is her husband. Her life with him exists beyond the rooms where Lumon has kept her, and reaching him means the possibility of returning to that life. The man who brings her out has her husband's face and can help her escape. But innie Mark doesn't have their marriage available to him as a life he's lived. He knows Gemma matters to his outie. He can understand what her captivity has cost. That understanding doesn't supply the shared experiences that would make their reunion feel like his own return home.

The episode gives that difference room before the rescue. Through recorded messages, Mark's outie and innie address each other about what they need. The outie wants help getting Gemma back. The innie has to consider what happens to him afterward. Their exchange gives the rescue a difficulty that physical access alone can't settle. They occupy the same body, so either one's future depends on what the other agrees to do. They can explain themselves across the division, although neither can hand over the life that makes his request feel necessary.

Outie Mark's request has enormous force. He's learned that the wife he believed dead is alive inside Lumon. Helping her is an immediate responsibility, and his innie is in a position to reach her. Still, that request asks the innie to cooperate with a future in which his own continued existence has no secure place. If Mark leaves Lumon with Gemma and never returns, the innie's life stops receiving any more time. For someone whose entire experience has happened on the severed side, that prospect reaches far beyond giving up a job.

Helly belongs to the life the innie is afraid of losing. She's someone he has worked beside, someone who has resisted the conditions they were given, and someone with whom he's formed an intimate attachment. His outie can't draw on those experiences as his own, any more than the innie can recall being married to Gemma. That leaves both Marks asking for consideration of a person the other hasn't loved through the same encounters. Their shared body gives them an urgent reason to cooperate, without giving them a shared emotional starting point.

Helly also helps make the rescue possible by encouraging Mark to go after Gemma. Her place in his life doesn't require him to abandon the woman downstairs. Innie Mark can care about Helly and still act on what he's learned about Gemma's captivity. He enters the testing floor and gets Gemma out. Those actions matter when he later turns around. He's already accepted a risk for someone whose connection to him comes largely through his outie's life. His refusal to leave with her follows an act of help that he chose to carry through.

Inside Cold Harbor, Gemma has been made to dismantle a crib without the emotional response associated with her life outside that room. The task depends on a separation holding. Mark's arrival interrupts the situation in which Lumon has placed her, and his effort gets her moving toward an exit. There's a practical difference between the test ending and Gemma becoming free. Someone still has to find her, accompany her through Lumon's spaces, and bring her to the boundary. Innie Mark does that work even though he can't experience its meaning in the same way she does.

Gemma has already recognized Mark outside Cold Harbor, but the ascent reactivates Ms. Casey. Crossing the final exit restores Gemma again, while innie Mark remains inside. She recognizes her husband in the man who brought her there, though he cannot remember their marriage. The doorway lets Gemma recover the connection she's been denied while keeping Mark's side of that connection out of reach. They're close enough to see one another, and their responses belong to different histories. For Gemma, the person she's trying to reach is right in front of her.

That arrangement makes the ending more difficult than a decision between two romantic partners. Outie Mark's marriage and innie Mark's attachment to Helly have developed in lives Lumon has kept apart. Each Mark has someone he wants to reach, and neither can continue living on his own without the body they share. Gemma hasn't consented to losing her husband to this division. Helly hasn't created it either. Both women are caught in the consequences of a procedure that allows the same face to meet them with different recognition.

Lumon's achievement therefore remains present throughout the escape. The boundaries work well enough to keep the innie's experience separate at the moment Gemma most needs her husband. The same arrangement has also given Mark repeated encounters with Helly, time to care about her, and reasons to want another day as himself. The company can arrange where a consciousness becomes active. Its experiment has allowed that consciousness to acquire concerns that interfere with the rescue's expected outcome. Mark's return toward Helly is a decision made by someone Lumon has helped bring into being.

The final movement leaves Gemma at the door and takes Mark back into the corridor with Helly. Gemma has escaped the testing room, but she hasn't recovered the life with her husband that reaching him seemed to promise. Mark and Helly run together through the place where their relationship became possible. For the Mark who turns back, there's a person waiting whom he recognizes through his own lived experience. He takes her hand, and Gemma remains beyond the door, calling to the husband whose face she's just seen.
