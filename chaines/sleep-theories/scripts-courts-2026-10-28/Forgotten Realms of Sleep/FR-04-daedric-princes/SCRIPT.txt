At Azura's shrine in the Jerall Mountains, the Hero of Kvatch brings glow dust as an offering and approaches at dawn or dusk. Azura answers with a task concerning worshippers who haven't returned from a mine. An offering has led to an obligation, and the people waiting at the end of it belong to her. We'll meet the Daedric Princes through encounters like this, through the bargains mortals accept, the realms they enter, and the changes those encounters leave in ordinary places.

When Azura speaks, she describes five followers who went to destroy vampires in the Gutted Mine. They succeeded in killing the vampire Dratik, but contracted vampirism themselves. After recognizing what had happened, they sealed themselves inside. The journey that began as service to Azura ended with her worshippers cut off from the world outside. Their devotion hadn't protected them from infection. It did shape their response to it, because they chose confinement rather than return to the lives they'd left.

Azura asks the Hero to enter the mine and end their suffering. That's the obligation attached to this particular audience. The Hero isn't sent to recover a stolen treasure or settle an argument among the people at the shrine. The task concerns followers whose earlier service has left them in a condition they couldn't accept. Azura presents their deaths as a release, and the Hero must decide whether to carry out what she asks. Answering a Prince can mean taking responsibility for the unfinished consequences of someone else's devotion.

The mine gives that request a location small enough to understand. Cyrodiil has roads, settlements, farms and mines scattered beyond the walls of its cities. A place where people once worked can become shelter for vampires, and an expedition sent to clear it can become trapped there in turn. The Gutted Mine's danger has a history by the time the Hero arrives. Dratik is already dead. Killing him didn't remove the infection from those who confronted him, so the problem Azura wants resolved has outlasted the enemy they went there to destroy.

If the Hero accepts and completes the task, the worshippers inside must be killed. The game doesn't provide a successful version of this commission in which the Hero escorts them back to the shrine. That gives the encounter an uncomfortable weight even without dwelling on the fighting. The people in the mine were able to recognize their condition and take action to protect others. By the time the Hero reaches them, their decision to remain secluded has become part of the situation the visitor must enter.

Returning to Azura completes a journey between two very different places of worship. At the shrine, Mels and the other followers can gather openly beneath their patron's statue. In the mine, the afflicted worshippers have continued their service through withdrawal from the outside world. Azura thanks the Hero and says their spirits are free. That assurance comes from Azura herself. For the surviving followers at the shrine, their Prince has answered a loss within their own community through the work of a passing stranger.

The reward is Azura's Star, a reusable soul gem. Ordinary soul gems are consumed when their stored souls are used for enchantment or recharging enchanted equipment. The Star can be filled and used again. Its usefulness makes the reward easy to appreciate within the Hero's traveling life, where enchanted equipment needs replenishment and carrying supplies matters. After a task involving the release of Azura's followers, the Hero receives an object that can hold souls. The connection is striking, although Azura doesn't offer an explanation of that pairing.

This is also where a Daedric encounter begins to extend beyond its original setting. The Hero can leave the mountains with something that remains useful far from the shrine. Mels doesn't have to accompany them, and the Hero doesn't have to live among Azura's followers to carry her gift. A traveler who arrived with glow dust can depart with an artifact associated with a Prince. The exchange has brought the Hero into the affairs of a religious community, then placed a lasting reminder of that service among their possessions.


An Altmer reader opening Aedra and Daedra encounters a distinction built into the names themselves. The book translates Aedra as "our ancestors" and Daedra as those who aren't their ancestors. Those terms belong to an Elven account of where the world and its peoples came from. Before anyone reaches a shrine or asks a Prince for a favor, the language has already placed that encounter within a family history. Someone raised in another tradition may recognize the beings while understanding their place in creation quite differently.

The same book connects the Aedra with the making of the mortal world. They contributed to its creation, and that involvement changed what they could be. The Daedra, in this account, didn't take part in making it. That's the broad distinction behind the familiar names, though the word ancestor carries a stronger meaning for Elves than it does for the Empire's human worshippers. An Imperial praying to Akatosh needn't believe that the god occupies exactly the same ancestral position described by an Altmer religious writer.

The Monomyth gathers creation traditions that give this distinction a setting. Its accounts begin with a condition before the ordinary world, when the spirits who would become gods hadn't yet acquired all the separations familiar to mortals. The text discusses Anu and Padomay, and the emergence of spirits within the tension between them. These are religious explanations of how existence began. Their language moves through stillness, change, and the discovery of identity because there isn't yet a Tamriel with roads, kingdoms, or people to describe.

In the Altmeri account included in The Monomyth, the spirits learn to persist as themselves. Some grow stronger and more distinct, and recognizable divine figures emerge. Lorkhan eventually proposes the creation of a mortal world. That proposal gives the story its difficult turn. A world with its own existence requires something from the spirits who undertake it. Creation consumes their substance, and the participants discover that their undertaking has consequences they can't all escape. The ancestors of the Elves become bound up with the world their descendants inhabit.

Magnus has a particular place in this telling. He's associated with the design of the world, but withdraws when the cost of completing it becomes apparent. Other spirits depart as well. In this tradition, the sun is associated with Magnus's departure, while the stars are connected with the spirits who followed him. An Altmer looking upward can therefore understand the sky through an account of beings leaving creation. The lights above Tamriel belong to the same story that explains why mortal existence is limited.

Others remain. The Altmeri account describes Earthbones and the establishment of the conditions under which the world can exist. Y'ffre is associated with this giving of form and stability, a connection especially important in Bosmeri religion. The tale then follows a descent toward mortal ancestry, with later generations diminished from those before them. This helps explain the sorrow running through the Elven version of creation. Life in the world carries an inherited distance from an earlier divine condition, and Lorkhan bears much of the blame for bringing that condition to an end.

The human traditions collected in The Monomyth give the undertaking a different meaning. In its Cyrodilic account, Shezarr speaks in favor of making the world, and the spirits who agree put themselves into the work. They become connected with what they've created and care for it. Here, the expenditure involved in creation can be understood as generosity. The same broad event that an Altmeri account treats as a disastrous diminishment becomes the basis for a relationship between gods and the mortal beings who live within their creation.

That Cyrodilic story also describes spirits who refuse the undertaking. They keep their power and make realms of their own. It associates this refusal with the Daedric Princes, whose dealings with mortals develop after the mortal world exists. This gives a worshipper a way to explain why a Prince can possess an extraordinary realm without being counted among the makers of Mundus. In that account, the Prince's realm belongs to a separate act of creation, undertaken without the same sacrifice attributed to the Aedra.

Lorkhan's fate brings the argument into a place a traveler can actually reach. In the Altmeri telling, the other gods punish him, and his Heart is cast away. In Morrowind, the Heart of Lorkhan lies beneath Red Mountain. The Dwemer sought to use it, and the Tribunal drew divine power from it. Whatever a reader makes of the creation accounts surrounding Lorkhan, the Heart has material consequences for Vvardenfell. Religious history reaches into the mountain, where living people have tried to turn a divine remnant toward their own purposes.

The Heart doesn't settle every disagreement about why the world was made. Its presence does help explain why these stories remain so immediate to the people telling them. A Dunmer living under the Tribunal encounters rulers whose divine status depends upon something connected with creation itself. An Imperial pilgrim may approach the gods through a tradition of sacrifice and continued care. An Altmer can understand mortal limits through an account of ancestral loss. Each begins with a world inhabited by the same peoples, yet arrives at a different account of what life there means.

Aedra and Daedra adds another useful complication. It describes Aedra as bound to the Earth Bones and associates Daedra with a capacity for change. It also distinguishes the mortality of the Aedra from the possibility of banishing Daedra. Those claims help explain the religious categories without turning them into a division between kindly beings and malicious ones. Ancestry and participation in creation are the concerns of the terminology. A mortal still has to consider what a particular being asks of them, and what accepting that request will do.

For someone standing in Tamriel, creation is therefore present in ordinary acts of worship. The name spoken at an altar carries an account of who helped make the world, who withdrew, and who remained outside the undertaking. The books preserve disagreements because the people of Tamriel inherited different explanations of that past. A Prince can enter a mortal's life without belonging to the ancestral company described by Elven religion. That mortal already lives within Mundus, with all its limits, before the Prince makes any offer at all.


At Malacath's shrine in Cyrodiil, the trouble concerns ogres working in Lord Drad's mine. Drad treats them as labor he can possess. Malacath wants them freed. A traveler who accepts the task enters a dispute in which a wealthy landowner's business depends on captivity, while a Daedric Prince demands the captives' release. That can be an awkward introduction for anyone expecting the respectable estate and the Daedric shrine to occupy predictable sides of a moral divide. The ogres have a more immediate concern. They need someone to open their cages.

Malacath's instruction includes a condition that matters to the creatures inside the mine: the traveler mustn't kill the ogres they're supposed to rescue. Completing the task leaves the ogres free, and the balance at Drad's estate changes. Drad and his wife can afterward be found working under the ogres' supervision. The reversal carries Malacath's taste for retribution as well as his concern for those who were mistreated. His intervention helps particular victims, and it also gives their former owners a share of the humiliation they imposed.

The religious book Varieties of Faith places Malacath among the gods acknowledged by the Orcs, and associates him with the ostracized and the sworn oath. Those associations help explain why this mine attracts his attention. The ogres belong within his concern, and Drad has made them vulnerable to someone else's demands. Calling Malacath a protector makes sense here, provided we keep the people he's protecting in view. An Orc who honors him and a noble who has enslaved his creatures have very different reasons to care about his arrival.

Morrowind gives Malacath another public identity. In Tribunal religious teaching, he's one of the Four Corners of the House of Troubles, alongside Mehrunes Dagon, Molag Bal and Sheogorath. These Princes occupy a hostile place in the Dunmer religious world. Their dangers are understood through a tradition in which the Dunmer face tests and enemies. An Orc's reverence for Malacath therefore doesn't translate neatly into approval from a Tribunal priest. The same named Prince appears within communities that have inherited different stories about him and different expectations of his followers.

Even the layout of Morrowind makes that disagreement tangible. Daedric ruins stand across Vvardenfell, and worshippers may occupy their interiors. A traveler approaching one of these places can encounter hostility rather than the welcome available at a town's Tribunal temple. Yet danger at a ruin doesn't explain every person's reasons for worshipping there. The island also contains people whose devotion to a Daedric Prince belongs to established Dunmer religious life. Azura, Boethiah and Mephala have recognized places in traditions that Tribunal teaching itself has had to accommodate.

Molag Bal's shrine in Cyrodiil draws a traveler into a very different intervention. He directs attention toward Melus Petilius, a former fighter who has renounced violence after his wife's death. Melus has withdrawn from his old life, and Molag Bal wants that renunciation broken. The Prince's task requires provoking Melus at his wife's grave until he kills the petitioner with the supplied cursed mace. Molag Bal then restores the petitioner, rewarding a service that has broken Melus's renunciation of violence. Someone accepting this bargain helps a Daedric patron force an unwilling man back into conduct he's deliberately abandoned.

The reward belongs to the person who cooperates with Molag Bal. Melus bears the intrusion. That arrangement is worth keeping distinct from a shrine where worshippers ask for relief from an existing danger. Here, the Prince creates a task out of another person's grief and restraint. His interest in domination takes a recognizable form: he wants Melus to violate his own commitment. The traveler isn't asked to discover whether Melus secretly needs a fight. His refusal to fight is precisely what Molag Bal wants overcome.

These encounters explain why a sphere, the domain associated with a Prince, can't tell a traveler everything about the next request. Malacath's concern for the outcast produces a rescue at Drad's mine. Azura's concern for her followers produces an act of release that those carrying it out may find sorrowful. Molag Bal's interest in domination produces an assault on Melus's chosen way of living. Each domain gives the intervention a recognizable direction. The actual people involved tell us what accepting the task will cost.

Varieties of Faith also describes Azurah within Khajiiti religion, where she has a place in accounts of the Khajiit's origins and their proper form. That setting differs from a Dunmer account of Azura, even though the connection is recognizable. A Khajiit honoring Azurah isn't necessarily approaching her through the Tribunal's categories, and an Orc honoring Malacath isn't borrowing the Dunmer House of Troubles. Worship reaches a mortal through family, community and inherited practice. Before anyone hears a voice at a shrine, those traditions may already have taught them whether approaching it is an act of reverence or a dangerous departure.

A traveler can therefore recognize compassion in the freeing of Malacath's ogres while still examining the next demand on its own terms. Melus's grave gives an equally concrete reason to refuse Molag Bal. Neither decision requires settling every religious dispute in Tamriel. At the shrine, the immediate work is to understand who's asking, who needs help, and who will suffer if the bargain is accepted. The Prince's name matters. So does the person waiting in the mine, or standing beside his wife's grave.


In the Cavern of the Incarnate, the Nerevarine finds people who came before them and failed. Their spirits remain beside the path to Azura's ring, Moon-and-Star. Each had some part of what the prophecy seemed to require, yet none completed the task. Some lacked the strength or judgment to survive it. Others reached the wrong conclusion about their own importance. Azura's promise has already occupied mortal lives by the time the player arrives, and those lives haven't all ended in deliverance.

The ring gives this encounter a particular weight. Moon-and-Star belonged to Nerevar, and the traditions surrounding it say that anyone else who wears it will die. When the player takes it and survives, Azura names them the Nerevarine. The failed incarnates then offer their possessions and speak about their failures. They help someone who can continue where they couldn't. Their presence makes the prophecy feel like something that has passed through individual people, each with limitations, rather than a prediction waiting untouched for its appointed moment.

Azura has already reached the player through dreams. Early in Morrowind, her voice offers reassurance while the prisoner is still traveling toward an unfamiliar country. Later dreams bring Dagoth Ur's invitations and threats into that same private space. The journey involves learning which speaker is approaching and what each wants. Azura points the player toward a task that will take them through Ashlander camps, Tribunal institutions, and the blight beneath Red Mountain. Her guidance doesn't spare them the work of meeting people who have reasons to doubt them.

Nibani Maesa, the wise woman of the Urshilaku, is especially important here. She doesn't accept the newcomer as the finished answer to her people's prophecies. She studies what they bring and explains the trials still ahead. The Nerevarine must earn recognition among communities with their own disputes and expectations. Azura's intervention in the cavern gives the player a divine acknowledgment, but that acknowledgment doesn't make the Ashlander wise women or the leaders of the Great Houses unnecessary. The promise has to become credible within the country it's meant to change.

Behind that promise lies Nerevar's death and the Tribunal's use of the Heart of Lorkhan. In the Ashlander account preserved in Nerevar at Red Mountain, Azura witnesses an oath concerning Kagrenac's tools and the Heart. The Tribunal later use those tools to make themselves divine. That tradition ties their betrayal to the transformation of the Chimer into the Dunmer and to Azura's announcement that Nerevar will return. The change in their skin and eyes becomes part of a remembered religious dispute, carried by descendants who weren't present at Red Mountain.

The accounts of Nerevar's death disagree, including over the Tribunal's responsibility. What the player encounters in the present is more immediate. Vivec acknowledges the Tribunal's dependence on the Heart, and Dagoth Ur's return has prevented them from renewing their power as they once did. Their strength is diminishing while the blight spreads. Azura's old accusation now belongs to a situation in which the living gods need the person their Temple has treated as a threat. Vivec eventually gives the Nerevarine information and tools needed to reach the Heart.

That meeting allows the task to proceed without requiring the player to settle every disputed detail of the ancient battle. Vivec explains how Wraithguard protects its wearer from Kagrenac's tools and describes the blows needed to break the enchantments around the Heart. The Nerevarine must enter Dagoth Ur's stronghold and carry out that work. Azura has guided them toward this confrontation, while the means of completing it pass through a member of the Tribunal. The people caught in the broken oath still have practical knowledge the returning hero needs.

When the Heart is freed from those enchantments and Dagoth Ur falls, Azura appears to the Nerevarine. She acknowledges the fulfillment of the prophecy and gives them a ring. The blight has ended, and the Tribunal can no longer draw power from the Heart. Her promise has reached a recognizable conclusion, though Vvardenfell still contains the people and institutions shaped by everything that preceded it. The Nerevarine has accomplished the task through travel, persuasion, endurance, and help from mortals whose contributions remain their own.

In Cyrodiil's Gutted Mine, Ghola gro-Muzgol's note gives Azura's afflicted followers their own account of the infection and their decision to remain shut away. If the Hero fulfills her request, Azura thanks them for releasing those followers and grants her Star. Beside the Nerevarine's work against the blight, this smaller commission shows how her stated care can still require a mortal to carry out a sorrowful act.


At Boethia's shrine in Cyrodiil, a willing challenger can be sent away from Tamriel to compete in a tournament. The other contestants have come to prove themselves as well. There are rules, opponents and a promised reward, and the traveler enters knowing that the contest is lethal. In Skyrim, someone approaching Boethiah's shrine encounters a different requirement. Before the Prince's favor can be pursued, a companion must be brought to a place of sacrifice. Both encounters concern proving oneself, though the people who bear the cost arrive under very different circumstances.

In Oblivion, the shrine's worshippers explain that an offering of a Daedra heart is needed to approach Boethia. That requirement gives the meeting a deliberate beginning. A traveler has to obtain the offering and bring it to a community already gathered around the Prince. Once addressed, Boethia offers the opportunity to enter the Tournament of Ten Bloods. Accepting means leaving through a portal to an arena in Oblivion, where the challenger will face the other competitors.

The tournament draws its contestants from the different peoples of Tamriel. Within the arena, the traveler proceeds through successive opponents, each seeking the same victory. Their participation gives this trial a recognizable shape. They aren't residents whose village has been chosen for punishment, or companions brought along under another explanation. They are rival champions. Boethia has arranged a contest in which martial ability can be compared directly, and the challenger who survives returns having met the terms announced at the shrine.

Victory earns Goldbrand, a sword associated with Boethia. The reward suits the work that secured it: someone who has prevailed through fighting receives a weapon for further fighting. There's no release from the Prince's favored activity contained in the prize. Goldbrand accompanies its bearer back into Tamriel, where the successful contestant can continue to use what the tournament tested. For this encounter, Boethia's interest is easy to follow. The shrine offers a dangerous opportunity, the traveler accepts, and the contest produces a winner.

Boethiah's Calling in Skyrim begins around a cult whose members also expect strength to be demonstrated. At the Sacellum of Boethiah, east of Windhelm, the priestess describes a test requiring someone who trusts the traveler. That person must be persuaded to accompany them to the shrine and use its Pillar of Sacrifice. If the traveler follows this demand, the companion becomes trapped at the pillar. The instruction depends on the ordinary cooperation that makes traveling together possible.

A follower can carry out a request because they recognize the Dragonborn as someone they're willing to accompany. At the pillar, that familiar arrangement exposes them to betrayal. They haven't entered a tournament after hearing its terms. The Dragonborn has brought them into a ritual whose purpose wasn't shared with them. This makes the Skyrim demand more personal than the Cyrodiil contest. The participant's ability to secure another person's cooperation becomes part of the offering, and the companion pays for having cooperated.

If the sacrifice is completed, Boethiah speaks through the dead follower. The gathered cultists then receive a further test: they must contend with one another until a single survivor remains. Belonging to the shrine's community gives them no exemption. The priestess who explained the ritual and the worshippers who accepted its demands are themselves subject to the Prince's judgment. Their gathering cannot provide lasting shelter from the competition they honor. In this encounter, devotion brings them close enough to be tested, with consequences that reach the whole group.

The survivor is then sent after Boethiah's existing champion, who has displeased the Prince. This champion is at Knifepoint Ridge among bandits, and the task leads the Dragonborn away from the shrine to displace someone who already holds the position being offered. The Ebony Mail belongs to that champion. Once the task is fulfilled and the armor claimed, Boethiah recognizes a replacement. The former favorite's fate gives the new champion a concrete example of how service can end, even after someone has already earned the Prince's favor.

The sequence also changes what victory means across the two games. In Cyrodiil, winning the tournament completes the announced competition. At Knifepoint Ridge, success puts the Dragonborn into an office another mortal has just lost. The armor is useful, but wearing it also identifies the successful claimant with Boethiah's service. The Prince has assessed the cultists, rejected a champion and selected another. Someone accepting the reward has participated in that process from the sacrifice onward.

Dunmer religious writing places Boethiah within a much older account of how a people should live. In The Anticipations, the Tribunal Temple names Boethiah as the anticipation of Almalexia. The book credits Boethiah with teaching the Chimer and associates the Prince with their departure from the ancestral traditions of the elves. Within that religious account, conflict with an inherited way of life belongs to the people's beginnings. Boethiah is remembered as a teacher whose lessons helped make a distinct culture possible.

Varieties of Faith likewise gives Boethiah a broad place in Chimer tradition, including associations with philosophy, warfare and the establishment of their society. These accounts help explain why a Dunmer worshipper might approach the Prince with reverence. The worshipper has inherited stories about instruction and collective achievement alongside stories of conspiracy and betrayal. A shrine visitor in Skyrim meets one cult's immediate demand. A Dunmer reading these religious histories encounters a patron woven into explanations of where their people's customs came from.

Those traditions give Boethiah's trials a religious setting without making the sacrificed companion a willing contestant. The distinction remains there in the actions themselves. Cyrodiil's challenger enters an announced tournament. Skyrim's follower obeys someone they have agreed to accompany, while the cultists discover that their own lives are also available for testing. At Knifepoint Ridge, the previous champion has already lost the patron's approval. If the Dragonborn takes the Ebony Mail, they leave wearing the armor of a servant Boethiah ordered them to replace.


Beneath the Arena in Vivec, the Morag Tong has a headquarters that a visitor can easily miss. Reaching it takes the player away from the city's public walkways and into its lower passages. Inside, Eno Hlaalu receives those who want to join an organization devoted to sanctioned assassination. Mephala belongs to the religious life of this guild. Here, her association with murder has an established place in Dunmer society, complete with ranks, assignments, and documents that members can present to the guards.

A Morag Tong writ names someone the guild has authorized its members to kill. If a guard confronts the assassin over an authorized killing, presenting the appropriate writ can settle the matter. That arrangement gives the guild an unusual position within Morrowind. The same authorities who pursue an ordinary murderer can recognize a Tong execution as lawful. For the person named on the document, the distinction offers little comfort. For the assassin, it determines whether the journey back through a city ends in arrest.

Joining the Tong begins with a killing as well. Eno Hlaalu sends the applicant after Feruren Oran, who can be found at the Elven Nations Cornerclub in Vivec. The assignment brings the guild's work into a place where people gather and conduct their ordinary affairs. Once accepted, the player receives further writs against named individuals. The work requires finding particular people within the settlements and households of Vvardenfell. An assassin has to learn where someone belongs before carrying out the commission against them.

The book The Black Glove describes the Morag Tong's worship of Mephala and presents the guild's killings as religious acts. It's the guild's own account of its purpose, and that devotion matters to how its members understand their work. A writ can carry civic permission while the execution also carries sacred meaning for the person performing it. Mephala's worship has therefore become part of an institution whose business passes through both government and religion. The guild keeps its headquarters concealed, even though its authority can be acknowledged in public.

The Tong's conflict with the Dark Brotherhood adds another connection to this religious setting. In Morrowind, the player can undertake work against the rival organization, and Eno Hlaalu has concerns beyond the names on routine writs. The guild protects its own position and pursues enemies within the same profession. That gives service to Mephala a practical daily shape. Members receive instructions from a superior, advance through a hierarchy, and act on behalf of an organization with rivals. Their devotion comes with obligations to other mortals who can judge their performance.

In Skyrim, Mephala reaches into a household through a much smaller opening. At Dragonsreach, Balgruuf's son Nelkir has been listening to a voice behind a locked door. The trouble comes to the player's attention through reports about the Jarl's children, and Balgruuf can direct the player toward his son. Nelkir describes the Whispering Lady as someone who tells him things. Among them is a claim about his parentage. The game gives that information through the boy's account of what the voice has said, so it remains part of the confidence she's cultivated with him.

Nelkir's interest is understandable within the situation. He's a child in the Jarl's residence, surrounded by adults whose conversations and decisions aren't all open to him. The voice offers access to household secrets and encourages his resentment toward his father. Mephala has found a listener who lives close to the person governing Whiterun. She doesn't need Nelkir to hold an official position. His place in the family gives her a way into Dragonsreach, and his curiosity brings another person to her door.

When the player speaks at the door, Mephala directs them toward the Ebony Blade locked beyond it. Nelkir knows that Balgruuf and the court wizard, Farengar Secret-Fire, have keys. Following that information means taking a key from someone within the residence and opening a space the household has kept shut. The quest draws the player through a short chain of confidences. Balgruuf asks for help with his son, the son shares what he's learned, and the hidden speaker uses that access to arrange the weapon's release.

The chamber contains a written warning about the blade. It describes efforts to destroy the weapon at the Skyforge and urges that it remain concealed. The sword has already caused enough concern for someone to shut it away and leave an explanation beside it. By the time the player reaches it, the Whispering Lady has supplied a different reason to enter that room. She wants the blade taken up again. The warning and Mephala's invitation meet in the same enclosed place, with the player able to read one while considering the other.

The Ebony Blade absorbs health from those it strikes, and Mephala explains how its enchantment can regain strength. It feeds on the deaths of people who regard its wielder as a friend. That condition ties the weapon's improvement to relationships the player has formed elsewhere. Someone helped through a quest, or won over through a favor, may qualify because of that goodwill. The invitation gives an ugly use to work that previously made a person's life easier. Mephala asks the wielder to turn an existing bond into nourishment for her artifact.

Taking the sword doesn't require the player to carry out that further demand. They can leave its enchantment at its initial strength and decline to feed it through betrayal. If they do pursue its improvement, Mephala responds as the blade grows stronger. The bargain is specific enough that its consequence is difficult to mistake. The weapon rewards the destruction of friendly ties, and its patron encourages the wielder along that course. Nothing about finding the chamber makes those later deaths an unavoidable part of the story.

Eno Hlaalu hands an assassin a document naming an authorized target. The voice in Dragonsreach sends a visitor toward a sword that rewards turning on friends. These are different encounters with Mephala, shaped by the mortals and customs around her. In Vivec, her worshippers have built a guild that gives killings a recognized procedure. In Whiterun, her influence passes through a child's confidences and a stolen key. Once the door is open, the player can carry the Ebony Blade back into a city full of people they've already helped.


In Raven Rock, Elder Othreloth tends a temple where Azura, Boethiah and Mephala receive public worship. A visitor arriving from the mainland might associate Daedric shrines with remote places and small gatherings outside ordinary civic life. Here, the temple belongs to the settlement's daily religious arrangements. Its priests care for the dead as well as the living. The Princes honored inside have a recognized place in Dunmer tradition, and Othreloth explains that place through a change that has already overtaken the religion of Morrowind.

He calls them the Reclamations. The name refers to Azura, Boethiah and Mephala recovering the standing they held before the ascendancy of the Tribunal. For a long period, the public religion of Morrowind centered on Almalexia, Sotha Sil and Vivec, three beings worshipped as living gods. By the time the Dragonborn reaches Raven Rock, the temple teaches that these former rulers were false gods. Othreloth speaks as a priest of the restored faith, with a clear judgment about the religious order that came before his own.

That judgment would have sounded very different inside the Temple encountered in Morrowind. In that game, Vivec still inhabits his palace, pilgrims travel between sacred sites, and priests teach a faith organized around the Tribunal's continuing presence. The pilgrimage described in The Pilgrim's Path takes worshippers to places associated with divine deeds and Dunmer history. A pilgrim makes offerings and receives blessings. The Tribunal's standing is built into journeys across Vvardenfell, into the work of its priests, and into the stories people are expected to know.

Azura, Boethiah and Mephala haven't vanished from that older Temple's teaching. The Anticipations gives each a place in relation to a member of the Tribunal. Azura anticipates Sotha Sil, Boethiah anticipates Almalexia, and Mephala anticipates Vivec. Within this account, their earlier teaching prepares the way for the gods the Temple now worships. The book allows the Temple to acknowledge the Princes' importance to the ancestors of the Dunmer while placing the Tribunal above them in the religious order. Someone reading it encounters an explanation for why older patrons still deserve recognition.

This arrangement also distinguishes the three Princes from the figures the Temple identifies as the House of Troubles. Religious texts place Mehrunes Dagon, Molag Bal, Malacath and Sheogorath in that adversarial group. Their opposition and the hardships associated with them have a place in Temple teaching, too. Dunmer religion therefore doesn't gather every Daedric Prince into one shared position. A priest can revere Mephala's ancient instruction while describing another Prince through stories of danger and testing. Even under the Tribunal, the particular identity of the Prince matters.

Outside the Temple's approved account, the Ashlanders preserve another understanding of the past. Their wise women carry traditions concerning Nerevar, the Tribunal and the return promised through the Nerevarine prophecies. In Morrowind, the Nerevarine must deal with these communities directly, including Sul-Matuul and Nibani Maesa among the Urshilaku. These encounters reveal religious disagreements within Dunmer society while the Tribunal Temple still holds public authority. An Ashlander camp and a Temple establishment can share names from the sacred past and tell sharply different stories about what those names mean.

The Dissident Priests bring another dispute inside the Temple's own world. At Holamayan, Gilvas Barelo preserves and explains writings that challenge the approved history. During the main quest, those records help the Nerevarine investigate claims that established religious authorities have rejected. This matters to the later Reclamations because the Tribunal's account was being contested before Raven Rock's priests pronounced it false. Doubt had specific keepers and specific places of refuge. It survived in manuscripts, in conversations with priests, and in traditions passed down beyond the Temple's usual reach.

The events beneath Red Mountain then change the material circumstances of Tribunal worship. The Nerevarine breaks the enchantments binding the Heart of Lorkhan, ending the source from which the Tribunal renewed their divine power. Vivec explains his dependence on that source during the main quest. After Dagoth Ur's defeat, the Tribunal can no longer maintain their old position in the same way. Azura appears to the Nerevarine at the conclusion of that struggle. The patron whose prophecies the Temple resisted has an immediate presence in the outcome.

Raven Rock's temple belongs to the religious world that follows this decline. Othreloth presents the return to the three Princes as a recovery of proper worship. From his position, the Reclamations have resumed their rightful place after the Tribunal's deception. The name itself carries that claim of restoration. Yet the earlier arrangements remain visible in the texts and encounters of Morrowind. A reader can follow how Azura moves from the Temple's account of an anticipation to the central place she holds in the later temple, without treating those two settings as interchangeable.

The Tribunal also remains within the later religion, though its position has changed. Almalexia, Sotha Sil and Vivec are remembered as saints rather than worshipped as the ruling gods of the Dunmer. That leaves room for their historical and religious significance to continue after the rejection of their divine claims. For a community inheriting generations of Tribunal devotion, this is a substantial adjustment. The names attached to its older sacred stories survive, while the priests teach a different account of who deserves worship and how the former gods should be regarded.

Othreloth's responsibilities make the restored religion tangible. In Raven Rock's temple quest, ash spawn have entered the ancestral tomb, and he needs someone to clear them out. If the Dragonborn helps, the service addresses a problem in the community's burial place. The priest's concern reaches beyond explaining which divine beings have precedence. The dead require care, and the living need access to a place where their ancestors are honored. Under the Reclamations, that familiar Dunmer obligation continues. Raven Rock's restored faith has a tomb to tend, and Othreloth is responsible for tending it.


At Kvatch, an Oblivion Gate stands outside a city that its surviving residents can no longer enter safely. In Oblivion, however, the consequences reach people who haven't asked for any transformation. Kvatch's residents lose their city because a movement devoted to Dagon has worked to admit his forces into Tamriel. The distance between a cult's promised new world and the experience of the people already living there is visible in the refugee camp.

Passing through the gate takes the player into the Deadlands, Dagon's realm of Oblivion. Lava divides stretches of scorched ground, and towers rise above an environment hostile to a mortal visitor. The tribe would hear about a victory achieved by the chief while the person who actually accomplished it remained silent.

The encounter can proceed differently depending on the player's response. If the Dragonborn refuses to do Yamarz's work, he attempts to fight the giant and is killed. If the Dragonborn agrees and defeats the giant, Yamarz turns against the outsider to keep his arrangement secret. That route also ends with his death. These alternatives give his final failure different forms. In one, he can't survive the challenge he was ordered to undertake. In the other, his effort to preserve his reputation leads him to attack the person who made his apparent success possible.

After the giant leader is defeated, the Dragonborn can recover Shagrol's Warhammer. Malacath addresses the survivor and directs them to take it back to Largashbur. His response leaves no need to maintain Yamarz's proposed account of the expedition. The Prince knows the chief has failed, and the return to the stronghold carries that judgment home. Atub, who began by seeking help for her people, receives news of Yamarz's death along with the recovered weapon. The danger at the shrine has been addressed, but the settlement must now continue without its chief.

Malacath names Gularzob as the new chief and instructs the Dragonborn to place the warhammer upon the shrine. There it becomes Volendrung, the Daedric warhammer given as the reward for completing the task. The stronghold's curse is lifted. For Largashbur, the result includes a replacement for Yamarz and the restoration of its relationship with its patron. Atub's appeal has brought a severe answer, followed by a way for the community to continue. Gularzob remains among the Orcs as their chief, while the outsider can leave with Volendrung.


Mercer Frey has taken something that belongs beneath a temple, and the damage reaches the thieves working in Riften. The Skeleton Key is Nocturnal's artifact, an unbreakable lockpick with powers beyond opening an ordinary lock. Its proper place is the Ebonmere, a conduit between her realm of Evergloam and the Twilight Sepulcher. When Mercer removes it, he breaks the arrangement he was sworn to protect. Karliah connects that theft to the Thieves Guild's failing fortunes, giving its troubles an explanation that reaches beyond bad jobs and empty coffers.

She takes Brynjolf and the Dragonborn to Nightingale Hall, where the Nightingales' connection to Nocturnal becomes explicit. They are a small, secretive circle associated with the Guild, pledged to defend her shrine and the Ebonmere. Gallus, Karliah and Mercer had belonged to that circle. Membership in the Thieves Guild alone doesn't carry the same oath. The merchants, burglars and other members working out of Riften haven't all entered the agreement that Karliah now asks her companions to accept.

If the Dragonborn continues through this part of the Guild's story, Karliah leads an induction before Nocturnal. The oath carries service beyond mortal life: a Nightingale is bound to guard the Twilight Sepulcher after death. Brynjolf has to consider that prospect alongside the immediate need to stop Mercer. Nocturnal accepts the new arrangement, though her response gives little comfort to Karliah. The Prince expects the stolen Key returned. Becoming a Nightingale places the Dragonborn inside an existing obligation, with the earlier betrayal still unresolved.

At Irkngthand, Mercer demonstrates why the Key is more dangerous in his possession than an unusually good lockpick would suggest. Karliah explains that it can unlock potential as well as physical barriers, and she connects Mercer's extraordinary abilities to his misuse of it. The pursuit leads to the great statue holding the Eyes of the Falmer. If the Dragonborn completes the confrontation, Mercer dies, the chamber floods, and the survivors escape with the recovered Key. The Guild's betrayer is gone, while Nocturnal's property still needs to reach its destination.

Septimus Signus lives beside a sealed Dwemer lockbox in an outpost north of the College of Winterhold. The object occupies his attention so completely that conversation keeps returning to what might be inside it. He believes it contains the Heart of Lorkhan. That belief gives his work an extraordinary purpose, though the contents will turn out to be something else. By the time the Dragonborn finds him, Septimus has already accepted help from Hermaeus Mora, and his search has become part of the Prince's business.

The Dragonborn has a practical reason to consult him. Septimus knows how to reach an Elder Scroll beneath Skyrim, and he supplies a blank lexicon and an attunement sphere for the journey. His directions lead through Dwemer ruins into Blackreach, then to the machinery in the Tower of Mzark. There, the Scroll can be released while the lexicon receives an inscription. For someone following the main quest, the Scroll is the immediate concern. Septimus wants the information brought back in the smaller device.

When Septimus examines the inscribed lexicon, his problem becomes more specific. The lock requires Dwemer blood, which he cannot obtain. He proposes creating a substitute by combining blood from the surviving elven peoples. If the Dragonborn continues his work, the next collection includes Altmer, Bosmer, Dunmer, Orsimer, and Falmer blood. His scholarship now asks the traveler to bring back material taken from bodies. The quest's change of errand is plain enough: recovering an inscription has led to gathering the ingredients for a mechanism that imitates an absent people.

Hermaeus Mora addresses the Dragonborn through a dark, hovering manifestation at the outpost. He identifies himself as the power behind Septimus's pursuit and presents the scholar as someone whose usefulness is nearly exhausted. Septimus is still speaking about his discovery as the goal of his life's work. His patron is already discussing a successor. The Dragonborn can answer Mora with resistance or receptiveness, but the encounter makes clear that Mora's interest reaches beyond the contents of the lockbox. He's assessing the person who has made the expedition possible.

Once the blood mixture opens the lock, Septimus enters and finds the Oghma Infinium. His expected discovery has vanished in the presence of an actual one, and he has only a brief opportunity to recognize the discrepancy before he's reduced to ash. Mora then offers the book to the Dragonborn. The scholar who spent himself pursuing the sealed chamber doesn't leave with its treasure. The traveler who completed his work receives an artifact that can increase their abilities through reading, with its benefits directed toward different kinds of skill.

On Solstheim, the Black Books give the same patron a wider reach. These volumes can carry a reader into Apocrypha, Mora's realm, where books fill immense structures and Seekers and Lurkers threaten visitors. Reading becomes a physical crossing into a place with passages to navigate and dangers to survive. A volume found in a ruin can therefore alter what happens far outside its resting place. Someone opens a book on Solstheim, and Mora gains an opportunity to meet that reader within his own domain.

Mora's demand for the Skaal's secrets shows what he still wants from outside his library. He offers the knowledge the Dragonborn needs in exchange for traditions kept by Storn Crag-Strider's people. Storn agrees because of the danger facing Solstheim, despite his distrust of the Prince. When he reads the Black Book, Mora takes the knowledge from him, and Storn dies. Frea witnesses the loss of her father. The bargain reaches a village whose guarded teachings matter enough to Mora that he makes their surrender a condition of help.

Mora's collection therefore includes knowledge maintained through living people, with consequences for those left behind. Septimus supplies labor toward opening a box. Storn yields teachings preserved among the Skaal. The Dragonborn receives usable rewards and further demands. These encounters suggest a reason for Mora's continued bargaining: even a realm crowded with books has things its ruler wants mortals to bring him. At Skaal Village, that acquisition leaves Frea without her father, while the knowledge he protected passes into Apocrypha.


Dervenin waits in Solitude with a problem that sounds, at first, like a servant's complaint. His master has gone away, and he wants someone to bring him back. Following that request takes the Dragonborn toward the Blue Palace, where the abandoned Pelagius Wing sits within a working royal household. Elisif's court carries on elsewhere in the building. The disused rooms belong to another history, associated with Emperor Pelagius Septim III. Sheogorath enters this ordinary city through a servant, a locked part of a palace, and an errand whose meaning changes once someone agrees to it.

Inside the wing, the journey breaks away from the building. The Dragonborn arrives at a strange outdoor table where Sheogorath is speaking with Pelagius. The quest places this encounter within Pelagius's mind. Ordinary equipment is unavailable, and Sheogorath supplies the Wabbajack for the work he demands. Asking him to return with Dervenin becomes a negotiation over his holiday. He'll leave after the Dragonborn deals with the difficulties he presents. The visitor who came to retrieve someone now has to take part in the Prince's arrangement.

Pelagius's troubles appear as separate situations that can be approached and changed. In the arena associated with his suspicions, the obvious contest draws attention toward fighting creatures. The answer instead concerns the figures watching the contest. Sheogorath has built a problem in which following the most conspicuous action doesn't settle anything. The Dragonborn must recognize that the surrounding audience belongs to the situation too. It's an encounter designed around redirected attention, with the Wabbajack giving the visitor a way to alter what initially looks fixed.

Another part of the mind gives Pelagius's confidence and anger visible bodies. His confidence is small, while the anger facing it is much larger. The Wabbajack changes their proportions, allowing the Dragonborn to strengthen one and diminish the other. This is the quest's own fantasy language for Pelagius's distress.  Sheogorath has turned an internal struggle into a confrontation whose participants can be resized by a Daedric staff.

The sleeping Pelagius presents a different task. Disturbing figures arrive around him, and the staff can transform them into harmless replacements. Each change allows the sequence to continue until his sleep is no longer surrounded by those threats. The Dragonborn's actions bring the local task to an end, and Sheogorath agrees to depart. Dervenin gets the return he wanted. The traveler returns to the palace with the Wabbajack, having passed through an experience that the ordinary rooms of the Pelagius Wing couldn't have suggested on their own.

Haskill receives the traveler in a small room beyond the strange door in Niben Bay. He has a desk, a manner suited to official business, and questions about whether the visitor intends to proceed. The entrance to the Shivering Isles therefore includes something like an interview. When the room gives way around the traveler, the wider realm appears beyond it. That transformation is extraordinary, but Haskill's role remains recognizable. Someone has to receive arrivals, explain the invitation, and direct them toward the work their host expects them to perform.

The first inhabited stopping place is Passwall, a settlement within the Fringe. Arrivals haven't yet reached unrestricted passage through the realm. The Gatekeeper blocks the Gates of Madness, and the people nearby have their own responses to its presence. Some travelers attempt to fight it and fail. Jayred Ice-Veins studies a way to overcome it, while Relmyna Verenim has a much more personal connection to the creature she made. Before the visitor reaches Sheogorath's court, passage already depends on local knowledge and on a being assigned to guard a boundary.

The Gatekeeper's defeat opens access to the routes into Mania and Dementia. These names describe the major divisions of the Isles, with different appearances and different political households. Mania has brightly colored growth and extravagant vegetation. Dementia has darker wetlands and a more enclosed atmosphere. People live, travel, trade, and serve within both regions. The distinction affects the surroundings a traveler crosses, but it also reaches into government. Sheogorath's realm has offices attached to these divisions, and those offices come with rivals, servants, and expectations about succession.

New Sheoth gathers that government into a city. Bliss is associated with Mania, while Crucible belongs to Dementia. Their streets contain homes and businesses, so reaching the capital also means entering places where residents have practical concerns. Cutter works as a smith in Crucible, and the Missing Pauldron supplies armor in Bliss. Merchants need materials and customers. Residents have complaints about their neighbors. The city gives the visitor a setting in which Daedric strangeness continues alongside employment, property, and the need to obtain something useful from another person.

Above these districts, the palace contains Sheogorath's court and the households of the Duke of Mania and Duchess of Dementia. Thadon holds the first office, and Syl holds the second. They serve beneath Sheogorath, but each maintains a separate position within the realm. Meeting them introduces obligations that can't be reduced to the Prince's passing remarks. Their servants know their habits, their households have internal dangers, and their titles can be transferred through recognized rituals. A newcomer is stepping into an arrangement that existed before the invitation arrived.

The offices of Mania and Dementia become especially concrete when Sheogorath allows the traveler to replace one of their rulers. The routes differ. Taking Thadon's place involves his ritual and the lethal use of greenmote. Taking Syl's place involves pursuing her and obtaining her heart. These are conditional alternatives, each with its own participants and consequences. Whichever route is taken, the title comes through a process the court recognizes. Sheogorath's approval matters, and so does the prescribed act that makes the succession acceptable within his realm.

Order begins appearing in the Shivering Isles as something its residents fear. Crystals emerge, Knights of Order advance, and familiar places become sites of attack. At Passwall, the settlement where the traveler first waited beyond the strange door, the danger reaches people who had seemed part of the realm's entrance. Sheogorath calls this recurring destruction the Greymarch. He knows its approach and has sought a champion to interfere with it. The preparations that brought the visitor into his service belong to an attempt to prevent a catastrophe he has experienced before.

Yet Sheogorath's own presence becomes increasingly uncertain as the crisis develops. His explanation places him inside the recurring event. When the decisive change comes, he disappears as Sheogorath and becomes Jyggalag, the Prince of Order. The threat isn't merely another ruler arriving at his border. The identity that has governed the Isles gives way to the identity leading their destruction. The player is left with Haskill and an unfinished effort to preserve a realm whose sovereign can no longer perform the role that the crisis requires.

Jyggalag later explains the history behind that transformation. According to his account, his growing power once frightened the other Princes, who cursed him to become Sheogorath. At the end of each era, he could return as Jyggalag and destroy the realm he had inhabited, only to become Sheogorath again afterward. That is the explanation he gives within the story. The events of the Greymarch make the relationship between the two identities immediate, while the ancient gathering and motives of the other Princes reach the player through Jyggalag's testimony.

Haskill's response to Sheogorath's disappearance is practical. The office remains, and the visitor must acquire the means to occupy it. The Staff of Sheogorath needs to be remade, so the search leads to Dyus in Knifepoint Hollow. Dyus served Jyggalag as keeper of his great library and retains knowledge associated with the earlier order. His survival provides a connection to a history that the current court can't supply by itself. Haskill can direct the player toward him, but Dyus must explain what materials the new staff requires.

Dyus makes the staff once the materials are brought to him, but it still needs the power associated with Sheogorath's office. The Font of Madness in the palace should provide that. Order has reached the source, however, and the roots beneath the palace must be cleared before the process can be completed. The court's conflict also returns here through the ruler who wasn't replaced by the player. That ruler's allegiance to Order carries the earlier succession dispute into the attempt to preserve the Isles. Local politics and the Greymarch now obstruct the same work.

Restoring the font allows the staff to be completed. Its role is connected to governing and defending the realm, and the final attack reaches the palace itself. The player stands where Sheogorath should have stood, carrying the instrument prepared for that responsibility. The confrontation with Jyggalag brings the effort to a direct conclusion. He can be defeated, and this time the familiar cycle doesn't simply resume with the destruction and rebuilding of his realm. Someone else has taken the place that previously returned to Sheogorath alone.

Afterward, Jyggalag acknowledges the break. He departs as himself, while the player remains to hold the position of Sheogorath. His account allows him a future beyond the repeated return to the same curse. For the inhabitants of the Isles, the immediate consequence is that their realm survives under a new holder of its ruling role. Haskill continues to serve. The duties attached to the court continue too. Becoming the ruler doesn't empty the streets of residents or remove the practical needs that made the threatened domain worth defending.

The Elder Scrolls Online gives the Princes an earlier-era setting, long before the journeys through Morrowind, Oblivion, and Skyrim. Its Gold Road story introduces Ithelia, the Forgotten Prince, associated with paths and possibilities. Her return belongs to that separate story, rather than an unseen encounter in the Dragonborn's travels.

Ithelia's absence has a cause within the fiction. Hermaeus Mora helped erase the memory of her existence, fearing the danger of her power. Her return confronts him with a history he tried to keep inaccessible. A missing name in a mortal account can therefore reflect an act of concealment, although Mora's assessment remains the judgment of an interested participant.

The disturbance has a recognizable place in Tamriel. West Weald includes Skingrad, its surrounding countryside, and the sudden appearance of the Dawnwood. People living there face changes in the landscape as well as the emergence of unfamiliar Daedra. The Forgotten Prince's return reaches beyond a revised entry in a scholar's book. It has brought uncertainty into roads and settlements whose inhabitants must now find their way through it.


At the Twilight Sepulcher, the Dragonborn can finally complete the journey begun with the recovery of the Skeleton Key. Gallus waits as a Nightingale Sentinel, able to explain the Pilgrim's Path to the living bearer. Beyond him, the sanctuary's trials lead inward, through passages where light itself can be dangerous.

The key remains in the Dragonborn's possession during the approach. Its return is a choice the player can postpone, but the restoration remains unfinished while the traveler carries it elsewhere. In the completed quest, that delay ends at the Ebonmere. The Dragonborn places the stolen artifact where it belongs, restoring the connection with Nocturnal's realm.

Nocturnal appears after the placement. She recognizes the service and grants access to the Nightingale powers, while the oath already undertaken remains. The traveler can choose an ability and later return to change it. The sanctuary now offers a reason to revisit that no longer depends on recovering Mercer's stolen possession.

Karliah and Gallus are reunited at the restored Ebonmere. For Gallus, the work of guarding the damaged sanctuary is finally over; he can depart for Evergloam. Karliah remains with the knowledge of what has been repaired and of the separation that still follows their meeting.

The Thieves Guild has other work waiting outside this chamber. Its standing across Skyrim depends on members, contacts, and jobs beyond the Sepulcher. Here, one particular journey has reached its destination. As the Dragonborn turns toward the way out, the Skeleton Key stays behind. The path back through the sanctuary can be traveled with an empty place among the belongings carried in.
