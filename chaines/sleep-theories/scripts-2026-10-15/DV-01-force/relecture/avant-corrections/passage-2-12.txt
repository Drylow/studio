The Daughter receives the wound meant for her father. The Son has taken the dagger that can kill the beings on Mortis, and when he turns it against the Father, she moves into its path. For all the unusual abilities within this family, the injury has a direct consequence. She's dying. Her intervention saves the Father, while leaving Anakin and his companions with another loss unfolding beside them. Ahsoka has also been struck down, after the Son used her to draw Anakin into his struggle.

Ahsoka's part in this begins with her capture. The Son separates her from the others and corrupts her, turning Anakin's apprentice against the people who came to bring her back. When Anakin reaches her, she attacks him. Obi-Wan becomes involved in the confrontation too, and their effort to recover Ahsoka gets tangled in the fight over the dagger. They're dealing with someone they know, whose behavior has changed under another being's influence. Anakin can't approach her as an ordinary opponent without endangering the apprentice he's trying to save.

The Son discards Ahsoka once she's helped him obtain what he wants. He withdraws the influence he's placed in her, and she falls lifeless. That makes her condition different from the Daughter's wound. Ahsoka hasn't stepped between the dagger and its intended victim. She's been taken into the Son's scheme and abandoned when her usefulness ends. Beside her, Anakin has very little time to understand what's happened. The Daughter, still conscious despite her injury, directs attention toward the possibility that Ahsoka can be restored.

The Father explains that the Daughter's remaining life can be passed to Ahsoka, with Anakin acting as the conduit. Anakin makes contact with them, and the transfer takes place through him. The Daughter dies, and Ahsoka revives. The sequence gives the rescue a physical arrangement and named participants. There's a dying donor, someone who can still be helped, and Anakin between them. He doesn't discover a procedure on his own or recover Ahsoka through determination alone. He receives guidance from the Father and participates in what the Daughter is willing to give.

It's a striking change in what Anakin is being asked to do on Mortis. Earlier, his strength allowed him to restrain the Father's children. Here, he has to help life pass between two people while the Father guides him. His unusual ability matters in both situations, though the actions are quite different. Bringing someone to submission wouldn't solve the problem beside Ahsoka's body. The Daughter's remaining strength has to reach her, and Anakin's role is to make that possible. For this moment, he follows another person's understanding of the situation.

The Daughter's decision also continues the action that brought her to this point. She protected the Father from the Son's attack, and now she uses what remains of her life to help Ahsoka. There's no suggestion that Ahsoka has earned a reward by defeating her or completing a test. Ahsoka was caught in a conflict within this family. The Daughter responds to the harm done to her, even as her own injury becomes fatal. That gives the transfer an immediate personal meaning before any larger interpretation of Mortis enters into it.

The Son's treatment of Ahsoka provides a close comparison. He alters her, sends her against her companions, and leaves her dead after securing the dagger. The Daughter intervenes so that Ahsoka can return to those companions. We can follow that difference through what happens to the apprentice herself. Under the Son's influence, she fights Anakin. After the Daughter's gift, she wakes and can leave with him. The two encounters change the same person's condition in ways that show what each sibling has done with the abilities available to them.

Ahsoka doesn't come away from the transfer with an explanation of the family or an assigned place in it. The story returns her to Anakin and Obi-Wan as their companion. She's alive again, and their concern remains getting away from Mortis together. That matters because the imagery around the Daughter can make the event feel like an appointment to some larger office. In the immediate aftermath, Ahsoka hasn't accepted the Daughter's duties or announced a new identity. The practical result is that Anakin's apprentice has survived an encounter that had already killed her.

Later, Rebels gives that survival a visual connection beyond Mortis. Morai, the convor associated with Ahsoka, resembles the bird depicted with the Daughter. At the Jedi temple on Lothal, the mural of the Mortis family includes a convor beside the Daughter. The image places the bird within the same group of figures Ezra is studying. For someone who remembers Ahsoka's restoration, that detail brings her encounter with the Daughter back into view without requiring Ahsoka to explain it herself.

Morai also appears in the World Between Worlds, where Ezra encounters the possibility of reaching Ahsoka. The bird's presence links Ahsoka to a place already connected with the Mortis family through the temple entrance. This is a more particular association than a passing resemblance between two animals. Rebels brings together the Daughter's image, Morai, and Ahsoka across related scenes. The connection has continuity, even though the characters aren't given a full account of why the convor accompanies her or exactly what that companionship means.

It's possible to read Morai as a sign that the Daughter's gift continues to matter in Ahsoka's life. The transfer on Mortis gives that reading a concrete basis. Ahsoka's identification as the Daughter reborn, though, remains an interpretation rather than a confirmed consequence of her revival. The story doesn't provide a general method for repeating the transfer either. What it gives Anakin is one opportunity, under the Father's guidance, to help a dying being restore his apprentice. Ahsoka wakes beside the person who refused to leave her, carrying on with a life the Daughter has helped return to her.
