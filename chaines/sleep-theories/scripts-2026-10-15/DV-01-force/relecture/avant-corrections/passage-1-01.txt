Luke can lift stones on Dagobah, but when his X-wing sinks into the swamp, he can't bring it back. Yoda lifts the fighter out and settles it on dry ground. It's a clear demonstration, and Luke still struggles to accept what he's seen. That gap is worth spending some time with: what Yoda has taught him, why the ship feels different from the stones, and how much this achievement actually tells us about the Force.

We'll stay within the released canon films and the named scenes from The Clone Wars, Rebels and Ahsoka, with spoilers for those stories. Here, our starting point is The Empire Strikes Back, where Luke's training takes place beside the ship that brought him to Dagobah. The X-wing is his way off the planet. Its condition matters to him in a practical way, so when it sinks farther into the water, the lesson suddenly involves something he needs. He has to apply an unfamiliar ability to a problem he can't afford to leave unsolved.

Luke already accepts that the Force can move objects. During his exercises, he balances upside down and lifts stones while Yoda instructs him. He's doing several difficult things together, and the interruption of his concentration brings that arrangement down. So his difficulty with the fighter isn't disbelief in every kind of telekinesis. He draws a boundary within an ability he's already used. A stone belongs inside that boundary. A spacecraft seems too large. Before trying, he's already treating the difference in size as a decisive obstacle.

Luke's boundary gives us a useful place to begin, because Yoda addresses it directly. He asks Luke to reconsider the importance of size, including the small body of the teacher standing before him. Luke has arrived with expectations about what powerful things should look like. Yoda's appearance gives him very little help with those expectations.

Yoda's explanation connects their immediate surroundings to the Force. Living things generate it, and its presence extends through the world Luke is touching and moving through. The trees and the ground belong in that explanation, along with Luke and Yoda themselves. The ship can be affected within this wider field. Yoda doesn't give Luke a calculation for converting a particular amount of effort into a particular weight lifted. He asks him to attend to a connection that his ordinary sense of physical strength doesn't describe very well.

Luke does make an attempt. He closes his eyes and reaches toward the submerged fighter, and part of it begins to rise before sinking again. That brief movement matters. The attempt gets a result, even though Luke can't sustain it. His failure leaves room for several difficulties within the task: concentration, confidence, and his incomplete command of what he's learning. The film doesn't separate those into measurable portions. It does, however, show enough movement to make his immediate conclusion that the task is impossible look premature.

Afterward, Luke is exhausted and discouraged. Yoda then raises the fighter, carries it across the water, and brings it to rest on land. The difference is visible without needing an explanation of every step. Luke has strained toward a result he expects to be beyond him. Yoda approaches the same object with assurance and completes the movement. When Luke expresses disbelief, Yoda identifies that disbelief as the reason for his failure. Within the lesson, Luke's expectation has become part of the difficulty he needs to overcome.

There's a tempting shortcut here: decide that anyone who believes strongly enough can move anything. Yoda's words about belief can sound broad when separated from the training around them. Luke, though, is an apprentice receiving instruction from someone who already knows how to perform the task. Yoda corrects his assumptions while also making him practice. Nothing in the swamp removes the need for that practice. Confidence alone hasn't taught Luke how to maintain his balance, keep his attention steady, or carry through a movement once it begins.

Luke's earlier experience aboard the Millennium Falcon helps explain why this lesson takes time. In A New Hope, Obi-Wan has him practice with a training remote while his sight is blocked. Luke begins responding without relying on his eyes, and the exercise gives him a small experience of the perception Obi-Wan describes. On Dagobah, he faces a related adjustment. He can see the fighter's bulk and knows what a machine of that size means in ordinary physical terms. That familiar knowledge is difficult to set aside when he reaches for it through the Force.

Yoda's success answers a local question completely. The X-wing can be lifted, and Luke was mistaken about its being beyond what the Force could accomplish there. We can also take Yoda's explanation seriously without turning it into an exhaustive account of every limitation. He hasn't tested every object at every distance under every condition. The scene gives us a teacher moving a particular ship while his student watches. An unlimited power claim would go much further than the achievement we're shown, and would leave out the very real work of learning that occupies Luke's time on Dagobah.

Yoda's knowledge also has a particular shape. He can guide Luke through exercises, recognize a mistaken assumption, and demonstrate an ability Luke can't yet manage. Those are substantial reasons for Luke to listen. They don't require Yoda to know everything the Force might do. A teacher can understand the next step clearly while the larger subject remains unfinished. Here, Yoda knows enough to bring the ship out of the swamp and to see that Luke has given up too soon. The lesson reaches Luke through that practical difference.

By the end, Luke has his fighter back, along with an experience that his earlier expectations can't comfortably explain. He still has to decide how to understand it. The Force has acted through a trained living being upon a familiar machine, and Luke has watched the result arrive on solid ground. For now, that's a useful answer to carry away from Dagobah: his failed attempt revealed a limit in his present understanding. Yoda has given him a concrete reason to keep learning, with the recovered X-wing standing nearby.
