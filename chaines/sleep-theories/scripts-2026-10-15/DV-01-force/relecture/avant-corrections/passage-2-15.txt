Yoda tries to reject the creature that looks like him. In The Clone Wars episode Destiny, the familiar small figure has a distorted counterpart, a dark presence that confronts him during his trials. Yoda's first response is to deny that it belongs to him. He's spent his life resisting the dark side, teaching others to recognize it, and serving the Jedi. Yet this encounter gives him something uncomfortable to face: his long discipline hasn't removed every feeling he might be ashamed to recognize.

The confrontation happens during the journey that begins with Qui-Gon's voice. Yoda has followed a teacher who died before him toward a form of understanding he doesn't yet possess. On the world associated with the source of life and the midi-chlorians, the Force Priestesses test whether he's ready to continue. His reputation among the Jedi doesn't excuse him from the trials. Here, the Grand Master has to accept instruction, enter unfamiliar places, and discover that his own description of himself can get in his way.

The dark reflection makes that difficulty physical. It resembles Yoda closely enough that he can't treat it as an entirely unfamiliar enemy, though he initially tries to separate himself from it. The creature taunts him and fights him. Yoda responds with resistance, and the struggle continues. A teacher accustomed to confronting the dark side in other people now has an opponent whose connection to him is the very thing he refuses to accept. Striking at it doesn't settle the question of why it's there.

Yoda eventually recognizes the dark reflection as part of himself. He acknowledges the darkness he carries and refuses to let it govern him. As his response changes, the creature loses its strength and diminishes. The struggle ends through an admission that his earlier resistance avoided. He can experience the impulses this figure embodies without allowing them to direct his conduct. The trial gives that realization a visible result: the threatening counterpart can no longer sustain the same fight once Yoda stops denying their connection.

There's a particular kind of pride involved in his initial refusal. Yoda has good reason to value what he's learned, and his discipline has helped him resist temptations that overcome others. Still, in this encounter, his certainty that the creature cannot belong to him prevents him from dealing with it. The belief that he has already settled the matter becomes a difficulty of its own. He has to allow something unwelcome into his understanding of himself before he can respond to it effectively.

Fear belongs in that understanding too. Throughout the prequels, Yoda recognizes how fear of loss can lead someone toward destructive actions. He hears Anakin's concerns and warns him about attachment. Destiny turns the examination toward the teacher who gives those warnings. Yoda doesn't receive an exemption from fear because he can explain its dangers. His trial asks whether he can recognize troubling feelings in himself with the same seriousness he brings to examining a prospective student or advising a distressed Jedi.

The episode's other trials make the personal cost of that recognition harder to avoid. Yoda encounters visions involving the Jedi and the terrible losses facing them. He also encounters an appealing vision of peace and companionship, with figures whose presence offers comfort. His preparation requires him to face experiences that disturb him and to look beyond an experience he'd like to accept. The dark reflection belongs within this demanding education. Yoda has attachments, grief, and hopes that an illusion can reach, even after so much training.

Acknowledging those feelings doesn't give them permission to decide what he should do. When Yoda accepts that the dark reflection belongs to him, he doesn't begin following its advice or invite it to share responsibility for his actions. He denies it that influence. Nothing in the encounter asks him to make cruelty part of his practice, or to treat anger as a necessary partner in wise judgment. The creature's shrinking presence accompanies Yoda's refusal to submit to it, and the Priestesses allow his preparation to continue.

That matters for someone who helps lead the Jedi during a war. Yoda's decisions affect students and others who depend on his judgment. If he treats his own motives as beyond examination, his rank gives that mistake room to affect other people. The trial suggests a practical reason for humility: a teacher who can acknowledge pride has a better chance of noticing when it intrudes on a decision. It doesn't follow that every decision Yoda makes afterward will be correct. He's learned something he must keep applying.

His later teaching on Dagobah sits naturally beside this experience. Luke arrives with expectations about what a great warrior should look like and impatience about the instruction he receives. Yoda watches those habits interfere with Luke's attention. In Destiny, Yoda has undergone his own uncomfortable correction. He, too, enters a lesson with an assumption about who he is, and that assumption obstructs him. The comparison gives his teaching some added weight. Learning to recognize a fault can remain difficult even for the person qualified to explain it.

The figure that attacked Yoda has diminished. He can continue the trials without pretending it was never part of him.
