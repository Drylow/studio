On the Trade Federation ship in The Phantom Menace, Obi-Wan senses something troubling beyond the immediate dispute over Naboo. Qui-Gon asks him to keep his attention on what's happening around him. Obi-Wan has learned from Yoda to consider the future, and Qui-Gon doesn't dismiss that teaching. He cautions against letting it draw him away from the present. It's a small exchange while they're waiting to meet their hosts, but it gives us a useful way to understand how Qui-Gon approaches the people he encounters afterward.

The danger aboard the ship soon becomes immediate. Gas enters the room, and the Jedi have to escape an attempt on their lives. Qui-Gon's advice has a practical setting here. He and Obi-Wan are negotiators who need to notice what their hosts are doing, even while they sense that something larger may be wrong. Attention to the living Force belongs within that work. A feeling about distant trouble can't supply every detail of the room they're standing in or tell them exactly what action to take.

When Qui-Gon reaches Tatooine, this attention settles on a particular child. Anakin works in Watto's shop, lives in slavery with his mother, and offers help to strangers who can't pay for the parts they need. Qui-Gon has an urgent reason to visit the shop, but his interest in Anakin grows beyond that errand. He listens to the boy's account of racing and notices his unusual abilities. The person in front of him becomes worth investigating, despite having no apparent connection to the diplomatic mission that brought the Jedi there.

Anakin's generosity gives Qui-Gon something a blood sample couldn't tell him. The boy brings the travelers into his home and wants to help them leave the planet. Shmi describes his concern for others, and Qui-Gon sees some of that concern for himself. These encounters matter because the decision ahead concerns whether Anakin should become a Jedi. Unusual perception might explain how a human child can compete in a dangerous race. It doesn't, by itself, explain how he treats someone who needs shelter or assistance.

Qui-Gon also looks for a more specific explanation of Anakin's abilities. He has Obi-Wan analyze a blood sample, and the result confirms an exceptional midi-chlorian count. Later, Qui-Gon explains to Anakin that these living organisms allow communication with the Force. That gives the boy a way to begin understanding what Qui-Gon has detected. It still leaves the teacher with separate questions. A test can indicate extraordinary potential without telling him how Anakin will respond to instruction, separation from home, or the responsibilities of Jedi life.

Shmi's account of Anakin's birth adds to Qui-Gon's conviction. She tells him that Anakin had no father, and he considers the possibility that the boy was conceived through the midi-chlorians. When he speaks to the Council, he connects Anakin with the prophecy of someone who will bring balance to the Force. Those are Qui-Gon's conclusions about a remarkable encounter. The film gives clear reasons for his interest, while leaving his interpretation dependent on what he thinks those signs mean. His confidence reaches further than the blood test alone can carry him.

There's also a distinction between finding Anakin and arranging a secure future for him. Qui-Gon wins the boy's freedom through his wager with Watto, but he can't free Shmi as well. Anakin leaves with an opportunity his mother wants him to have, and with the pain of leaving her enslaved. Qui-Gon hasn't overlooked her situation. He tries to include her in the bargain and fails. That limit follows them away from Tatooine. The Jedi can recognize something extraordinary in the child without being able to resolve the circumstances that have shaped him.

On Coruscant, the Council meets the same boy under very different conditions. Its members weren't guests in Shmi's home, and they didn't share the struggle to repair the ship. They examine Anakin as a possible student. He demonstrates unusual perception during their test, yet their questions turn toward his feelings about his mother. Yoda identifies fear in him. Anakin's concern makes sense after the departure we've just seen, and it gives the Council a concrete reason to hesitate about bringing him into its training.

It's tempting to treat that hesitation as proof that the Council has failed to recognize what Qui-Gon recognizes. The encounter allows a more complicated reading. The Council hears his account and examines Anakin's abilities; its objection concerns whether the child should be trained. Qui-Gon believes he should, despite the difficulties. The Council gives greater weight to his age and emotional attachments. Both judgments concern something real about Anakin. He's exceptionally gifted, and he's frightened about the mother he couldn't bring with him. Neither observation makes the other disappear.

Qui-Gon eventually offers to train Anakin himself. That proposal places an obligation on the person making the claim: he wants to undertake the teaching that his conviction requires. It suggests he understands that discovering potential is only a beginning. Still, his willingness can't guarantee the result. A teacher may be attentive, experienced, and sincere while misunderstanding part of a student's needs. Qui-Gon's sensitivity gives him reasons to care for Anakin and to advocate for him. It doesn't give him a finished account of the life the boy will lead.

Before leaving home, Anakin turns back to his mother and struggles with the separation. Shmi remains on Tatooine as her son leaves to begin his training.
