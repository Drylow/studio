Luke sees Vader strike Obi-Wan aboard the Death Star, and there's no body left where his teacher stood. Obi-Wan's robe falls to the floor. Luke has only recently begun learning from him, and his immediate response is grief and anger. He fires at the stormtroopers instead of leaving with the others. Then Obi-Wan's voice reaches him and tells him to run. The teacher Luke has just lost can still address him, and his first instruction concerns the danger Luke is standing in.

That makes the contact unusually personal. Luke doesn't receive an unfamiliar message from some distant presence. He recognizes the person who was teaching him aboard the Millennium Falcon. Obi-Wan had helped him practice with the training remote, encouraging him to act without depending entirely on his eyes. Now Luke hears that same teacher without being able to see him at all. Their lessons continue through a form of contact Luke hasn't been prepared to expect, while Han and Leia still need him to escape.

Obi-Wan's disappearance also comes after a deliberate action. During the duel, he sees Luke and raises his lightsaber before Vader strikes. Earlier, he's warned Vader that killing him will have consequences Vader doesn't understand. His conduct suggests that he expects something beyond the end of the fight. Still, Luke experiences an actual loss. Obi-Wan doesn't walk onto the Falcon with them. His continuing voice doesn't restore the physical companion who could travel beside Luke, explain unfamiliar places, and carry a lightsaber into danger.

The attack on the Death Star gives that voice a different task. Luke is flying toward an exhaust port while Imperial fighters pursue the Rebel pilots. He has the targeting computer in front of him and a very narrow opportunity to fire. Obi-Wan encourages him to rely on the Force. Luke switches off the computer, which prompts concern from the Rebel base. The instruction reaches him in the middle of a practical decision, with no time for an extended lesson about how the voice has survived.

Luke still has to fly the fighter and release the torpedoes. Han's return also matters because he breaks up Vader's attack and gives Luke his opening. Obi-Wan's presence fits into that chain of actions as guidance. The dead teacher doesn't replace the pilot, the other Rebels, or the friend who comes back to help. This is a useful measure of what his continued existence means for Luke. He can offer an instruction at the right moment, while the people in the battle remain responsible for carrying it out.

By The Empire Strikes Back, Luke can see Obi-Wan as well as hear him. On Hoth, after escaping the wampa, Luke collapses in the snow and sees his teacher's pale figure. Obi-Wan directs him to Dagobah, where Yoda can continue his training. It's a specific destination and a specific teacher. Obi-Wan doesn't use the encounter to explain the whole experience of death. He gives Luke the next piece of help he needs, returning to the unfinished business of teaching someone who has barely begun.

Han provides the physical rescue on Hoth. He finds Luke and shelters him until they can be recovered. Placed beside Obi-Wan's appearance, that rescue keeps the situation concrete. Luke has received guidance, but he's still injured and exposed to the cold. His teacher's presence hasn't made those conditions harmless. Both kinds of help matter to his survival and what follows: Han gets him through the immediate danger, and Obi-Wan gives him a reason to travel somewhere the Rebellion hasn't sent him.

On Dagobah, Obi-Wan's conversations with Yoda make his surviving identity clearer. He remembers his own training and speaks on Luke's behalf when Yoda questions whether the young man should be taught. Yoda sees Luke's impatience and the attention he gives to distant adventures instead of the task in front of him. Obi-Wan answers as someone who knows both teacher and pupil. He can recall having faults himself. The exchange has the familiarity of two people resuming an old discussion, even though one of them no longer has a living body.

There's affection in Obi-Wan's advocacy, and there's still disagreement. Yoda doesn't accept Luke merely because Obi-Wan brought him there. Luke has to listen while the teachers consider his readiness, and he has to confront how little he understood about the person he came to find. Obi-Wan's death hasn't made his judgment unquestionable. He continues to participate in a relationship where another teacher can object, where a pupil can disappoint him, and where persuasion takes an ordinary conversation.

The background to this persistence appears in Yoda's journey during The Clone Wars. Qui-Gon's voice draws Yoda toward something the Jedi haven't mastered, and Yoda undergoes trials connected with retaining identity after death. Later, in Revenge of the Sith, Yoda tells Obi-Wan that Qui-Gon has found a way to return and that Obi-Wan has further training ahead of him. That context gives Obi-Wan's appearances a history of learning. His condition isn't presented as the automatic destination of every Jedi who dies. A teacher who once trained him has become his teacher again.

When Luke decides to leave Dagobah for Bespin, the limits of this continuing instruction become painful. Obi-Wan and Yoda urge him to remain. They recognize the danger in abandoning his preparation, but Luke is determined to help his friends. Obi-Wan tells him that he won't be able to intervene if Luke faces Vader. Luke leaves anyway. The conversation ends without obedience, and Obi-Wan can't settle it by drawing a weapon or taking Luke's place.

Luke meets Vader without the teacher who once stood between them on the Death Star. He loses his hand, receives a devastating revelation about his father, and needs Leia and the others to get him away. Obi-Wan's continued presence hasn't spared him those experiences. What remains available is contact with a recognizable person, someone who remembers teaching him and still cares where his path leads. On Dagobah, that person can stand beside Yoda and speak to Luke. Luke can hear the advice, answer it, and climb into his fighter.
