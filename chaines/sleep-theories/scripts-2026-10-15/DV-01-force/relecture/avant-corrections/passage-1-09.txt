Luke takes his lightsaber into the cave even after Yoda tells him he won't need his weapons. He brings his blaster too. It's an understandable precaution for someone who has survived by carrying both, and who knows there's something dangerous ahead. But Yoda has already drawn his attention to what he's bringing into this encounter. Luke asks what's inside, and Yoda directs the answer back toward Luke himself. Before anything appears among the roots, Luke has made a decision about how he's going to meet it.

The cave lies beneath the tangled growth of Dagobah, close to the place where Luke is training. Luke senses cold and a powerful connection to the dark side. Yoda recognizes the place and sends him in alone. That gives the encounter a different shape from their exercises together. Yoda won't be beside him to correct a movement or answer a question as it happens. Luke has to notice what he feels and decide what to do with it, while his teacher remains outside.

Inside, Darth Vader appears before him. Luke recognizes the armored figure, draws his lightsaber, and ignites it before Vader ignites his own. That order matters to the psychological reading of the encounter. Luke has entered expecting danger, and the figure he most associates with danger has arrived. There's no conversation to consider and no other person to protect. His response is to prepare for a duel. Vader advances, their blades meet, and Luke strikes off the apparition's head. For a moment, he's defeated the enemy he came prepared to fight.

Then the fallen helmet opens, and Luke sees his own face beneath the mask. The apparent victory leaves him staring at an image he hadn't expected. Vader's armor has given the threat a familiar outline, while the face inside brings it close to Luke. The encounter ends without another opponent appearing or another instruction from Yoda. Luke has to carry that image back out with him. His skill with a weapon has brought him through the fight, but it hasn't given him an explanation for what he's just seen.

A literal prediction is one way to read that face. The Force can give Luke glimpses of events beyond his immediate surroundings, so a vision with some connection to his future belongs within what the film shows. At this point, he fears Vader and wants to defeat him. Seeing himself inside Vader's helmet could suggest that his own future includes becoming like the man he hates. It's a disturbing possibility because Luke has begun his training with good intentions. Wanting to oppose the Empire hasn't removed the possibility of going wrong.

The film later gives the image another association when Vader reveals that he's Luke's father. Looking back from that revelation, a family connection beneath the helmet becomes an available reading. Luke has more in common with Vader than he knew when he entered the cave. Still, the cave doesn't present a plain account of his parentage. It shows Luke's face, and Luke receives no explanation that would let him identify his father. Treating it as a complete message about the family would give him information the encounter never actually provides.

The psychological warning has a more immediate connection to his behavior. Luke is afraid, carries weapons despite Yoda's advice, and meets the figure in front of him with his blade. The face can be understood as a warning about where that response might lead if fear keeps deciding his actions. Vader is already the person Luke expects to find at the center of danger. By placing Luke inside the armor, the vision gives him a reason to examine his own response as well as the enemy's.

That doesn't mean carrying a lightsaber is itself a moral failure. Yoda is teaching Luke to become a Jedi, and Luke's training includes learning how to defend himself. The particular instruction here is that he doesn't need his weapons for this encounter. Luke hears that advice and equips himself anyway. His difficulty is concrete: he can accept Yoda as a teacher while still falling back on a familiar precaution when he's frightened. The cave gives that small disagreement a consequence Luke can see, even if he can't yet explain it.

The apparition also offers Luke an unusually simple encounter with Vader. There's no prisoner to rescue, no Imperial installation to destroy, and no escape route to keep open for his friends. Luke can direct his attention toward the armored figure in front of him. When the head falls, the fight seems finished. Then his own face makes that outcome troubling. On the psychological reading, defeating the image of Vader has done little to settle the fear that brought Luke's weapon into his hand. He's left with something he can't answer by striking again.

Yoda's later response places the cave within Luke's unfinished training. When Luke prepares to leave Dagobah, Yoda refers to his failure there. That judgment makes sense even though Luke won the apparent duel. Yoda is assessing how Luke faced what appeared, including the fear and aggression he brought with him. He doesn't congratulate Luke for a successful strike. For the teacher waiting outside, the encounter has exposed a difficulty that more practice with a blade alone won't resolve. Luke still needs to learn how to act when an enemy draws out his fear.

The future-facing and psychological readings can sit together here, although the film doesn't explain exactly how the cave produces its vision. A warning about becoming like Vader could concern a possible future and Luke's present habits at the same time. Either way, Luke has been given something more personal than a demonstration of dark-side danger. He entered expecting to confront whatever lived beneath the roots. He leaves having seen his own face inside the helmet of the man he fears, with his weapons still in his possession and Yoda waiting outside.
