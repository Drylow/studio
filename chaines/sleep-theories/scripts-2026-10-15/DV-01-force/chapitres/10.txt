Anakin brings balance to the Force while the Emperor is hurting his son. In Return of the Jedi, Luke lies helpless before Sidious, and Vader stands close enough to intervene. The Emperor expects his apprentice to accept what's happening. Vader has served him for years, and Luke has refused to take his father's place. Then Vader lifts Sidious and throws him into the reactor shaft. The action that fulfills Anakin's promised role begins with his response to someone suffering directly in front of him.

The prequels introduce that role through Qui-Gon. After finding Anakin on Tatooine, he brings the child before the Jedi Council and argues that he's the person described by the prophecy. Anakin's unusual connection to the Force matters to Qui-Gon's judgment, as does the account of his birth. The Council doesn't greet that conclusion with the same confidence. Its members examine Anakin, recognize his potential, and worry about his fear. Everyone involved is considering a child whose life will continue beyond this meeting, with consequences none of them can see completely.

By Revenge of the Sith, that uncertainty has become uncomfortable for the Jedi. Anakin is already trained, the war has drawn the Order into military command, and the Sith remain concealed near the center of the Republic. When Obi-Wan raises the expectation that the chosen one will destroy the Sith and bring balance, Yoda allows that the prophecy may have been misunderstood. That's a specific admission from a teacher who has helped guide Anakin's life. The Jedi have an expectation about his future, but they can't turn that expectation into reliable knowledge of his next decision.

Nothing in this situation supplies a rule about equal numbers of Jedi and Sith. The Council doesn't discuss recruiting Sith to correct an excess of Jedi. Qui-Gon doesn't propose reducing the Order until its membership matches its enemies. The anxiety concerns the Sith's return and the danger surrounding Anakin. A numerical interpretation would have to explain why the destruction of Jedi communities, including children, should count as a healthy condition for the Force. The films show those killings as part of Anakin's fall and Sidious's seizure of the Republic.

The Force itself also extends beyond these groups. Obi-Wan describes a field created by living things, and Yoda teaches Luke to recognize its presence around him on Dagobah. Trees and the life of the swamp belong to that explanation. Jedi and Sith draw on something that isn't confined to their memberships. Counting the people who carry lightsabers therefore leaves out almost everything Obi-Wan and Yoda describe. Their teachings give us a reason to look at how Anakin and Sidious affect life around them when considering what their struggle means for balance.

Sidious's rule gives that question a concrete setting. In Return of the Jedi, he watches the battle outside the Death Star while drawing Luke toward violence inside it. He has allowed the Rebels to learn about the station, arranged a trap, and made Luke's concern for his friends part of his effort to recruit him. Luke is supposed to feel that attacking in anger is his only useful response. Sidious can exploit affection as readily as ambition. He doesn't need Luke to stop caring about his friends if he can turn that care into desperation.

Luke nearly follows the course prepared for him. When Vader threatens to pursue Leia, Luke attacks and overwhelms his father. He cuts off Vader's hand, then looks at his own artificial hand. The resemblance gives him a moment to recognize where the fight has brought him. Sidious encourages the final killing, which would leave Luke ready to become his apprentice. Luke refuses and throws away his weapon. His claim to being a Jedi comes at the point when he declines the victory the Emperor is offering him.

That's an important distinction when considering the Jedi Order. Luke's refusal doesn't restore the old Council chamber, recover the Republic's institutions, or settle every failure of the prewar Order. He's acting in a room where neither Yoda nor Obi-Wan can make the decision for him. Their teaching has helped bring him there, but he must decide what to do with a defeated father and an Emperor encouraging murder. The Jedi identity he accepts carries a responsibility he can practice even with the institution that trained his father gone.

Vader's response has the same personal immediacy. Luke hasn't defeated Sidious, and discarding his lightsaber leaves him exposed to the Emperor's attack. Vader must act without any promise that he'll survive. When he intervenes, Sidious's lightning strikes him as well, and the damage proves fatal. He saves Luke and turns against the master he has obeyed. Reading this as Anakin's return to selfless action fits the cost he accepts: his son can leave the station, while Anakin has only a brief time left with him.

Obi-Wan's grief on Mustafar helps explain why this ending matters to the prophecy. After defeating Anakin in Revenge of the Sith, he confronts the difference between the future he expected and the destruction Anakin has helped cause. He had believed Anakin would oppose the Sith. Years later, Luke reaches the person Obi-Wan mourned as lost. Anakin finally acts against Sidious after his son refuses to abandon him. The fulfillment comes through a decision that his former teachers couldn't arrange and the Emperor failed to anticipate.

The Rise of Skywalker later brings Sidious back into the story on Exegol. His return means the victory at Endor didn't prevent every future appearance of Sith evil. Even so, Luke survived that encounter because Anakin intervened, and Sidious lost the apprentice who had enforced his rule. Anakin's voice later urges Rey to restore balance as he once did. That reference treats his earlier action as meaningful while allowing that another generation faces a renewed danger. It gives no timetable requiring darkness to return whenever the Jedi recover.

Balance, as these events present it, doesn't offer the characters an accounting method for arranging the galaxy. Qui-Gon can recognize promise in a child without knowing how that child's life will unfold. Luke can follow Jedi teaching without rebuilding every part of the old Order. And Anakin can answer his son's suffering after years of serving Sidious. Once Luke has carried him away from the throne room, his remaining request is to see his son without the mask. Luke helps him remove it, and they speak face to face.
