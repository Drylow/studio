The Son shows Anakin a future in which he becomes the very thing he's spent his life fighting. In Ghosts of Mortis, the images include his fall to the dark side and the masked figure of Darth Vader. Anakin recoils from what he's being shown. He doesn't greet it as a promise of greatness. He sees suffering attached to his own actions, and his immediate concern is whether he can keep those actions from ever happening. The Son has an answer ready, with a place for himself in Anakin's attempt to escape that future.

Anakin encounters him at the Well of the Dark Side, after the struggle within the family has already cost the Daughter her life. Obi-Wan and Ahsoka are still trying to leave Mortis with him. The Son draws Anakin away from that practical task and gives him something much harder to set aside than another threat. He presents Anakin with knowledge about himself. For a Jedi accustomed to confronting an enemy in front of him, this creates a different problem. The person Anakin wants to stop is someone he hasn't become.

There's a painful feature of Anakin's response worth staying with. He understands that the suffering matters, and he doesn't want to cause it. His distress gives the Son a way to recruit him. Anakin's concern for the people he might hurt becomes attached to the Son's claim that, together, they can prevent the future. The offer reaches him through a desire to do something good. Once he accepts the Son as the person who can make that possible, he's prepared to act against the companions who came to Mortis with him.

The Son offers their combined strength as a means of overcoming the Sith and ending the conflict. That's an appealing promise for Anakin, who has spent the war fighting battles without being able to bring the war itself to an end. Yet accepting it means helping the Son leave Mortis, despite everything the family and the Jedi have already suffered because of him. Anakin has seen how the Son treats other people. The proposal asks him to set that experience aside because of the catastrophe the Son now claims they can avert.

When Obi-Wan confronts him, Anakin explains his changed allegiance through that future. Obi-Wan hasn't been given the visions. What he encounters is his former apprentice siding with the Son and treating their escape together as something that must be prevented. Anakin attacks him. The gap between the two men is painfully concrete here. Anakin believes he's acting to prevent terrible harm, while Obi-Wan has to deal with the harm Anakin is doing in front of him. Their long relationship doesn't make that encounter safe.

Ahsoka's work on their ship brings the consequences back to something practical. The Jedi need transport to get away, and the Son needs a means of escape as well. Once Anakin has joined him, repairing the ship can help the wrong person leave Mortis. Ahsoka has to prevent that departure. The visions haven't remained a private burden inside Anakin's mind. They've changed what his companions can safely do, forcing them to respond to a plan they haven't agreed to and a danger he now believes he must accept.

The Father intervenes by taking away Anakin's knowledge of the future. He regards the revelation as something Anakin shouldn't have been given, and he removes the visions from Anakin's mind. It's a striking response because the Father doesn't argue through every image or offer Anakin a better account of the coming years. He removes the experience around which the Son has built his appeal. Anakin can no longer use that remembered future as his reason for continuing the alliance. He returns to his companions without carrying the same terrible knowledge.

That intervention changes what Anakin remembers. The episode doesn't show the Father erasing the future events themselves. For the audience, Anakin's later fall remains part of the story told in the films. What disappears on Mortis is his advance awareness of it. This distinction helps keep the Father's action clear: he interrupts the Son's attempt to use a revelation, and the struggle on Mortis continues. The Jedi still have to face the Son and find a way to leave. Their immediate circumstances haven't vanished with Anakin's recollection.

Compared with Anakin's dreams about Padme in Revenge of the Sith, the Mortis encounter gives him a much more explicit view of his own responsibility. Here he sees himself implicated in the disaster. Even that doesn't tell him how to respond wisely. His horror makes him vulnerable to someone offering an immediate escape from guilt. As an interpretation of the encounter, that seems especially important: the Son succeeds by making domination look like a necessary precaution against worse suffering. Anakin accepts the proposed remedy before examining what it already asks him to do.

After the Father intervenes, Anakin has no remembered warning to carry home and discuss with Obi-Wan. He can't return to those images later, reconsider the Son's argument, or ask his teacher to help him understand them. The Father has ended an immediate danger by removing the warning Anakin was using to justify siding with the Son. That leaves a difficult consequence alongside the rescue. Anakin can stand with his companions again, but the conversation he tried to have with Obi-Wan about his future has lost its subject. For Anakin, the images of the helmet are gone.
