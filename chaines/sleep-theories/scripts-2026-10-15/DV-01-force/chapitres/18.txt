On Pasaana, Rey approaches a wounded serpent while her companions are trying to find a way out of the underground tunnels. The creature is large enough to threaten them, and the encounter could easily become another fight. Rey notices its injury and moves closer. She places her hand against the wound, and the damaged flesh closes. Afterward, the serpent moves away, opening a route out. Helping it changes the situation for everyone trapped there, although Rey begins without any assurance that the creature will cooperate.

When Rey explains what she's done to BB-8, she describes giving some of her own life energy through the Force. That's the explanation the film gives us for her healing. Something passes from the healer to the injured creature, and the wound repairs. Rey remains alive and able to continue the journey. The first example therefore gives the practice a cost without making that cost fatal. She can give something of herself and still walk away with her friends.

The serpent also matters because it has no special connection to Rey. It isn't part of her bond with Ben, and it hasn't asked for her help in words. Rey recognizes an injury in an animal that everyone has reason to fear. Her response suggests an attention to its suffering that reaches beyond the immediate danger it presents. The practical benefit comes afterward, when it clears their way. She doesn't first secure a bargain or establish that the animal will be useful.

On the wreckage of the second Death Star, healing follows a much more painful encounter. Rey and Kylo Ren have been fighting when Leia reaches out to her son through the Force. His attention breaks, his weapon falls, and Rey catches it and stabs him. Then she senses Leia's death. The fight stops with Ben wounded in front of her, and Rey has to face an injury she's just inflicted. Her ability to heal now has a direct place in the consequences of her own actions.

Rey kneels beside him and places her hand over the wound. It closes, and the scar on his face disappears as well. Again, the person giving life energy survives the act. Rey is able to speak to him and then leave in his TIE fighter. Ben remains on the wreckage, alive and physically restored. Whatever the effort requires of her, this second use still doesn't demand her death. The film has now shown healing an animal and healing a person without either act becoming a fatal exchange.

Ben's recovery also leaves him with something Rey can't perform for him. She can repair his body, but he still has to decide what to do after Leia's death and Rey's departure. His encounter with the memory of Han follows, and he throws away his lightsaber. Healing gives him the opportunity to continue living. His rejection of Kylo Ren happens through his response to the people he's hurt and the love he has resisted. Rey doesn't remove that responsibility along with his wound.

By the time Ben reaches Rey on Exegol, the circumstances have changed again. Sidious has drawn strength from their dyad and cast Ben into the abyss. Rey rises to confront Sidious, defeats him, and collapses after the confrontation. When Ben climbs back and reaches her, she's lifeless. He holds her and places a hand against her, giving his own life energy until she returns. They recognize each other, share a kiss, and then Ben dies. His body disappears as Rey remains beside him.

That final act is much more demanding than closing the serpent's wound or repairing the injury on the wreckage. Ben is bringing Rey back after her collapse and death, and he gives his life in the process. The sequence invites the reading that restoring her requires everything he has left. It doesn't supply a measured scale for how much energy each injury takes, or a rule that every healer would pay exactly the same price. The clear comparison is there in the outcomes: Rey survives her earlier acts, while Ben doesn't survive this one.

Their dyad belongs to the circumstances on Exegol, too. Sidious has already recognized their unusual connection and used it to restore himself. Ben's gift takes place between those same two people after both have endured that encounter. The film doesn't separate every contribution made by their bond, their condition, and the act of restoration itself. Still, the serpent gives us a useful comparison. Rey's earlier healing reaches a living being outside the dyad, so the entire practice can't be explained as something available only between Rey and Ben.

For Anakin, the prospect of saving someone from death arrived through a very different route. In Revenge of the Sith, he fears losing Padme and turns toward Palpatine's account of a Sith who could preserve life. Anakin never receives a demonstrated lesson that would let him do what Rey does on Pasaana. Even after his pledge, Palpatine presents saving Padme as something they must discover together. Seeing healing decades later doesn't put that knowledge into Anakin's hands, and closing an existing wound doesn't establish a way to prevent the death he fears.

The difference also lies in what the characters ask of the person they're trying to save. Anakin's fear becomes entangled with his demand that Padme remain with him, even as his actions endanger her. Ben reaches Rey after abandoning his weapon and coming to help her oppose Sidious. He gives her a chance to live without securing any future for himself beside her. Rey's first healing offers a smaller, clearer version of that generosity. She touches an injured creature, repairs what she can, and lets it leave. In the tunnel, the serpent moves away under its own strength, and the passage is open.
