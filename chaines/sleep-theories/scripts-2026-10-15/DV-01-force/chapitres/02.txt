Qui-Gon takes a blood sample from Anakin on Tatooine and asks Obi-Wan to analyze the reading. Anakin understands the procedure as a check for infection. Qui-Gon is investigating something else, because the child has already drawn his attention through abilities that seem extraordinary. In The Phantom Menace, this small medical procedure becomes part of a decision with enormous consequences for Anakin. A Jedi visitor can find something in his blood that the boy himself hasn't learned to recognize or explain.

Obi-Wan reports an exceptionally high midi-chlorian count, higher even than Yoda's. Qui-Gon receives that result as confirmation that Anakin deserves serious attention. The comparison with Yoda gives the finding meaning for him. He knows what an accomplished Jedi can do, and now he's looking at a child whose biological reading exceeds that familiar example. Still, the test hasn't asked Anakin to demonstrate judgment, patience, or an understanding of the Force. Those questions remain open when the result arrives.

The word midi-chlorian can make the explanation sound as though the Jedi have found the Force itself inside a blood sample. Qui-Gon's later conversation with Anakin gives a different account. He describes microscopic living organisms within their cells, living in a symbiotic relationship with them. Through these organisms, living beings can communicate with the Force. That gives the blood test a particular purpose. It measures something involved in Anakin's connection, while the wider field the Jedi speak about remains outside the scope of that measurement.

That wider account is already familiar from Obi-Wan's explanation to Luke in A New Hope. Living things create the Force, and it surrounds and connects them. Qui-Gon's account adds a biological part to how a person participates in that connection. The films don't take us through a complete process between a microscopic organism, a person's awareness, and a ship rising into the air. They give us enough to understand why a Jedi might examine blood while still teaching a student to listen and concentrate.

Anakin's own experience comes before he has that vocabulary. He races a podracer, a dangerous activity that Qui-Gon associates with the boy's ability to anticipate events. Anakin describes something that feels natural to him, although the people around him recognize how unusual his participation is. Qui-Gon interprets his quick reactions through what he knows about the Force. The blood result strengthens an explanation he's already considering. He has observed an ability, formed a judgment about it, and sought another way to investigate what he's found.

Anakin hasn't grown up in the Jedi Temple. He lives with his mother under slavery, works for Watto, and has practical skills the visiting Jedi needs.

The podrace makes that decision especially concrete. Qui-Gon's arrangements with Watto lead to Anakin winning his freedom, but Shmi remains behind. For Anakin, the possibility of becoming a Jedi arrives together with separation from his mother. His exceptional reading doesn't make leaving easy, and it doesn't tell the adults how he will bear that separation. A teacher considering him has to hold both facts in mind: the child may have remarkable ability, and he's still a child who needs help with what is happening to him.

It's possible to read Qui-Gon's confidence as a sensible response to several unusual findings. He hasn't relied on blood alone. He's seen Anakin's abilities, heard Shmi's account of his birth, and come to believe that the boy may be the one described in the prophecy. Within the film, that belief belongs to Qui-Gon. The test can strengthen his conviction without independently settling the prophecy's meaning. Nothing in the sample itself explains why Anakin was born or supplies instructions for the life he's supposed to lead.

The Jedi Council approaches the same child with reservations. When Qui-Gon brings Anakin before them, they examine him and discuss whether he should be trained. His age and his emotional situation matter to their decision, even with Qui-Gon's report of extraordinary potential before them. Their response shows that the Jedi don't treat a high count as an automatic admission. They have a teaching system, and they consider whether Anakin can enter it under circumstances very different from those of children raised within the Order.

Anakin asks Qui-Gon about midi-chlorians because he doesn't understand the term the adults have used around him. Qui-Gon's answer ends with learning to quiet the mind so that their communication can be heard. Even in this explanation of biology, there's something Anakin must learn to do. Having the organisms within him hasn't given him a ready understanding of their role. He needs instruction to recognize a connection that's already part of his life. The exceptional result begins an education whose outcome the test can't supply.

By the film's end, Qui-Gon has died, and Obi-Wan takes on Anakin's training. The boy's biological potential hasn't changed, but the person responsible for helping him develop it has. That's a practical limit on what the original finding could tell anyone. It identified something extraordinary in Anakin, while his education would depend on people, circumstances, and years still to come. He arrives at the Jedi Temple with that potential intact, and with the work of becoming a Jedi still ahead of him.
