The story meets Anakin's fear with the suggestion that a technique exists somewhere beyond Jedi teaching. Palpatine also places himself in the position of someone who knows about it. Anakin can ask Yoda for help and hear about letting go, or listen to the Chancellor describe a power that sounds fitted to his exact need. Palpatine makes the second path inviting without demonstrating the promised result.

When Palpatine reveals that he's a Sith Lord, Anakin initially reports him to Mace Windu. He hasn't yet accepted the bargain. Anakin has helped destroy the person who came to arrest the Sith because he believes that Sith may be necessary to save his wife. The dream hasn't told him to do any of this. He's acting on Palpatine's claim about what might prevent it.

After Anakin submits to him, Palpatine admits that they still have to discover the secret together. That qualification leaves Anakin without the knowledge he thought he was preserving. Nevertheless, he continues as Darth Vader. He attacks the Jedi Temple and later kills the Separatist leaders on Mustafar under his new master's orders. His commitment now includes acts he can't undo, all while Padme remains unaware of what he's become. The promise of saving her has carried him into a course of action that makes an honest return to her increasingly difficult.

Padme travels to Mustafar after Obi-Wan tells her what Anakin has done. She offers Anakin a way to leave with her, but he speaks about overthrowing Palpatine and ruling together. His proposed solution has grown far beyond keeping her safe through childbirth. When Obi-Wan appears, Anakin assumes Padme has betrayed him and uses the Force to choke her. The person whose death he's been trying to prevent is now being hurt by him. His fear has become entangled with suspicion, and he attacks before allowing her words to alter what he believes.

Obi-Wan takes Padme away from Mustafar, and she gives birth to Luke and Leia on Polis Massa. The medical droid says that her condition can't be explained medically and describes her as having lost the will to live. Padme dies after the births. Elsewhere, Anakin survives his injuries in the suit Palpatine provides. When he asks about her, Palpatine tells him that he killed her in his anger. Anakin knows he attacked her, so the accusation has something terrible to attach itself to, even though Palpatine wasn't present at her death.

The sequence invites a self-fulfilling reading: Anakin's efforts to avoid the dream help bring about the catastrophe surrounding Padme's death. He accepts a bargain, destroys people who might have helped him, and finally assaults Padme herself. Still, the film never shows a version in which he responds differently, so we can't know whether her death was otherwise avoidable. The dream gives him a possible future to fear, without settling how fixed it is. What we can follow is his response, all the way to the harm he causes. Before Padme dies, she tells Obi-Wan that she still believes there's good in Anakin. Obi-Wan is beside her to hear it. Anakin is far away, unable to hear the faith she still has in him.


Luke is balancing upside down on Dagobah when his friends' suffering breaks into his training. He's been learning to reach beyond whatever happens to be directly in front of him, and now that wider perception brings him something he can't set aside. He sees a city in the clouds. Han and Leia are there, in pain. His concentration breaks, and he falls. The exercise has reached him in a way that lifting stones hasn't. People he cares about may need him, somewhere far from Yoda's home.

Yoda recognizes that Luke has perceived the future. That gives Luke a reason to take the vision seriously, but it doesn't answer his most pressing concern. He wants to know whether his friends will die. Yoda concentrates, then admits that he can't see their fate clearly. The future is still moving. So Luke has reached something real through the Force, while his teacher, with far greater experience, still can't turn that glimpse into a definite account of what will happen.

On Bespin, Vader is arranging that situation. Han, Leia, and Chewbacca have reached Cloud City aboard the damaged Millennium Falcon, hoping that Lando Calrissian can help them. But the Empire has arrived before them, and Lando's attempt to protect his city has put his guests in Vader's hands. Vader has Han tortured without even questioning him. That detail makes the suffering especially disturbing: Vader doesn't need information from Han. He's using Luke's attachment to his friends to draw Luke toward Bespin.

Luke has no way to hear the conversations in which Vader changes his arrangement with Lando. He doesn't know that Boba Fett is there to collect Han, or that Vader intends to use the carbon-freezing chamber on him. The vision gives him no briefing on the equipment waiting below the city. Vader, meanwhile, has chosen a trap suited to the person he expects. Luke's willingness to help his friends is familiar enough for Vader to build a plan around it.

Back on Dagobah, Yoda urges Luke to continue his training. Obi-Wan appears and adds his warning. Both understand that Vader wants Luke, and both believe he's unprepared for that encounter. Their concern includes the possibility that he'll be captured or drawn toward the dark side. Luke hears them, but staying carries its own terrible possibility for him. If Han and Leia die while he's still practicing with Yoda, he will have remained away despite having seen their pain. His teachers can't offer certainty that waiting will keep them safe.

This is where Yoda's uncertainty has a practical weight. He can explain why Luke is vulnerable, and he can recognize the danger of abandoning his preparation. He can't provide a safe departure time or tell Luke exactly how the rescue will unfold. Luke is asking for an answer that would settle the decision. What he receives is experienced advice about a situation whose outcome his teachers also can't fully see. He leaves with R2-D2, while Yoda and Obi-Wan remain behind.

By the time Luke reaches Cloud City, Han has been frozen in carbonite and handed over to Boba Fett. Luke sees the others being escorted through the city, and Leia manages to warn him that he's walking into a trap. Her warning is much more direct than the vision. She knows Vader is waiting. But Luke has already traveled to Bespin to help them, and he continues into the confrontation. Vader draws him away from the people he came to rescue and toward the freezing chamber.

The chamber reveals how much preparation Luke's glimpse left out. Vader has already tested the process on Han because he wants Luke alive for delivery to the Emperor. Luke escapes the freezing pit, and the fight moves through the city's machinery and passages. His training helps him survive the first part of the encounter. It hasn't prepared him to defeat Vader. As the duel continues, Luke loses his hand, and Vader tells him that he's his father. None of that was available to Luke when he saw his friends suffering.

Meanwhile, Leia and Chewbacca's circumstances change through Lando's actions. After Vader repeatedly alters their agreement, Lando has his guards turn against the Imperial escort and releases the prisoners. Chewbacca is furious with him, with good reason, but they still have to get away. Their attempt to reach Han comes too late to stop Fett leaving. They escape aboard the Falcon with R2-D2, who gets the ship's hyperdrive working. Luke's arrival doesn't account for these decisions or these acts of help.

Luke ends up needing the rescue himself. After refusing Vader's offer, he falls into the shaft and is carried out beneath Cloud City, where he clings to an antenna. He reaches toward Leia through the Force. She senses him and insists that the Falcon turn back. That's a smaller, more immediate exchange than the earlier vision, and it gives her something she can act on: Luke is nearby and needs help. Lando brings him aboard while Leia waits inside. The friends Luke feared losing are now carrying him away.

The warning on Dagobah was genuine. Han and Leia suffered, and Vader was a danger to them. Luke leaves before completing his training and continues despite warnings that Vader is waiting for him. In the confrontation, he loses his hand and hears a truth about his father that the vision hadn't prepared him for. Aboard the Falcon, Leia tends to him as they flee. He'd crossed the distance to Bespin because he'd seen her in pain. Now she's beside him, helping him endure his own.


Luke takes his lightsaber into the cave even after Yoda tells him he won't need his weapons. He brings his blaster too. It's an understandable precaution for someone who has survived by carrying both, and who knows there's something dangerous ahead. But Yoda has already drawn his attention to what he's bringing into this encounter. Luke asks what's inside, and Yoda directs the answer back toward Luke himself. Before anything appears among the roots, Luke has made a decision about how he's going to meet it.

The cave lies beneath the tangled growth of Dagobah, close to the place where Luke is training. Luke senses cold and a powerful connection to the dark side. Yoda recognizes the place and sends him in alone. That gives the encounter a different shape from their exercises together. Yoda won't be beside him to correct a movement or answer a question as it happens. Luke has to notice what he feels and decide what to do with it, while his teacher remains outside.

Inside, Darth Vader appears before him. Luke recognizes the armored figure, draws his lightsaber, and ignites it before Vader ignites his own. That order matters to the psychological reading of the encounter. Luke has entered expecting danger, and the figure he most associates with danger has arrived. There's no conversation to consider and no other person to protect. His response is to prepare for a duel. Vader advances, their blades meet, and Luke strikes off the apparition's head. For a moment, he's defeated the enemy he came prepared to fight.

Then the fallen helmet opens, and Luke sees his own face beneath the mask. The apparent victory leaves him staring at an image he hadn't expected. Vader's armor has given the threat a familiar outline, while the face inside brings it close to Luke. The encounter ends without another opponent appearing or another instruction from Yoda. Luke has to carry that image back out with him. His skill with a weapon has brought him through the fight, but it hasn't given him an explanation for what he's just seen.

A literal prediction is one way to read that face. The Force can give Luke glimpses of events beyond his immediate surroundings, so a vision with some connection to his future belongs within what the film shows. At this point, he fears Vader and wants to defeat him. Seeing himself inside Vader's helmet could suggest that his own future includes becoming like the man he hates. It's a disturbing possibility because Luke has begun his training with good intentions. Wanting to oppose the Empire hasn't removed the possibility of going wrong.

The film later gives the image another association when Vader reveals that he's Luke's father. Looking back from that revelation, a family connection beneath the helmet becomes an available reading. Luke has more in common with Vader than he knew when he entered the cave. Still, the cave doesn't present a plain account of his parentage. It shows Luke's face, and Luke receives no explanation that would let him identify his father. Treating it as a complete message about the family would give him information the encounter never actually provides.

The psychological warning has a more immediate connection to his behavior. Luke is afraid, carries weapons despite Yoda's advice, and meets the figure in front of him with his blade. The face can be understood as a warning about where that response might lead if fear keeps deciding his actions. Vader is already the person Luke expects to find at the center of danger. By placing Luke inside the armor, the vision gives him a reason to examine his own response as well as the enemy's.

That doesn't mean carrying a lightsaber is itself a moral failure. Yoda is teaching Luke to become a Jedi, and Luke's training includes learning how to defend himself. The particular instruction here is that he doesn't need his weapons for this encounter. Luke hears that advice and equips himself anyway. His difficulty is concrete: he can accept Yoda as a teacher while still falling back on a familiar precaution when he's frightened. The cave gives that small disagreement a consequence Luke can see, even if he can't yet explain it.

Yoda's later response places the cave within Luke's unfinished training. When Luke prepares to leave Dagobah, Yoda refers to his failure there. That judgment makes sense even though Luke won the apparent duel. Yoda is assessing how Luke faced what appeared, including the fear and aggression he brought with him. He doesn't congratulate Luke for a successful strike. For the teacher waiting outside, the encounter has exposed a difficulty that more practice with a blade alone won't resolve. Luke still needs to learn how to act when an enemy draws out his fear.

The future-facing and psychological readings can sit together here, although the film doesn't explain exactly how the cave produces its vision. A warning about becoming like Vader could concern a possible future and Luke's present habits at the same time. Either way, Luke has been given something more personal than a demonstration of dark-side danger. He entered prepared to meet danger with his weapons. He leaves having seen his own face inside the helmet of the man he fears, with his weapons still in his possession and Yoda waiting outside.


Anakin brings balance to the Force while the Emperor is hurting his son. In Return of the Jedi, Luke lies helpless before Sidious, and Vader stands close enough to intervene. The Emperor expects his apprentice to accept what's happening. Vader has served him for years, and Luke has refused to take his father's place. Then Vader lifts Sidious and throws him into the reactor shaft. The action that fulfills Anakin's promised role begins with his response to someone suffering directly in front of him.

The prequels introduce that role through Qui-Gon. After finding Anakin on Tatooine, he brings the child before the Jedi Council and argues that he's the person described by the prophecy. Anakin's unusual connection to the Force matters to Qui-Gon's judgment, as does the account of his birth. The Council doesn't greet that conclusion with the same confidence. Its members examine Anakin, recognize his potential, and worry about his fear. Everyone involved is considering a child whose life will continue beyond this meeting, with consequences none of them can see completely.

By Revenge of the Sith, that uncertainty has become uncomfortable for the Jedi. Anakin is already trained, the war has drawn the Order into military command, and the Sith remain concealed near the center of the Republic. When Obi-Wan raises the expectation that the chosen one will destroy the Sith and bring balance, Yoda allows that the prophecy may have been misunderstood. That's a specific admission from a teacher who has helped guide Anakin's life. The Jedi have an expectation about his future, but they can't turn that expectation into reliable knowledge of his next decision.

Nothing in this situation supplies a rule about equal numbers of Jedi and Sith. The Council doesn't discuss recruiting Sith to correct an excess of Jedi. Qui-Gon doesn't propose reducing the Order until its membership matches its enemies. The anxiety concerns the Sith's return and the danger surrounding Anakin. A numerical interpretation would have to explain why the destruction of Jedi communities, including children, should count as a healthy condition for the Force. The films show those killings as part of Anakin's fall and Sidious's seizure of the Republic.

The Force itself also extends beyond these groups. Obi-Wan describes a field created by living things, and Yoda teaches Luke to recognize its presence around him on Dagobah. Trees and the life of the swamp belong to that explanation. Jedi and Sith draw on something that isn't confined to their memberships. Counting the people who carry lightsabers therefore leaves out almost everything Obi-Wan and Yoda describe. Their teachings give us a reason to look at how Anakin and Sidious affect life around them when considering what their struggle means for balance.

Sidious's rule gives that question a concrete setting. In Return of the Jedi, he watches the battle outside the Death Star while drawing Luke toward violence inside it. He has allowed the Rebels to learn about the station, arranged a trap, and made Luke's concern for his friends part of his effort to recruit him. Luke is supposed to feel that attacking in anger is his only useful response. Sidious can exploit affection as readily as ambition. He doesn't need Luke to stop caring about his friends if he can turn that care into desperation.

Luke nearly follows the course prepared for him. When Vader threatens to pursue Leia, Luke attacks and overwhelms his father. He cuts off Vader's hand, then looks at his own artificial hand. The resemblance gives him a moment to recognize where the fight has brought him. Sidious encourages the final killing, which would leave Luke ready to become his apprentice. Luke refuses and throws away his weapon. His claim to being a Jedi comes at the point when he declines the victory the Emperor is offering him.

That's an important distinction when considering the Jedi Order. Luke's refusal doesn't restore the old Council chamber, recover the Republic's institutions, or settle every failure of the prewar Order. He's acting in a room where neither Yoda nor Obi-Wan can make the decision for him. Their teaching has helped bring him there, but he must decide what to do with a defeated father and an Emperor encouraging murder. The Jedi identity he accepts carries a responsibility he can practice even with the institution that trained his father gone.

Vader's response has the same personal immediacy. Luke hasn't defeated Sidious, and discarding his lightsaber leaves him exposed to the Emperor's attack. Vader must act without any promise that he'll survive. When he intervenes, Sidious's lightning strikes him as well, and the damage proves fatal. He saves Luke and turns against the master he has obeyed. Reading this as Anakin's return to selfless action fits the cost he accepts: his son can leave the station, while Anakin has only a brief time left with him.

Obi-Wan's grief on Mustafar helps explain why this ending matters to the prophecy. After defeating Anakin in Revenge of the Sith, he confronts the difference between the future he expected and the destruction Anakin has helped cause. He had believed Anakin would oppose the Sith. Years later, Luke reaches the person Obi-Wan mourned as lost. Anakin finally acts against Sidious after his son refuses to abandon him. The fulfillment comes through a decision that his former teachers couldn't arrange and the Emperor failed to anticipate.

The Rise of Skywalker later brings Sidious back into the story on Exegol. His return means the victory at Endor didn't prevent every future appearance of Sith evil. Even so, Luke survived that encounter because Anakin intervened, and Sidious lost the apprentice who had enforced his rule. Anakin's voice later urges Rey to restore balance as he once did. That reference treats his earlier action as meaningful while allowing that another generation faces a renewed danger. It gives no timetable requiring darkness to return whenever the Jedi recover.

Balance, as these events present it, doesn't offer the characters an accounting method for arranging the galaxy. Qui-Gon can recognize promise in a child without knowing how that child's life will unfold. Luke can follow Jedi teaching without rebuilding every part of the old Order. And Anakin can answer his son's suffering after years of serving Sidious. Once Luke has carried him away from the throne room, his remaining request is to see his son without the mask. Luke helps him remove it, and they speak face to face.


The Father puts Anakin in a situation where being able to fight won't be enough. On Mortis, the Son and the Daughter hold Obi-Wan and Ahsoka in danger, and Anakin is told he must decide which companion to save. He refuses that limit. Reaching through the Force, he brings both of the Father's children under his command and compels them to release their captives. For the Father, this is the demonstration he's been waiting for. Anakin has done something that makes him a possible successor.

The encounter begins with Anakin, Obi-Wan, and Ahsoka investigating a distress signal. Their journey takes them somewhere their ordinary understanding of travel can't adequately explain. They find themselves on Mortis, separated from the familiar setting of the Clone Wars. The Daughter meets them and leads them toward the Father. There are no armies waiting for orders here, and no government asking the Jedi to negotiate. The problem they're drawn into belongs to a family whose members can exert extraordinary force on one another and on their visitors.

Mortis itself makes that family difficult to separate from its surroundings. Its cycle of light and darkness brings dramatic changes in the living world. Vegetation grows and withers as the environment passes between its different states. The Daughter is associated with the light side, while the Son is associated with the dark. They can appear in humanoid forms, but they also take winged forms unlike the people the Jedi normally encounter. Anakin has entered a place where familiar teachings about the Force have visible, living counterparts.

The Father describes his children as beings he must keep in balance. He has withdrawn with them to Mortis because of the danger their abilities could present beyond it. That explanation gives his household a particular purpose. Their isolation allows him to contain a conflict that could otherwise reach the wider galaxy. His children aren't merely disagreeing over how to live together. The Father treats their continued restraint as necessary to protect others, and he believes that maintaining it requires someone capable of holding both of them in check.

Age has made that arrangement uncertain. The Father knows he can't continue indefinitely, so Anakin's arrival matters to him for a reason very different from the Republic's need for a Jedi general. He wants someone who can take over his responsibility. The prophecy of the Chosen One gives him a reason to consider Anakin, but he doesn't accept the title without a test. He places Anakin under pressure and watches what he can actually do. The result convinces him that the person he's sought has arrived.

For Anakin, the test reaches a very immediate concern. Obi-Wan is his teacher and friend. Ahsoka is his Padawan, someone whose safety already falls within his responsibilities. The Father turns those attachments into the conditions of an examination. Anakin isn't asked to move an enormous object or defeat an unfamiliar opponent in an ordinary duel. He's confronted with the threatened loss of people he knows. His response is to reach for both of them, despite the Father's insistence that he must select only one.

What Anakin demonstrates here is specific: on Mortis, he can restrain both children at once.

It's worth keeping the Father's own role in view. The test takes place because he arranges a threat to Anakin's companions. His hope of securing a successor leads him to put visitors through an ordeal they haven't agreed to undertake. That makes the invitation difficult even before Anakin considers its duration. The Father has a real problem, and his children present a real danger. Still, Anakin encounters the proposed duty through coercion. He's being asked to accept responsibility by someone who has just demonstrated how far he'll go to fill the vacancy.

Accepting would mean remaining on Mortis and taking the Father's place. Anakin couldn't treat that as another mission with a return journey once the immediate danger had passed. Obi-Wan and Ahsoka would leave without him, and his life beyond Mortis would have to continue in his absence. His marriage to Padme makes that separation particularly consequential. The Father is offering a task that reaches into Anakin's whole future, even though their acquaintance is brief and Anakin has only begun to understand the household he would inherit.

That differs from the responsibilities Anakin already carries as a Jedi. In the war, he can finish an operation, bring his troops back, and receive another assignment. On Mortis, the continuing presence of a guardian is the arrangement. The Father's declining strength creates the opening, and Anakin's successful test makes him eligible to fill it. But the ability to bring the children to their knees doesn't teach him how to live with them, how to settle their disputes, or how to sustain the Father's work over time. Those demands remain part of the offer.

The family also invites a symbolic reading. A father holding a light-associated Daughter and a dark-associated Son in balance resembles a living picture of the language Jedi use about the Force. Mortis gives those ideas faces and a shared home. That resemblance doesn't establish that these three beings created the Force or that every struggle elsewhere follows the rules of their household. The Father's immediate concern is specific: his children require restraint, his own life is nearing its end, and Anakin has shown the strength to replace him.

Anakin declines to stay. The Father warns him about the consequences of refusing what he regards as Anakin's destiny, but Anakin doesn't accept the place prepared for him. His companions remain the people he intends to leave with. The test has answered whether he can subdue the Son and the Daughter. The offered succession asks for something beyond that successful moment: a life spent on Mortis, carrying the responsibility of an aging father who can no longer keep his family contained alone.


The Daughter receives the wound meant for her father. The Son has taken the dagger that can kill the beings on Mortis, and when he turns it against the Father, she moves into its path. For all the unusual abilities within this family, the injury has a direct consequence. She's dying. Her intervention saves the Father, while leaving Anakin and his companions with another loss unfolding beside them. Ahsoka has also been struck down, after the Son used her to draw Anakin into his struggle.

Ahsoka's part in this begins with her capture. The Son separates her from the others and corrupts her, turning Anakin's apprentice against the people who came to bring her back. The Son discards Ahsoka once she's helped him obtain what he wants. He withdraws the influence he's placed in her, and she falls lifeless. That makes her condition different from the Daughter's wound. Ahsoka hasn't stepped between the dagger and its intended victim. She's been taken into the Son's scheme and abandoned when her usefulness ends. Beside her, Anakin has very little time to understand what's happened. The Daughter, still conscious despite her injury, directs attention toward the possibility that Ahsoka can be restored.

The Father explains that the Daughter's remaining life can be passed to Ahsoka, with Anakin acting as the conduit. Anakin makes contact with them, and the transfer takes place through him. The Daughter dies, and Ahsoka revives. The sequence gives the rescue a physical arrangement and named participants. There's a dying donor, someone who can still be helped, and Anakin between them. He doesn't discover a procedure on his own or recover Ahsoka through determination alone. He receives guidance from the Father and participates in what the Daughter is willing to give.

It's a striking change in what Anakin is being asked to do on Mortis. Earlier, his strength allowed him to restrain the Father's children. Here, he has to help life pass between two people while the Father guides him. His unusual ability matters in both situations, though the actions are quite different. Bringing someone to submission wouldn't solve the problem beside Ahsoka's body. The Daughter's remaining strength has to reach her, and Anakin's role is to make that possible. For this moment, he follows another person's understanding of the situation.

The Daughter's decision also continues the action that brought her to this point. She protected the Father from the Son's attack, and now she uses what remains of her life to help Ahsoka. There's no suggestion that Ahsoka has earned a reward by defeating her or completing a test. Ahsoka was caught in a conflict within this family. The Daughter responds to the harm done to her, even as her own injury becomes fatal. That gives the transfer an immediate personal meaning before any larger interpretation of Mortis enters into it.

The Son's treatment of Ahsoka provides a close comparison. He alters her, sends her against her companions, and leaves her dead after securing the dagger. The Daughter intervenes so that Ahsoka can return to those companions. We can follow that difference through what happens to the apprentice herself. Under the Son's influence, she fights Anakin. After the Daughter's gift, she wakes and can leave with him. The two encounters change the same person's condition in ways that show what each sibling has done with the abilities available to them.

Ahsoka doesn't come away from the transfer with an explanation of the family or an assigned place in it. The story returns her to Anakin and Obi-Wan as their companion. She's alive again, and their concern remains getting away from Mortis together. That matters because the imagery around the Daughter can make the event feel like an appointment to some larger office. In the immediate aftermath, Ahsoka hasn't accepted the Daughter's duties or announced a new identity. The practical result is that Anakin's apprentice has survived an encounter that had already killed her.

Later, Rebels gives that survival a visual connection beyond Mortis. Morai, the convor associated with Ahsoka, resembles the bird depicted with the Daughter. At the Jedi temple on Lothal, the mural of the Mortis family includes a convor beside the Daughter. The image places the bird within the same group of figures Ezra is studying. For someone who remembers Ahsoka's restoration, that detail brings her encounter with the Daughter back into view without requiring Ahsoka to explain it herself.

Morai also appears in the World Between Worlds, where Ezra encounters the possibility of reaching Ahsoka. The bird's presence links Ahsoka to a place already connected with the Mortis family through the temple entrance. This is a more particular association than a passing resemblance between two animals. Rebels brings together the Daughter's image, Morai, and Ahsoka across related scenes. It's possible to read Morai as a sign that the Daughter's gift continues to matter in Ahsoka's life. The transfer on Mortis gives that reading a concrete basis. Ahsoka's identification as the Daughter reborn, though, remains an interpretation rather than a confirmed consequence of her revival. The story doesn't provide a general method for repeating the transfer either. What it gives Anakin is one opportunity, under the Father's guidance, to help a dying being restore his apprentice. Ahsoka wakes beside the person who refused to leave her, carrying on with a life the Daughter has helped return to her.


The Son shows Anakin a future in which he becomes the very thing he's spent his life fighting. In Ghosts of Mortis, the images include his fall to the dark side and the masked figure of Darth Vader. Anakin recoils from what he's being shown. He doesn't greet it as a promise of greatness. He sees suffering attached to his own actions, and his immediate concern is whether he can keep those actions from ever happening. The Son has an answer ready, with a place for himself in Anakin's attempt to escape that future.

Anakin encounters him at the Well of the Dark Side, after the struggle within the family has already cost the Daughter her life. Obi-Wan and Ahsoka are still trying to leave Mortis with him. The Son draws Anakin away from that practical task and gives him something much harder to set aside than another threat. He presents Anakin with knowledge about himself. For a Jedi accustomed to confronting an enemy in front of him, this creates a different problem. The person Anakin wants to stop is someone he hasn't become.

There's a painful feature of Anakin's response worth staying with. He understands that the suffering matters, and he doesn't want to cause it. His distress gives the Son a way to recruit him. Anakin's concern for the people he might hurt becomes attached to the Son's claim that, together, they can prevent the future. The offer reaches him through a desire to do something good. Once he accepts the Son as the person who can make that possible, he's prepared to act against the companions who came to Mortis with him.

The Son offers their combined strength as a means of overcoming the Sith and ending the conflict. That's an appealing promise for Anakin, who has spent the war fighting battles without being able to bring the war itself to an end. Yet accepting it means helping the Son leave Mortis, despite everything the family and the Jedi have already suffered because of him. Anakin has seen how the Son treats other people. The proposal asks him to set that experience aside because of the catastrophe the Son now claims they can avert.

When Obi-Wan confronts him, Anakin explains his changed allegiance through that future. Obi-Wan hasn't been given the visions. What he encounters is his former apprentice siding with the Son and treating their escape together as something that must be prevented. Anakin attacks him. The gap between the two men is painfully concrete here. Anakin believes he's acting to prevent terrible harm, while Obi-Wan has to deal with the harm Anakin is doing in front of him. Their long relationship doesn't make that encounter safe.

Ahsoka's work on their ship brings the consequences back to something practical. The Jedi need transport to get away, and the Son needs a means of escape as well. Once Anakin has joined him, repairing the ship can help the wrong person leave Mortis. Ahsoka has to prevent that departure. The visions haven't remained a private burden inside Anakin's mind. They've changed what his companions can safely do, forcing them to respond to a plan they haven't agreed to and a danger he now believes he must accept.

This makes the Son's proposed solution deeply suspect even on its own terms. Anakin wants to prevent his future participation in oppression, but the first steps require him to overpower Obi-Wan and assist someone already willing to use others. His knowledge hasn't supplied a patient way to avoid each harmful decision. It has made one sweeping promise feel necessary. The Son places himself beside Anakin as a partner in saving the galaxy, while Anakin begins accepting the use of force against people trying to help him.
