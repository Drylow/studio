Stilgar's people have practical reasons to be cautious about the two outsiders they find in the desert. Water is precious, strangers can bring danger, and the destruction of House Atreides hasn't made Arrakis safer. Paul and Jessica arrive with useful equipment and unusual training, but they can't demand hospitality as though they're receiving visitors on Caladan. The Fremen have to decide whether these fugitives are worth the risk of keeping alive. Jessica, in particular, faces the possibility that they won't consider her useful enough to take with them.

Stilgar already has some reason to distinguish Paul from an unknown intruder. Duncan Idaho's dealings with the Fremen have given the Atreides a connection beyond the imperial household at Arrakeen. Paul also has youth in his favor. He can learn their ways. Jessica is an adult whose place is harder to determine, and the Fremen's water discipline gives that judgment a frightening consequence. Her body contains water the community could reclaim. Her standing as a duke's concubine doesn't settle what she can contribute here.

Jessica changes the encounter through an ability the Fremen haven't expected. Using her Bene Gesserit training, she overcomes Stilgar and holds him at her mercy. It's a concrete demonstration, made against the leader of an armed group on his own ground. Stilgar has to reconsider the woman he's been assessing. She possesses a fighting discipline that could benefit his people, and she can teach it. Jessica gains room to negotiate because she's shown that bringing her into the community could give the Fremen something they want.

Stilgar's willingness to revise his judgment also matters: Paul and Jessica need a leader who can recognize their usefulness even after Jessica has challenged him before his people.

Alongside this negotiation, Jessica begins to recognize something familiar in Fremen religious language. The Bene Gesserit have been here before, through the Missionaria Protectiva. That work spreads beliefs which a sister in danger may later use to find shelter or assistance. Jessica hasn't arrived with a complete account of Fremen life. She does, however, recognize patterns her training has prepared her to notice. Words and expectations that belong to these people's religion can also tell her how to present herself and her son.

The preparation extends beyond this meeting. Earlier, in the Arrakeen residence, the Fremen housekeeper Shadout Mapes tested Jessica through religious expectations surrounding a crysknife. Jessica's responses persuaded Mapes that she fitted a sacred role. That encounter gave Jessica an early indication that Bene Gesserit protective work had taken root on Arrakis. Out here, far from the residence and its servants, the same kind of preparation has immediate value again. She can speak within a tradition that gives the strangers a possible place among the Fremen.

For Jessica, using those beliefs is part of getting Paul through danger. For the Fremen who hear her, the significance can be much greater. Their expectations include a figure associated with a Bene Gesserit mother and an extraordinary son. Paul and Jessica resemble that expectation before either has earned a settled place in the community. The resemblance gives their arrival a meaning that stretches beyond two refugees asking for help. It also means that ordinary actions may be examined for signs of something people have already been taught to anticipate.

Still, the Missionaria Protectiva hasn't supplied every part of Fremen life. Stilgar's people have their own desert knowledge, obligations, and ambitions. Their relationship with Liet-Kynes includes a long effort to change Arrakis, carried through work and restraint that outsiders barely understand. They know how to travel where Paul and Jessica are still learning to survive. They maintain communities under conditions the imperial rulers have failed to comprehend. The religious material Jessica recognizes exists within that lived society, where it has acquired meanings she can't assume she knows in full.

The expectation that the newcomer will know Fremen ways without instruction helps explain Kynes's earlier reaction to Paul's stillsuit fitting. Practical competence can be read as fulfillment of a prophecy.

There's no need to treat that recognition as confirmation that a divine plan arranged his arrival. The Bene Gesserit's planted expectations explain why a trained mother and her unusually capable son can seem familiar to people who've never met them. Paul's developing prescience adds a genuine and unsettling ability to the situation, but the Fremen still encounter him through their own beliefs. Jessica can use that reception to protect him without knowing everything that will grow from it.

Nor does Stilgar's agreement settle every response within his group. Jamis remains hostile, and Jessica's success against Stilgar hasn't made Paul an accepted member of the community. Chani, meanwhile, gives Paul practical help when they meet, including guidance about the desert around them. These are separate people responding to particular circumstances. Their decisions begin to shape his survival as directly as his mother's training does. He's gaining protection among people who can teach him, challenge him, and ask things of him.

That makes this refuge different from the household Paul has lost. On Caladan, his teachers prepared him for a place he expected to inherit. Among the Fremen, Jessica negotiates for a place neither of them yet fully understands. Her fighting skill gives Stilgar a reason to preserve her life, and the religious expectations give mother and son a recognizable place in Fremen belief. Paul can enter under that protection. He'll still have to learn what living among these people requires.
