A Fremen worm rider uses hooks to lift the edge of one of the sandworm's ring segments. Sand can enter the exposed tissue beneath, so the worm rolls that part of its body upward, away from the desert floor. The rider goes up with it. For Paul, learning this technique means approaching an animal that could kill him and understanding precisely how a Fremen survives the encounter. Courage gets him close enough to try. What keeps him alive is the practical knowledge that Stilgar and his people have learned to use.

By this point in Dune, Paul has spent years among the Fremen. He's learned to preserve his body's moisture in a stillsuit, move through the desert without the regular footfalls that attract worms, and live within the shared discipline of a sietch. These practices matter even for someone with his unusual training. Jessica prepared him to command his muscles and attend to small changes around him. She couldn't give him the desert experience that lets Stilgar recognize a danger and respond before an outsider has understood what's happening.

Walking across open sand and calling a worm require different behavior. On foot, the Fremen avoid a steady rhythm because it can bring a worm toward them. When they want transport, they deliberately create the disturbance they've otherwise worked to prevent. A thumper draws the animal to a chosen place. The people waiting there need to be ready for its arrival. Paul has to learn when attracting a worm is useful, where to stand, and how to approach it without being caught beneath its movement.

The ride is therefore part of an ordinary Fremen system of travel, however extraordinary it appears to someone raised on Caladan. Across the deep desert, worms carry people through territory where the Harkonnens can't move with comparable ease. Fremen knowledge makes places that seem impassable into routes between communities. Paul has already benefited from that knowledge as a fugitive and as a fighter. Becoming a rider gives him a more active place within it. He can participate in a journey by doing something the others depend upon.

Stilgar takes Paul's first ride seriously. His guidance brings the task down to actions Paul can carry out, despite the expectations surrounding Muad'Dib. Paul must get his hooks into position and use the worm's response to bring himself safely onto its back. Once the exposed segment is held above the sand, the creature won't willingly roll that vulnerable area downward. The equipment works because the rider understands the animal's response. Strength alone wouldn't make the same arrangement possible, and Paul's skill with a blade wouldn't help him maintain it.

The worm that answers Paul's summons is exceptionally large. Among the Fremen watching him, its size adds to the meaning of the occasion. Paul succeeds in mounting it, and others can join him for the journey. That's an achievement they can recognize in their own terms. He hasn't merely survived another attack or demonstrated a fighting technique brought from an offworld household. He's performed a demanding Fremen task, using their equipment and their teaching, in front of people who understand how easily the attempt could fail.

There's a useful comparison here with Paul's earlier weapons training. Gurney could expose a weakness during a practice fight, correct it, and make Paul try again. A mistake belonged within a lesson arranged for him by his father's household. Stilgar also teaches, but the desert can't be made into that kind of protected room. The worm continues moving, and Paul must act within the conditions it creates. His education now involves learning how people without his inherited advantages have made a home in a place that offers very little forgiveness.

For the Fremen, riding also marks Paul's progress toward adult standing within their society. His position has always been unusual. He's the duke's son, Jessica's son, a gifted fighter, and the person around whom religious expectations have gathered. Yet he still needs to meet requirements that apply to life among them. Completing the ride helps close the distance between the newcomer they accepted and a man who can undertake their responsibilities. That makes his success satisfying, while leaving him exposed to demands that weren't attached to an inexperienced outsider.

One of those demands concerns Stilgar himself. Fremen leadership can pass through a challenge in which the challenger kills the existing leader. As Paul's standing rises, people begin to expect him to claim the position that his ability seems to warrant. Stilgar has protected him, taught him, and helped make his survival possible. Now the customs of the community can make that same man appear to be an obstacle Paul should remove. Paul's growing competence creates a problem within a relationship he has good reason to preserve.

Paul refuses to settle the matter by killing Stilgar. He needs Stilgar's experience and wants him alive. He also brings an Atreides understanding of command to the situation: a duke can lead capable men without having to eliminate each one whose authority is already established. This gives Paul a way to argue for a larger structure in which Stilgar can remain useful and respected. He isn't surrendering his claim to lead. He's trying to make room for that claim without destroying someone whose knowledge the Fremen campaign still needs.

The successful ride contributes to this pressure, though it doesn't by itself give Paul command over every Fremen. His standing has developed through combat, teaching, shared danger, and the religious stories attached to his arrival. People interpret new achievements through what they already expect of him. An unusually large worm can consequently become another sign in a story that began before he reached the desert. Paul has performed a real feat. The meaning others give that feat can travel beyond the practical skill he demonstrated.

Stilgar's teaching leaves Paul better able to live on Arrakis, and the Fremen now have another experienced participant in their desert travel. At the same time, Muad'Dib is becoming harder for them to regard as an ordinary member of the community. Paul can climb onto a worm and share the journey with the people who taught him. When he comes down again, their expectations are still there, including the demand that he take a greater place among them.
