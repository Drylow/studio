A practice fight with Gurney Halleck gives Paul a fairly direct lesson in what his position requires. Paul isn't always in the mood to train, and Gurney doesn't accept that as a reason to leave him unprepared. An enemy won't arrange an attack around the young heir's willingness to meet it. There's affection between them, but Gurney can still make the exercise uncomfortable. For Paul, being cared for includes having someone insist that he take a blade seriously before an actual opponent gives him no room to learn.

The personal shield makes this education more demanding than learning to strike hard or move fast. In Dune, a shield stops a rapidly moving attack, while a slower blade can penetrate it. Paul has to manage the speed of his own attack and recognize what his opponent is attempting. An instinctive lunge isn't enough. He needs the restraint to change pace at the moment when excitement or fear might encourage him to hurry. The equipment has shaped an entire practice of combat, and his body has to learn its rules.

Gurney brings something particular to that work. His history includes Harkonnen brutality, so the danger surrounding House Atreides has a personal meaning for him. Paul's family enemy has already harmed someone standing close enough to correct his swordsmanship. Gurney's music and rough humor also belong to the same man who trains him to fight. That combination gives their relationship a warmth a formal weapons instructor might lack. Paul receives military preparation from someone whose attachment to the household reaches beyond the duties of a paid appointment.

Duncan Idaho is another of Paul's swordmasters, and Paul's affection for him matters alongside his reputation as a fighter. Having both Duncan and Gurney in his education means that armed skill doesn't arrive through a single personality. They belong to the duke's service, yet Paul knows them as individual men. Later losses and reunions carry weight because those attachments already exist. During his upbringing, though, their immediate purpose is practical. The heir must be able to protect himself even when the household's soldiers can't stand between him and danger.

There's a limit to what these skills can provide. Paul is being trained for a society where a noble may need to face a blade, but his inheritance also exposes him to threats he can't settle through a duel. A family can be endangered through a political appointment or a servant's access. His fighting lessons belong inside a much wider education. They can keep him alive in a close encounter. They can't tell him, on their own, which encounter someone else has arranged or why it has been allowed to happen.

Thufir Hawat works on that wider problem. He's the Atreides Mentat and Master of Assassins, with responsibilities that combine analysis and the security of the house. The title doesn't mean Paul is growing up among people who regard assassination as an unusual breach of civilized behavior. It's a recognized danger within the political order they inhabit. Thufir's presence teaches him that the household must think about hidden attacks as part of its ordinary business. Safety requires attention even before an enemy appears with a weapon.

Mentat training develops another kind of discipline. In the world of Dune, Mentats perform demanding mental work that other societies might assign to computers. They organize information and draw conclusions from it, but the information available to them still matters. Paul has been receiving preparation along these lines without initially understanding its full purpose. When his father tells him about it, continuing becomes something Paul can knowingly accept. He's developing the capacity for that work. The opening of the novel doesn't present him as a fully qualified replacement for Thufir.

Jessica's teaching reaches into smaller, more immediate acts. Through Bene Gesserit disciplines, Paul learns to attend to his breathing, his physical responses and the behavior of people around him. Fear is something he has a practiced way of meeting. Observation includes changes in expression and speech that another person might miss. These abilities require repeated effort; they aren't interchangeable with the unusual dreams that trouble him. Jessica can teach him exercises and methods even while the meaning of his emerging gifts remains uncertain.

Her position as his mother gives that education an intimacy Thufir's official responsibilities don't have. Paul encounters Bene Gesserit habits within his family life, through someone he depends on and loves. She knows how much can be communicated without an open statement, and she teaches him to notice it. For a political heir, that's useful whenever courtesy conceals disagreement. It also means his preparation reaches past public behavior into the management of his own reactions. Even an emotion he doesn't speak aloud may have a physical sign that someone trained like Jessica could read.

The separate lessons begin to fit together when Paul has to respond under pressure. Weapons practice asks him to keep his movements deliberate. Jessica's disciplines help him work through fear. Mentat preparation encourages him to examine the information at hand. None of these removes the need to decide what to do. Each gives him something he can attempt when a situation becomes difficult. Their value lies partly in that overlap, although possessing several methods doesn't guarantee that he'll understand every danger correctly.

And all of this education is directed toward a place already reserved for him. Gurney and Duncan prepare the duke's son to survive; Thufir helps prepare him to judge; Jessica develops capacities unusual even within a noble household. Paul benefits from their attention before he's able to assess all their expectations. On Caladan, he can still turn to the people who know more than he does. His growing competence gradually makes it possible for those same people to ask more of him.
