A Guild heighliner carries the ship that a House owns. That distinction is easy to lose when the Navigator attracts most of our attention. A person with an extraordinary ability occupies one part of an enormous physical operation. The vessels still have to travel, and their passengers still need somewhere to arrive. The Navigator's foresight concerns the safety of that passage. It doesn't replace the carrier with an act of imagination.

Navigation belongs alongside this machinery. The Guild's specialists use melange to gain a limited awareness of possible futures. They find a course that avoids disaster, turning a difficult journey into something imperial society can regularly depend on. The unusual part is their access to information about what lies ahead. A Navigator doesn't need to announce a political prophecy to perform this work. The immediate question is whether the transport can complete its passage safely.

There are ordinary forms of navigation that offer a modest comparison. A sailor can have a sound vessel and still need to judge a route, weather and hazards. The vessel's ability to move doesn't make every course safe. A Guild Navigator's method is far stranger than a sailor's observation, because it involves possible futures. The comparison helps separate two tasks: moving the carrier, and selecting a course on which its movement will avoid danger. The Guild brings those tasks together as one purchased service.

Prescience gives the Navigator a way of looking beyond an ordinary instrument's immediate report. A reading can tell a pilot something about present conditions; foresight concerns what a course will lead to. Herbert makes that anticipatory awareness central to the Guild's usefulness. Safe arrival can then become the result that a customer expects, while the work required to obtain it stays hidden. The customer has bought passage, and may have neither the training nor the opportunity to inspect the Navigator's experience.

The foresight used for navigation is limited. Finding a viable passage doesn't require knowing every future event or every danger awaiting the passengers after they arrive.

That specialization also distinguishes a successful voyage from a safe destination. The Atreides reach Arrakis. Their arrival does not protect them from the political trap waiting there. The Guild has carried them to the world Leto has been ordered to govern; it hasn't made the appointment harmless. Paul will later learn how deeply the transport service reaches into imperial struggles, but the first distinction is already present in his family's journey. A ship can arrive safely with passengers whose position on the ground is desperately insecure.

We can carry that distinction back to the first voyage from Caladan. The Guild has combined craft, organization and a scarce human capacity into a system the Atreides need. Paul experiences its reach as a passenger before he discovers a way to threaten its supply. For the person waiting aboard a House ship, the result is a passage that reaches another world. Somewhere within the larger carrier, a Navigator has found a future in which that arrival is possible.
