Paul threatens the continued existence of spice while the Emperor is still on Arrakis. Shaddam has come with Sardaukar and the standing of the imperial throne behind him. Above the planet, the ships of the Great Houses carry another possible source of armed opposition. Paul has defeated the forces around Arrakeen, but the people gathered before him still belong to an empire with armies elsewhere. His demand reaches those distant forces through the Guild representatives present at the confrontation. They need to understand what will happen if anyone tries to continue the struggle.

By then, Paul has spent years among the Fremen. He's learned their desert practices, taken part in their religious life, and survived the Water of Life. His knowledge of Arrakis includes things that the imperial administration never needed to understand to collect spice revenues. That difference matters in the audience chamber. Shaddam can think in terms of recovering a planet, defeating a claimant, or bringing more soldiers to bear. Paul is threatening something that conquest would leave the conqueror unable to restore.

The threat concerns a destructive reaction within the living system that produces melange. Paul understands how the Water of Life can be used against that system, with consequences extending beyond a single patch of desert. Herbert describes the possibility through the substances and organisms already involved in Arrakis's ecology. The political point is clear without turning Paul's knowledge into a chemical recipe. He can bring future spice production to an end, and he makes continued access depend on acceptance of his terms.

There's a distinction here for anyone carrying an image of nuclear weapons aimed at spice fields from the film adaptation. In the novel, the Atreides family atomics have a separate role in the battle. Paul uses them against the Shield Wall, the natural barrier protecting the imperial position, so the storm and the Fremen assault can reach it. His argument about that use concerns the prohibition against employing atomics on human targets. The threat he subsequently makes to spice comes through his knowledge of the planet's biological processes.

Those two actions place different problems before his opponents. Breaching the Shield Wall helps Paul defeat the Emperor's immediate defenses. Ending the spice cycle would have consequences for people who never entered that battle, including the Navigators on whom interstellar transport depends. An army can occupy a broken defensive position. Soldiers sent to secure spice fields would still need the desert to produce another harvest. Paul has found a way to make a military response dangerous to the very supply the response might be intended to protect.

The Guild representatives have an advantage over Shaddam in recognizing the seriousness of this situation. Their own limited prescience allows them to confront the possibility Paul describes. They challenge him, but they can't dismiss his warning as an empty performance. Paul presses them to examine the future available if they refuse. Their response gives his demand weight within the room. The Emperor is hearing a challenger threaten an essential resource, and the people whose profession depends on anticipating safe passage are unable to offer him reassurance.

Their appearance also brings the Guild's dependence into view. The representatives' contact lenses conceal the blue eyes associated with spice saturation. During the confrontation, that concealment is exposed. This is a small, physical detail beside the Emperor's defeat, yet it connects the negotiation to bodies that require melange. The Guild has arrived as an institution accustomed to bargaining over passage. Paul is addressing people whose reliance on the cargo they're protecting reaches further than the income earned by carrying it.

That helps explain why the Guild's transport monopoly can't rescue it from this demand. Ordinarily, withholding passage gives the Guild leverage over a House. A ruler may own ships and command soldiers, yet still need the Guild to move them between worlds. Here, refusing Paul won't provide a replacement source of melange. Carrying his enemies to Arrakis could provoke the destruction he has threatened. The familiar means of applying pressure would endanger the supply that lets the Guild continue providing its service.

Paul therefore makes the Guild deal with the military danger above Arrakis. He demands that the forces waiting there leave, and the Guild accommodates him. Those ships don't become harmless because their owners have accepted his claim to rule. Their ability to intervene depends on a carrier that has just been given a reason to refuse them. The assembled Houses may retain their grievances and their weapons. In this confrontation, Paul can prevent those weapons from being added to the battle by directing his threat at the organization responsible for their passage.

The threat would be much weaker if the Guild believed Paul would always preserve spice for his own future use. He needs an empire that can travel, too. Yet he's prepared to stake that future on the confrontation, and the Guild accepts the danger as credible. His position among the Fremen matters here: he's backed by people who inhabit the source and can act within it. A distant ruler might announce that spice must be destroyed. Paul has the knowledge and the local means to make the warning something his opponents have to answer.

The spice threat doesn't settle every part of Paul's claim to the throne. It removes the Guild's willingness to carry on resistance and leaves the Emperor with fewer ways to refuse. In the room where imperial titles are being contested, the Guild representatives have already accepted a practical requirement: the forces overhead must withdraw if the desert beneath them is to keep producing what their voyages need.
