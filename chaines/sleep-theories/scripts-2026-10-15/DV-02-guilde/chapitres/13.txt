The Fremen pay the Spacing Guild to keep observation satellites out of the skies over Arrakis. Their payment is spice, the substance the Guild needs for its own work. For a people whose settlements and movements are poorly understood by the planet's rulers, that arrangement has a practical value. Outsiders can land on Arrakis, fly over parts of it and inspect the places they occupy. The bargain keeps observation satellites away, removing one way outsiders might check estimates of Fremen numbers and activity.

Paul and Jessica learn about this bargain after they've entered Fremen society in Dune. By then, they've already seen how little the usual descriptions of the desert people prepare anyone for meeting them. Stilgar leads an organized community with its own rules, trained fighters and carefully maintained water supplies. The Fremen have established ways of surviving beyond the reach of the settlements administered by the ruling House. The satellite arrangement belongs among those practical preparations. They understand that concealment requires attention to what an outsider could see from above.

Spice gives them something the Guild will accept in exchange for that concealment. The Fremen live in the desert where melange is found, and they can direct some of it toward purposes that don't appear in the official account of planetary production. Paying the Guild lets them use a resource from their own environment to influence an institution operating far beyond Arrakis. They don't need to own the interstellar ships or hold an imperial appointment to make this particular bargain. They need something the carrier wants badly enough to accommodate them.

The Guild receives spice, while the Fremen receive the continued absence of observation satellites. That exchange is quite specific. It doesn't require the Guild to announce support for Fremen independence, recognize Stilgar as a planetary ruler or intervene openly against the Harkonnens. The benefit can be delivered by keeping a piece of infrastructure from being installed. For the Fremen, that's useful precisely because the result doesn't have to look like an alliance. The sky remains unavailable for the kind of routine observation that might make their desert life easier to investigate.

There's a difference between seeing a patch of desert during a flight and having the opportunity to watch a region repeatedly. An ornithopter carries people through a particular stretch of country for a particular purpose. Leto's journey with Kynes, for example, takes him to a spice operation, where his attention turns to a harvester and its endangered crew. Satellite observation could give the ruling House another way to examine territory beyond such visits. Even without assigning those satellites any invented capabilities, their exclusion removes an opportunity to compare reports from the ground with observations made above it.

That matters when the subject is the Fremen population. An encounter with Stilgar tells Leto that he has capable people to negotiate with. It doesn't tell him how many other communities exist across the desert. Once Paul and Jessica reach Sietch Tabr, they begin learning about a society far larger and more organized than outsiders generally recognize. A sietch is a place where people live together and maintain the resources needed to survive. Knowing that one exists leaves plenty of questions about how widely that pattern extends.

The activities worth protecting extend beyond fighters and settlements. In the desert, Paul and Jessica discover the Fremen's patient work toward changing conditions on Arrakis. Water is collected and held in communal reserves. Planting and ecological work belong to a project that reaches beyond the immediate needs of a single household. These efforts require people to work in places outsiders consider empty or nearly useless. Keeping those regions poorly observed gives the Fremen room to pursue the project without providing the ruling House with a convenient way to survey its extent.

For the Guild, accepting the payment means accommodating interests that aren't identical to those of the House governing Arrakis. The carrier can transport a ruling House's people while also taking spice from desert communities that want less scrutiny from that ruler. The arrangement suggests a willingness to maintain separate bargains with parties who'd prefer different conditions on the same planet. Nothing about carrying the official administration obliges the Guild to make every part of Arrakis easier for that administration to inspect. Its own need for spice gives the Fremen a point of negotiation.

There's also an unequal burden hidden in the exchange. The Fremen have to keep supplying something valuable to preserve an absence that suits them. The Guild doesn't have to reveal their settlements to make its position felt; the possibility of observation gives continued cooperation a value.  The Fremen have made concealment part of their dealings with an outside institution, so maintaining that concealment involves a continuing relationship beyond the sietch.

For Paul and Jessica, learning about the payment changes the meaning of the country around them. The apparent emptiness has people behind it, people who gather spice, conserve water and arrange protection against being watched. The Fremen haven't made their planet disappear. They've persuaded the organization that carries its wealth between stars to keep observation satellites away, leaving the desert communities more room to conduct their lives beyond the ruling House's view.
