At the end of Dune, the Guild representatives have to accept Paul's demands while the ships above Arrakis still belong to a working transport system. His victory hasn't supplied the Great Houses with another way to cross the distances between their worlds. Their vessels still require passage, and the Guild still provides it. The political implication is practical: Paul can force an accommodation over spice, but he can't personally take over the work of every voyage. Becoming Emperor gives him new reasons to keep that work going.

The Guild's enormous carriers make movement available across an empire divided among separate rulers. That arrangement matters after Paul's accession because governing involves people and goods arriving where they're needed. Arrakis itself has long been part of this circulation, with spice leaving the planet for use elsewhere. Paul's command of its supply gives him extraordinary bargaining strength, while the substance still needs to reach those who consume it. The Guild has the means to carry cargo between worlds, including the commodity on which its own Navigators depend.

There's an awkwardness here that the final confrontation doesn't remove. Paul has threatened the continued existence of spice, and the Guild has yielded because it takes him seriously. Yet the result he seeks includes an imperial position within inhabited space. If movement between worlds failed, the consequences would spread through the realm he intends to rule. This makes preserving transport a practical interest for Paul. It doesn't soften his threat retrospectively. It helps explain why compelling the Guild to cooperate is more useful to him than destroying its capacity to operate.

By Dune Messiah, Paul's religious war has extended across other worlds. Whatever authority his followers claim in his name, their campaigns take place in a civilization where interstellar transport remains necessary. Paul's visions don't carry a military formation or deliver supplies. Reading his imperial position this way brings out a burden that can disappear behind the scale of his foresight. He must live with the results of actions taken far from Arrakis, and those distant actions depend on material arrangements that existed before he became ruler.

For the Guild, continued service preserves its business while avoiding a complete break with the ruler who controls its essential supply.

This leaves room for bargaining without making the two sides equally strong. Paul can threaten a condition essential to the Guild's survival. The Guild provides a service his empire continues to use. Those facts don't cancel each other out. They describe why an organization can submit at a decisive confrontation and remain necessary afterward. The representatives can accept Paul's demands without handing him the trained Navigators, vessels and accumulated practice of their institution as though these were another title attached to the imperial throne.

For Paul, the remaining problem is how to rule through institutions whose cooperation doesn't amount to loyalty. The Guild can recognize his position and continue carrying the traffic of imperial life without becoming part of House Atreides. Its Navigators still have work that the throne can't perform for them. Paul holds Arrakis and the spice leverage that brought its representatives to terms. Across the distances separating his subjects, Guild ships remain part of how his rule reaches beyond the planet where he won it.
