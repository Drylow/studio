Ixian navigation machines give a ship a way to make an interstellar passage without depending on a Guild Navigator's spice-fed foresight. For the Guild, that reaches into the particular service around which its monopoly developed. A rival ship alone wouldn't have done the same thing. The great Houses already had vessels of their own, and they still needed Guild transport. A machine that can take over the navigational task changes what a ship's operators have to obtain before they can depart. The later novels bring that alternative into the working world of interstellar travel.

There's a useful distinction here between building a vessel and making its passage possible. In Dune, the Guild's Navigators supply the limited prescience on which safe interstellar navigation depends. Ships and their machinery still carry the passengers and cargo. The Ixian development addresses the Navigator's part in that arrangement. Herbert doesn't supply an engineering account of its calculations, so there's no need to picture a mechanical imitation of every sensation a Navigator experiences. The practical result is clear enough: navigation no longer has to depend exclusively on that trained, spice-dependent human capacity.

That matters because the Guild's difficulty under Leto II couldn't be solved by acquiring another ordinary transport vessel. A Navigator aboard it would still need melange. An Ixian navigation machine gives operators an alternative to that particular requirement. It doesn't remove every expense or every difficulty of travel, and it doesn't manufacture the ship around itself. It changes one necessary part of the voyage. For an institution whose bargaining position rested on being the only reliable provider of that part, even this specific change has considerable consequences.

In God Emperor of Dune, the Ixians conceal the creation of Hwi Noree from Leto's prescient observation.

The concealed place associated with Hwi is especially helpful for separating two developments that can become muddled together. Concealment from prescience concerns whether someone such as Leto can perceive what's happening. Navigation concerns finding a safe course for a ship. A hidden room doesn't transport its occupants between planets. A navigational system doesn't, merely by finding a course, hide those occupants from a prescient observer. Ixian work reaches into both areas, and the later books bring both capabilities into spacecraft. They remain different jobs even when one vessel has both.

A no-ship carries that concealment into travel. In Heretics of Dune and Chapterhouse: Dune, such ships are part of the circumstances in which the Bene Gesserit move, preserve people and conduct their affairs. Their invisibility to prescient search has obvious value in a universe where foresight has helped rulers locate threats and anticipate opponents. But the term doesn't mean that a vessel has ceased to require navigation. It describes a protection against discovery. The navigation machinery answers the separate problem of how the vessel makes its passage without a Navigator guiding it.

That possibility would put pressure on Guild bargaining even where an operator continued to use Guild services. This is a political implication of the alternative, rather than a tariff schedule supplied by the novels. In the earlier arrangement, refusing the Guild's terms could leave a House without the interstellar movement it needed. Once another navigational method exists, the Guild has to contend with departures that don't require one of its Navigators. Its accumulated experience and transport capacity can remain useful, while the claim that every voyage must pass through its specialized human service becomes harder to sustain.

There's also a difference between reducing demand for Navigators and changing the lives of Navigators already dependent on spice. An Ixian machine doesn't reverse the bodily condition described through Edric in Dune Messiah. It supplies an alternative means of doing the work. The Guild's people and its existing resources don't disappear when that alternative becomes available.

By Chapterhouse: Dune, the no-ship sheltering Duncan Idaho, Murbella and Scytale makes these changes part of everyday life aboard a particular vessel. Its concealment helps make it a place of confinement and protection, while its navigational capability allows departure without a Guild Navigator. Duncan's eventual escape with Sheeana and others takes place in that setting. The ship can leave, carrying its occupants beyond the arrangements meant to contain them. Somewhere aboard it, machinery performs the navigational task for which earlier travelers needed a spice-dependent person.
