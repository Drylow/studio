A Guild Navigator needs to find a course a ship can survive. That task gives foresight a particular purpose. Among the possible outcomes ahead, the Navigator seeks passage that won't end in disaster. In Dune, Paul brings that same broad capacity for seeing possibilities into questions of war, succession and religion. He's concerned with what people will do after a voyage has ended, including people who have never set foot aboard the ship. The difference helps explain why the Guild can guide interstellar travel and still find itself cornered by him.

The Guild has organized its service around a repeated practical problem. Ships must reach their destinations, and spice allows its Navigators to exercise the limited prescience that makes safe navigation possible. A successful passage answers the immediate question facing the carrier and its passengers. It doesn't settle the political consequences of delivering an army or moving a noble household. The Atreides can arrive on Arrakis through Guild transport and then encounter dangers their carrier's navigational skill hasn't removed. Safe arrival leaves an enormous part of their future unsettled.

Paul's awareness develops under very different pressures. Before he becomes Emperor, he sees possible futures involving the Fremen and a religious war carried in his name. These visions concern events stretching far beyond his own movements across the desert. They include consequences he fears and struggles to avoid. His problem is already larger than finding a route through an immediate hazard. He has to act among people whose devotion to him may help bring about the future he most wants to prevent.

Even then, Paul's visions don't give him a complete account of everything awaiting him. In Dune, his anticipation of the encounter with Jamis doesn't spare him the uncertainty and pain of their actual confrontation. Possibilities seen beforehand don't arrive with a guarantee that he has understood their meaning. When the encounter becomes a duel, Paul has to fight a real man under Fremen rules. Jessica must watch her son survive an experience that his earlier training and unusual awareness haven't made emotionally simple.

Herbert describes Paul's prescient experience through changing possibilities, with clearer regions and obscured ones. Paul can recognize a dangerous direction without being able to inspect every event along it. His position also changes as he acts. Joining the Fremen, surviving their tests and accepting a place in their society bring him into circumstances that once lay ahead of him. Seeing possibilities doesn't let him remain outside the sequence of events. Each decision leaves him living with people who respond to what he's done.

The Guild's conduct at the end of Dune invites a particular interpretation of its foresight. An institution that repeatedly selects survivable routes may grow reluctant to commit itself where no safe continuation is visible. That's a reading of its response to Paul, rather than a rule governing every Navigator's temperament. The representatives confronted with danger to the spice supply accommodate him. Whatever futures their abilities help them perceive, they don't find a way to preserve their accustomed position by refusing his demands.

There are practical reasons for that caution beyond any habit formed through navigation. Guild ships carry the forces and commerce of an empire, while the Navigators depend on a substance produced on Arrakis. A political gamble can therefore endanger the means by which the Guild continues working. Paul threatens a requirement shared across its voyages. Under those conditions, accepting an unwelcome ruler can look safer than supporting resistance that puts spice at risk. The Guild's reach across inhabited worlds gives it plenty to lose.

Paul is prepared to enter a danger the Guild wants to avoid. His threat places the future availability of spice at stake, even though his own imperial ambitions also require interstellar travel. That willingness changes the negotiation. The Guild can't assume he'll preserve the arrangement merely because preserving it would benefit him. His visions haven't freed him from terrible outcomes, and his conduct makes the representatives face a possibility they can't comfortably dismiss. Their familiar preference for continuity gives him something to press against.

Dune Messiah provides a more personal example of what a Navigator's prescience can do. Edric takes part in the meeting with the Reverend Mother Gaius Helen Mohiam, Princess Irulan and the Tleilaxu Face Dancer Scytale. His presence helps conceal the participants from Paul's prescient sight. Here the capacity used by the Guild has a political application away from the work of guiding a ship. Edric can obstruct another prescient observer, which makes him useful to people trying to act without Paul's foreknowledge.

That meeting also puts a limit on any simple ranking of their abilities. Paul sees far into the political future, yet Edric can help create a region he can't clearly observe.

For Paul, such obscurity is part of the world he has to inhabit. His visions can't replace every conversation, investigation or encounter with another person. For Edric, concealment gives the conspirators room to act without supplying certainty about how all their actions will unfold. Those are different limitations attached to different uses of foresight. The Guild Navigator can be effective within his particular task and still participate in a political undertaking whose outcome exceeds what he can secure.

Edric can help hide a meeting from the Emperor. He still has to sit with the other conspirators and depend on what they decide to do.
