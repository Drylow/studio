The prohibition inherited from the Butlerian Jihad concerns machines made in the likeness of a human mind. In Dune, that prohibition is part of the religious and social order people grow up with. It helps explain why a great House employs a person like Thufir Hawat, and why the Guild's navigational service depends on an unusual human capacity. The Imperium still has sophisticated machinery. What it has rejected is a category of machinery that would do the work associated with human thought.

That leaves a practical problem for a civilization spread across planets. House Atreides needs to assess threats, manage its affairs, and prepare for an uncertain political appointment. Interstellar carriers need a way to navigate safely. These demands haven't disappeared because thinking machines are forbidden. Specialized human training has become part of the answer. Herbert introduces an Imperium where people can develop capacities far beyond ordinary experience, and where institutions organize themselves around those capacities. The people involved aren't interchangeable, even when their work answers a need once associated with computation.

Thufir Hawat gives that arrangement a familiar place inside the Atreides household. He's a Mentat as well as the Duke's Master of Assassins, and his work involves assessing information about enemies, security, and political danger. When Leto accepts Arrakis, Hawat has to consider what the Harkonnens have left behind and what they might be preparing. His value lies partly in his ability to bring information together and reason through its consequences. The Duke can consult him, challenge an assessment, or give him another problem to examine.

A Mentat's conclusions still depend on the information available. Extraordinary analysis doesn't guarantee a sound judgment when crucial facts are false or missing.

The Guild Navigator's specialized contribution belongs to a different kind of uncertainty. A Navigator uses limited prescience to find a safe course. Hawat examines information and works out implications; the Navigator's foresight reaches into possible outcomes in a way ordinary analysis can't. Both capacities matter to the Imperium, but calling both of them human replacements for computers can blur their actual jobs. A Duke seeking an assessment of Harkonnen intentions needs something different from a carrier seeking a usable route between worlds.

Paul's education brings the distinction into one person: his Mentat training and his developing prescience are separate capacities. His visions don't mean that every trained analyst can see possible futures.

A House can retain its own Mentat without gaining an independent equivalent of Guild navigation. Hawat's analysis helps Leto govern and prepare, but it doesn't supply the foresight needed to guide a heighliner.

There's a tempting explanation that takes this too far: perhaps the prohibition alone made the Guild's exact arrangement inevitable. Dune gives us reason to connect the rejection of thinking machines with the cultivation of specialized human abilities. It doesn't give a complete account of every alternative considered or every stage by which the Guild secured its position. Its monopoly also belongs to the political and economic setting of the novel. The religious prohibition helps explain the need these specialists answer, while the institution's particular history remains much less fully described.

Nor does this society reject every machine that assists a person. The Atreides use ornithopters, shields, and other complex equipment without treating technology itself as forbidden. Their world depends on manufactured devices. The distinction concerns the work assigned to machines and the kind of mindlike machinery the prohibition excludes. That distinction can feel unfamiliar because the Imperium combines advanced equipment with demanding human disciplines. Within the setting, though, the combination is ordinary enough that a Duke can rely on a Mentat and travel by spacecraft without seeing a conflict between those arrangements.
