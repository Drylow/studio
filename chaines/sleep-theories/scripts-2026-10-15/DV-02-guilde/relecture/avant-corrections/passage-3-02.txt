The Atreides move to Arrakis begins with a practical problem: the Duke's household has to leave Caladan, and his people and equipment have to go with him. An imperial grant can give Leto authority over another planet, but it can't carry a soldier or a crate across the distance. In Dune, that part of the transfer belongs to the Spacing Guild. Before the Atreides can govern their new possession, they have to use the transport system that connects it to the world they're leaving.

Caladan has been the Atreides home for generations. Leaving it means moving the working household around a ruling family, including the people responsible for security and administration. Paul and Jessica are among the travelers, along with the Duke and the personnel who make his rule possible. The relocation therefore has a different character from a visit by an imperial envoy. Leto is transferring the center of his government. The destination needs to receive a functioning House, with enough of its organization intact to begin work.

Herbert gives this movement a useful physical distinction. The Atreides have their own ships, while the Guild provides the larger interstellar passage. A House frigate can enter a Guild heighliner and travel within that transport arrangement. Owning a vessel doesn't remove the need for the carrier. That can seem strange if we imagine every spacecraft as a complete means of reaching any other world. In the imperial setting, a vessel's usefulness depends on which part of the journey it can undertake without outside help.

The heighliner makes the scale of that arrangement easier to picture. It's a carrier large enough to accommodate other vessels and their loads. The Atreides don't have to cease being an organized expedition when they use it. Their craft and the people aboard them can become part of a much larger movement between systems.  The distinction between the House's vessels and the Guild's carrier already explains the essential arrangement.

There are movements at the planetary end of the journey as well. Arriving in a system isn't the same task as establishing people and equipment on the surface. Dune's ships and smaller craft belong to a setting where travel has different stages, each with its own demands.  The Guild connects the worlds; the House then has to occupy its residence, organize its forces, and take up its responsibilities on Arrakis.

That last part is why the cargo matters alongside the named passengers. The Duke's family can't run Arrakis through their presence alone. His soldiers need the means to operate, and his administrators need a working organization around them.  What we do know is that the Atreides arrive as a governing and military presence. Interstellar transport has made that transfer of organized activity possible.

The political danger of the assignment doesn't make the logistical requirement disappear. Leto understands that Arrakis is hazardous ground for his House. He nevertheless has to bring people there if he's going to assume the position offered to him. He can't exercise the new appointment entirely from Caladan, leaving everyone safely at home. This gives the relocation its particular weight: the Guild carries a House into a situation its Duke already has reason to fear. Passage can be successfully provided even when the destination is dangerous.

The same distinction helps explain how an empire spread across planets can conduct ordinary business. People have to reach appointments, goods have to reach places where they're wanted, and forces have to reach the territories they're assigned to defend. The Atreides transfer gathers these needs into a single example. Its political purpose is unusually consequential, but the underlying service is familiar throughout the Imperium. Guild transport makes a decision taken on one world capable of producing a physical arrival on another.

When the Atreides settle into Arrakeen, attention turns toward the people and dangers waiting there. The journey has done its work, so the carrier can recede from the immediate story. Yet the move has shown what Guild service delivers before any dispute about its influence begins. A family, its retainers, and its forces have crossed from their established home to a new seat of rule. The House's own vessels belong to that movement, carried through the interstellar interval by the Guild's heighliner.
