The people who return from the Scattering in Heretics of Dune come from human communities beyond the old imperial center. Their return changes what a journey means in this setting. In the first novel, the great Houses move within an empire whose interstellar passages depend on the Guild. By the later books, people arrive from a human expansion that the old institutions can't fully describe. The Honored Matres bring military force and their own demands. They haven't come back to ask the surviving powers how humanity should be organized.

That expansion follows the end of Leto II's rule and the hardships that accompany its collapse. People leave the worlds held within his long peace and spread outward in the Scattering. Herbert doesn't give us a complete map of where they settle. The later story takes place among those who remained and those who return, so its characters encounter the consequences through particular arrivals. The Bene Gesserit have to deal with people whose abilities and experiences developed outside the arrangements the Sisterhood knows.

Under Leto, the Guild's need for spice gives him a direct means of restricting it. His command of distribution reaches into the conditions under which Navigators can work. An institution with ships and trained personnel still has to obtain what those personnel require. After his death, the spread of humanity and the availability of alternative navigation loosen that particular constraint. As a political consequence, withholding spice from the Guild can no longer be assumed to stop every community's access to interstellar travel.

There's a useful comparison here with the Emperor's position during the Atreides crisis. Shaddam can send Sardaukar to Arrakis, and the Harkonnens can assemble the force needed for their attack, but those forces travel through Guild transport. The rivals share a carrier even while they fight over a planet. In the later novels, armed groups returning from the Scattering don't fit into that familiar arrangement of Emperor, Houses and Guild. The Bene Gesserit must confront military arrivals without an imperial transport monopoly they can treat as the common limit on everyone's movements.

The Honored Matres make that change especially uncomfortable. They can attack the old worlds, seize positions and pursue enemies across space. Their ability to move doesn't make them willing to coexist with the people they find. On Gammu, the struggle around the young Duncan Idaho involves pursuit, concealment and escape from forces that have already established themselves there. Travel has become available to powers whose violence the surviving institutions can't contain. More independent movement means that the old center has fewer ways to keep a distant conflict distant.

The destruction of Rakis gives this wider setting a concrete loss. The Honored Matres destroy the planet that once supplied the spice around which imperial transport was organized. The attack is devastating, and it destroys the familiar home of the sandworms. Yet the later story continues across other worlds. Ships still carry people, the Bene Gesserit continue their work, and the struggle with the Honored Matres continues. The old planet's destruction doesn't bring all human movement to an end.

Before that destruction, a sandworm is removed from Rakis and taken to Chapterhouse. The Bene Gesserit undertake the transformation of their own world to establish the worm cycle there. That effort shows how much spice still matters after the Guild's exclusive position has weakened. The Sisterhood uses melange for purposes that navigation machines don't replace, including the practices of its Reverend Mothers. Moving a worm and changing a planet are demanding responses to the loss of Rakis. A substitute for a Navigator leaves those other needs in place.

Chapterhouse therefore contains an awkward combination of greater independence and continuing vulnerability. The Bene Gesserit can shelter people aboard a no-ship and work toward a new source of spice, but their worlds remain exposed to attack. Odrade has to make decisions about scattered communities, resources and opponents who won't wait for the Sisterhood to become secure. Independent transport gives her organization ways to act. It also gives its enemies ways to reach the places she's trying to preserve. No single improvement in travel resolves that practical struggle.

The Scattering adds distance to that independence. Humanity's continued existence no longer rests entirely with the inhabited worlds familiar to the old empire. The returning groups are proof that human life has continued elsewhere, even though the characters don't possess a full account of it. Leto's Golden Path is tied to that survival through dispersal. The later novels give the idea an uneasy form: communities survive beyond the center, and some of their descendants return as threats to the people who stayed behind.

By Chapterhouse: Dune, the Sisterhood has to negotiate, fight and adapt within that enlarged human world. It can't recover safety by restoring a single imperial arrangement, and the Guild can't make every departure depend on its Navigators again. Ships can carry enemies as well as refugees, and distant populations can remain outside the reach of the powers struggling over the old worlds. On Chapterhouse, the Bene Gesserit continue preparing desert for the sandworms while watching for attackers whose movements they can't prevent.
