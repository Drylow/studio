At the end of Dune, the Guild representatives have to accept Paul's demands while the ships above Arrakis still belong to a working transport system. His victory hasn't supplied the Great Houses with another way to cross the distances between their worlds. Their vessels still require passage, and the Guild still provides it. The political implication is practical: Paul can force an accommodation over spice, but he can't personally take over the work of every voyage. Becoming Emperor gives him new reasons to keep that work going.

The Atreides move to Arrakis gives us a familiar example of what remains necessary. Leto brings his household and military forces from Caladan through Guild transport. His authority over those people doesn't carry them between planets on its own. Paul inherits an empire whose powerful families face the same requirement. A House can command its soldiers, maintain its ships and possess a planet, yet moving beyond that planet involves an organization outside its household. Changing the person on the imperial throne leaves that distance in place.

The Guild's enormous carriers make movement available across an empire divided among separate rulers. That arrangement matters after Paul's accession because governing involves people and goods arriving where they're needed. Arrakis itself has long been part of this circulation, with spice leaving the planet for use elsewhere. Paul's command of its supply gives him extraordinary bargaining strength, while the substance still needs to reach those who consume it. The Guild has the means to carry cargo between worlds, including the commodity on which its own Navigators depend.

There's an awkwardness here that the final confrontation doesn't remove. Paul has threatened the continued existence of spice, and the Guild has yielded because it takes him seriously. Yet the result he seeks includes an imperial position within inhabited space. If movement between worlds failed, the consequences would spread through the realm he intends to rule. This makes preserving transport a practical interest for Paul. It doesn't soften his threat retrospectively. It helps explain why compelling the Guild to cooperate is more useful to him than destroying its capacity to operate.

Shaddam's presence on Arrakis provides a direct comparison. The old Emperor brings military force to the planet, but that expedition also depends on interstellar carriage. Imperial troops and Guild transport have separate jobs. Removing Shaddam doesn't merge those jobs into Paul's person or his household. Paul gains the throne through the settlement at Arrakis, while the organization that carried imperial forces there retains its specialized function. He has changed the conditions under which that organization must deal with the throne. The need to carry people across space survives the defeated Emperor.

By Dune Messiah, Paul's religious war has extended across other worlds. Whatever authority his followers claim in his name, their campaigns take place in a civilization where interstellar transport remains necessary. Paul's visions don't carry a military formation or deliver supplies. Reading his imperial position this way brings out a burden that can disappear behind the scale of his foresight. He must live with the results of actions taken far from Arrakis, and those distant actions depend on material arrangements that existed before he became ruler.

The Guild, meanwhile, has its own reason to continue serving a ruler it has been forced to accept. Its Navigators require spice. Refusing all dealings with Paul would put it at odds with the man who holds the supply it needs to keep working. Its customers also remain scattered across the empire. The transport monopoly still has value because those customers need passage, even though the Guild has lost its ability to treat Arrakis as a political problem it can safely manage from a distance.

This leaves room for bargaining without making the two sides equally strong. Paul can threaten a condition essential to the Guild's survival. The Guild provides a service his empire continues to use. Those facts don't cancel each other out. They describe why an organization can submit at a decisive confrontation and remain necessary afterward. The representatives can accept Paul's demands without handing him the trained Navigators, vessels and accumulated practice of their institution as though these were another title attached to the imperial throne.

Paul's own background makes the distinction quite concrete. He has learned to fight under Gurney Halleck and Duncan Idaho, and he has received Mentat training from Thufir Hawat. His abilities expand far beyond what his teachers expected. None of those accomplishments makes him the workforce of an interstellar carrier. Even his prescience doesn't place him aboard every ship that needs guidance. As a political implication of the setting, an Emperor with unusual personal abilities still needs other people to perform specialized work throughout his realm.

Dune Messiah also gives us a glimpse of how uncomfortable this continuing arrangement is for the Guild. Edric participates in a conspiracy against Paul alongside people whose interests differ from his own. The details of their undertaking belong to that gathering, but his participation already tells us something about the imperial settlement. The Guild hasn't disappeared, and compliance hasn't made every Guild interest identical to Paul's. A Navigator can come into contact with the new imperial order while seeking a way to change the circumstances under which his organization must exist.

Continued service can therefore coexist with political hostility. That shouldn't be mistaken for an ordinary commercial relationship with no consequences beyond payment. Paul has demonstrated that he can put the Guild's future at risk, and the Guild retains a necessary place in the movement of his empire. Each voyage takes place within that altered situation, although the novels don't require us to imagine a new negotiation for every departure. The larger accommodation allows transport to continue while leaving serious reasons for resentment among those who provide it.

For Paul, the remaining problem is how to rule through institutions whose cooperation doesn't amount to loyalty. The Guild can recognize his position and continue carrying the traffic of imperial life without becoming part of House Atreides. Its Navigators still have work that the throne can't perform for them. Paul holds Arrakis and the spice leverage that brought its representatives to terms. Across the distances separating his subjects, Guild ships remain part of how his rule reaches beyond the planet where he won it.
