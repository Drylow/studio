By the time of God Emperor of Dune, Arrakis has changed so much that the Guild's old dependence has become harder to escape. Water and vegetation have spread across a world once dominated by desert. The sandworms that produced melange have disappeared, apart from the living continuation of their kind within Leto II's transformed body. The Guild still needs spice for its Navigators, and the familiar source of fresh supplies is gone. Leto holds the reserves on which its continued service depends.

There's a practical difference between bargaining over the harvest of a desert planet and asking a ruler to release something from his stockpile. Under the old imperial arrangement, spice mining was an ongoing enterprise. Harvesters went onto the sand, crews watched for worms, and shipments carried the recovered spice away. Leto's empire lives on accumulated supplies under his supervision. A Guild representative can't answer an unfavorable allocation by arranging a larger harvest. The desert economy that once made such an answer possible has been dismantled over the course of his reign.

Leto has ruled for more than three thousand years when the novel opens. That's long enough for dependence on his distributions to become an ordinary condition of institutional life. Guild officers don't face a temporary interruption that they can wait out until an imperial succession. The same ruler remains in place across generations of the people who serve him. His extraordinary lifespan gives his management of spice a continuity no ordinary emperor could personally maintain. The Guild must conduct its affairs within that enduring arrangement.

For a Navigator, spice remains necessary to the work of finding safe passage between stars. The Guild's ships and accumulated experience don't remove that requirement. Its organization can retain its skills, preserve its procedures and continue to carry passengers, yet it still needs access to melange. Leto's reserves therefore reach into the operation of the transport system itself. He doesn't have to replace the Guild's personnel or take charge of each journey to make its leadership attentive to his wishes. Decisions about distribution already give him a powerful means of pressure.

The change on Arrakis is especially important because it prevents the Guild from treating this as a dispute with a difficult supplier whose competitors might welcome its business. In the original imperial order, rival Houses could compete for the privilege of administering the same planet. Their competition never created another Arrakis. Under Leto, even that succession of managers has given way to his personal rule. Melange remains bound to the history and ecology of one world, while the usable reserves sit within an order he has spent millennia shaping.

Paul had brought the Guild to terms by threatening the future of spice. Leto's method extends that vulnerability into daily administration. He possesses the stored resource, and he determines how much of it passes into other hands. The Guild must keep receiving what its Navigators require while continuing to run a service used throughout the empire. That makes each allocation consequential beyond the organization receiving it. Restrict the carrier's essential supply, and the effects can reach the people and institutions that expect the carrier to remain available.

This doesn't mean every Guild journey requires a fresh personal instruction from Leto. The political implication is that the Guild has to plan its continuing operations around access he can restrict. A ship already available for service doesn't become a replacement source of spice. Neither does a long record of successful voyages. The organization's achievements remain useful, but they can't refill its reserves. Leto can leave its specialized work in its own hands and still establish the material conditions under which that work continues.

Other institutions live with versions of the same problem. The Bene Gesserit also need melange, and God Emperor of Dune shows their concern with the supplies Leto permits them. For the Sisterhood, spice reaches into the practices through which they preserve their abilities and their continuity. For the Guild, it reaches into navigation. These are different organizations with different tasks, yet both must account for the same ruler's distribution decisions. Neither can assume that its importance to human society will secure an unlimited supply.

Leto's transformed Arrakis gives this arrangement a visible setting. The Sareer preserves a remaining desert within a planet that has otherwise moved away from its old aridity. Leto's body carries sandtrout joined with his human form, and his existence holds the possibility of the worms' eventual return. The Guild, meanwhile, has to meet its present needs from available spice. Whatever the long future of Arrakis might be, a Navigator working during Leto's reign depends on resources that can be distributed now. An eventual ecological change can't supply a current shortage.

Within Leto's Peace, restrictions on movement accompany the concentration of spice. The Guild continues to exist, but the empire no longer has the same freedom of circulation that supported the rivalries of the earlier Houses. From the Guild's position, the two conditions reinforce each other. Its essential resource is rationed by a ruler who also restricts the wider society in which transport takes place. The carrier has fewer grounds for assuming that the empire will accommodate its preferred level of activity merely because interstellar passage once made it indispensable.

Leto's own account of his long project concerns humanity's survival and the Golden Path. Those are his stated purposes, and they extend far beyond the Guild. For the institution trying to maintain its Navigators and its service, however, the immediate arrangement is concrete. The ruler has spice that it needs. He can withhold part of that supply. Its old monopoly doesn't give it a corresponding way to compel him to release more. Refusing transport would also endanger the activity through which the Guild sustains its own place in the empire.

The Guild survives this long subordination. Its ships, Navigators and institutional knowledge remain part of human life under Leto, even as its bargaining position has narrowed. That survival matters: he has kept a useful carrier in operation while making its continued functioning dependent on his allocations. Across the transformed Arrakis, there are no harvesting crews bringing in a fresh desert crop to relieve that dependence. The spice reaching the Guild comes from reserves, and Leto decides what leaves them.
