A spice harvester on Arrakis collects something the desert has already produced. By the time its machinery reaches a deposit, the processes that brought melange to the surface have happened out of sight. The crew's immediate work is to gather the spice and leave before a worm reaches them. That makes the worm an obvious danger to production. Yet the creature approaching the harvester belongs to the same living cycle on which the harvest depends. Removing that danger from Arrakis would have consequences far beyond making a crew's working day safer.

Duke Leto encounters the practical side of this during his inspection of spice operations with Paul and Liet-Kynes. A worm approaches a working harvester, and the carryall needed to lift the machine away fails to arrive. Leto abandons the equipment and rescues the men. The incident gives Paul an early look at the conditions behind the planet's wealth. Even an operation with aircraft and specialized machinery has to work around a creature it can't keep away from the spice sands.

The larger connection appears in the ecological account developed through Pardot Kynes and his son Liet. The great worms are the mature part of a life cycle that includes small creatures known as sandtrout. These live beneath the surface and seal away water. Their activity helps maintain the dry conditions in which adult worms can survive. Water on Arrakis therefore has a role that isn't apparent from looking at a stretch of bare sand. Beneath that surface, living things help determine where water can remain available.

The sandtrout's part in spice production also takes place underground. Water and their excretions enter into the formation of a pre-spice mass. As that mass develops, gases build up, and a spice blow can bring material to the surface. Exposure at the surface completes the production of the melange that harvesters collect. Herbert gives enough of this process to connect the saleable substance with underground life, water, and desert conditions. A patch of spice is the visible result of activity that began before any harvesting crew arrived.

That changes how a deposit looks in the context of Guild transport. Melange can be gathered, stored, and carried away, so a shipment can seem separate from Arrakis once it leaves the planet. A Navigator elsewhere can use spice without standing anywhere near a worm. But replacing what that Navigator consumes still depends on the desert continuing to produce it. A warehouse contains the result of an earlier cycle. However carefully its contents are guarded, the warehouse can't perform the work of sandtrout beneath the sands.

The adult worm's vulnerability to water makes the relationship especially important. The sandtrout isolate water, while the mature creatures inhabit the dry world that activity helps sustain. Fremen knowledge includes both sides of this relationship. Their lives require careful conservation of water, and their dealings with worms require an understanding of how dangerous water is to them. What sustains a Fremen household can kill a maker. Those facts belong to the same planet, so changes intended to improve human life have to take the worms into account.

The Fremen obtain the poisonous Water of Life by drowning a small maker. This gives them practical knowledge of a vulnerability that will matter when Paul threatens the source of spice.

Kynes approaches Arrakis on a different scale. His work concerns the connections among water, plants, animals, and the conditions that allow them to survive. The Fremen project grows from that understanding. Their stored water and efforts toward changing the environment belong to a plan extending beyond the needs of a single settlement. Greener conditions would affect the dry habitat of the makers. Kynes's ecological thinking therefore has to consider what happens across the system when one part of it changes, including the desert needed for spice production.

The Guild sits farther along that chain. Its Navigators need melange for the limited prescience used in navigation, and the organization needs supplies to keep its transport service operating. Its bargaining can concern access to harvested spice, including spice obtained through arrangements outside ordinary imperial channels. The living source imposes a different sort of requirement. Guild representatives can't negotiate with a pre-spice mass or persuade sandtrout to continue their work in unsuitable conditions. Commercial access depends on processes that don't respond to commercial agreements.

This is why a threat to continued spice production reaches beyond an attack on a harvester. Losing a machine interrupts collection at a particular place. Stored spice can remain available, and other equipment can keep working. Damage to the conditions that generate new spice would reach future harvests as well. The distinction matters even without knowing how much melange anyone has stored. A reserve can supply consumption for a time, but every use draws on material already produced. Its usefulness doesn't remove the need for a living source.

The makers give that source a concrete presence. One can approach a harvester and force an evacuation; another, much smaller, can be brought into a Fremen rite. Beneath the open desert, sandtrout help maintain the conditions from which future spice will emerge. All of these belong to the world behind the Guild's cargo. When a shipment leaves Arrakis, the harvester's work is finished for that load. Beneath the sands, the activity needed for another harvest has to continue.
