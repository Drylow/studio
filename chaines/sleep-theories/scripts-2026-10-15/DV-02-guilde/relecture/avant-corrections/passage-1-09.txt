The Baron expects the attack on House Atreides to burden Harkonnen finances long after the fighting has ended. In Dune, his discussion with Rabban makes the expense part of the occupation that follows. Arrakis has to produce the wealth that will repay what the Baron has spent to recover it. Guild transport is a major part of that bill. Before the Harkonnens can draw income from their restored possession, they've committed resources to getting an overwhelming force onto the planet.

That expense helps explain the scale of the Baron's commitment. He's trying to destroy a rival House whose soldiers have become dangerous enough to concern the Emperor. A modest expedition would risk leaving Leto with troops, leadership and time to recover. The attack therefore brings strength well beyond what the Atreides expect. Paying to move that strength is part of making the assault decisive. The Baron accepts a heavy financial burden because he expects the removal of House Atreides and the recovery of Arrakis to justify it.

Leto knows an attack is likely. The danger of the assignment is discussed before his House leaves Caladan, and his commanders prepare for Harkonnen action. Still, anticipating hostility doesn't tell them how much their enemy is willing to spend. Their preparations take place within an understanding of what deploying an army usually demands. The Baron's willingness to pay for an unusually large operation helps defeat those expectations. In that sense, wealth affects the battle before soldiers begin fighting: it allows an enemy to arrive in strength that defenders haven't adequately prepared to meet.

The Guild's position matters because the Baron can't solve this expense by ordering his own ship crews to make the interstellar journey independently. House forces need the Guild's transport system to reach Arrakis. He can command soldiers and arrange equipment, yet passage remains something he must obtain from another institution. With no established competing carrier available, he lacks the ordinary bargaining option of taking the same expedition elsewhere for transport. His military plan has to accommodate the cost of the service he can actually use.

It's worth keeping that conclusion close to what happens in the novel. The attack is expensive, and the Guild's charges contribute heavily to the expense. Herbert doesn't give us a tariff that would let us calculate the price of any other invasion. What the Harkonnen operation does show is that moving a force between planets can require a financial commitment large enough to shape policy afterward. The Baron wins possession of Arrakis with obligations already attached to his victory. Rabban inherits the work of extracting the income his uncle expects.

For the people of Arrakis, that connection is particularly harsh. The money spent on bringing the attackers becomes another reason for demanding more from the conquered world. When the Baron puts Rabban back in charge, he wants revenue and accepts brutal methods of obtaining it. The consequences of transport costs extend into the occupation through those instructions. The soldiers have arrived and the Atreides government has fallen, but the expense of the journey remains present in what Harkonnen rule requires of the population.

Arrakis makes this gamble possible because its spice production promises extraordinary income. The Baron isn't spending to acquire a possession with little prospect of repayment. He's returning to the source of the substance on which imperial life depends, and he expects renewed exploitation to restore his finances. That expectation ties his military spending to future production. His victory must be followed by working spice operations and shipments that can be sold. Possessing the planet won't repay the expedition if its wealth can't be extracted.

There's a useful comparison within the attack itself. The Harkonnens employ artillery against Atreides troops who take refuge in caves. In the circumstances of the battle, an old weapon has a specific use, and the Baron is willing to treat the equipment as expendable after it has done its job. Moving an army presents a different practical problem. The force has to reach Arrakis before any of its weapons can be useful. Clever decisions about equipment may improve an assault, while the requirement for interstellar passage remains an expense the commander has to face.

The Emperor's Sardaukar add another layer to this commitment. Their participation gives the assault military strength that the Baron couldn't obtain merely by recruiting more Harkonnen soldiers. They arrive disguised as Harkonnen troops, since the Emperor's involvement must be concealed. For the Atreides, the resulting force combines Harkonnen expenditure with imperial military support. The attack's success grows out of this arrangement as well as treachery within Leto's household. The transport bill belongs to a wider preparation in which money purchases access to the battlefield and allies provide some of the force used there.

A deployment on this scale also gives the Baron reasons to want a rapid result. Once he's paid to bring overwhelming strength to Arrakis, a surviving Atreides army would leave the purpose of that expense unfinished. Continued resistance could demand further commitments while delaying the income he wants from the planet. That's a practical implication of his investment, rather than a separate Guild rule. The assault's concentration of force, its reliance on surprise and Yueh's betrayal all help produce the swift collapse the Baron needs. His spending is intended to leave as little unfinished fighting as possible.

For other Houses, the same transport monopoly makes military planning a matter of available wealth as well as available troops. A ruler considering an expedition has to account for passage before measuring what the army might achieve after landing. Repeated interventions would require repeated commitments of resources, so the ability to win a battle doesn't automatically make a prolonged campaign affordable. The Harkonnen attack gives that problem a concrete form. Even a rich House recovering a profitable world expects the cost of deployment to reach well into its restored administration.

The Baron's demands on Rabban carry that expense into ordinary rule. The investment that helped put soldiers onto Arrakis becomes pressure on the governor to make the planet pay. This is where the Guild's transport charges acquire a consequence far beyond the journey itself. The Guild doesn't have to govern Arrakis or issue Rabban's orders for its costly service to affect life there. The Baron has already paid to move his war across interstellar space. Under Rabban, the conquered population faces his determination to recover the money.
