Miranda's paintings are still in Henry's home when Tabitha arrives there. That gives Tabitha something unusual to look at: images connected with the town, kept in a house beyond it. Victor isn't beside her to explain them. She hasn't brought him home or asked him to draw what she remembers. Henry shows her work Miranda made before she disappeared with the children, and Tabitha recognizes things that belong to the place she's been trying to escape. The connection reaches into a family home that had continued to exist without Victor.

For Tabitha, the house already has a connection to the town through Victor's lunchbox. The address inside it has led her to Henry. An address can lead someone to an ordinary house, though, without explaining how that house fits into everything else. The paintings add a different kind of connection. Tabitha has encountered the town herself. When she recognizes its imagery in Miranda's work, Henry has something beyond a stranger's account of meeting his missing son. He can show her something his wife left behind.

Henry explains that Miranda began having visions after they took acid together. That's his account of how this part of her life began. Miranda painted what she saw, including images associated with the town and the children who needed help. Henry could observe her painting and hear what she told him. He hadn't accompanied her into the place those paintings concerned. For years, he had lived with the work without being able to compare it with the streets, buildings and dangers Tabitha knew.

That difference matters while they're looking at the pictures. Henry knows who painted them and remembers the circumstances surrounding their creation. Tabitha recognizes their connection to a place she reached much later. Between them, they can bring the paintings into focus in a way neither could manage alone. Henry doesn't have to become an expert on the town for his contribution to help. Tabitha doesn't need to have known Miranda to recognize something from her own experience. Each has a specific part of the story.

The paintings also give Miranda a presence outside Victor's account of losing her. In his childhood recollections, she's the mother who left him and Eloise hidden while she went to try to help the children. In Henry's home, there's work she made before that separation. She spent time trying to put unfamiliar places and experiences into images. The paintings let Tabitha encounter that effort without asking Victor to retrieve another painful detail. Something Miranda did before entering the town has survived in a place her son couldn't reach.

There's a useful comparison with Victor's drawings here. Victor draws things connected with a life he's spent inside the town. Miranda painted visions while she was still living outside it. Their images have different starting points, even when they concern the same strange place. Treating both collections as one continuous illustrated history would lose that distinction. With Victor, a picture may preserve an experience he struggles to explain. With Miranda, Henry describes experiences that reached her before her eventual disappearance. The resemblance invites comparison, while their circumstances help explain what to ask about each picture.

Nothing in that encounter requires Victor to have consciously sent the images to his mother or arranged for Tabitha to find them. Miranda is the person Henry identifies as their painter. Tabitha reaches the house by following the address Victor carried in his lunchbox. Those actions are already strange enough in their consequences. Giving Victor responsibility for the visions would add a role the encounter never shows him performing. His family was caught up in the town's history before he could understand what was happening, and the paintings extend that involvement beyond his own recollections.

By the third-season finale, Tabitha and Jade's returning memories give Miranda's paintings a more particular place in the story. Tabitha recognizes her connection to Miranda, and Jade recognizes his connection to Christopher, within repeated lives and repeated attempts to save the children. That discovery changes how the earlier visit to Henry's house can be understood. Miranda's visions now belong alongside a longer history returning through people who don't initially understand it. Her paintings preserve part of her encounter with that history.

The later revelation doesn't make every mark in Miranda's work a decoded message. It does explain why her connection to the town can reach further back than her arrival there with Victor and Eloise. Her life outside the town wasn't sealed away from the children or the previous attempts to help them. Tabitha's recognition in the house becomes especially significant because she, too, will recover a connection she couldn't explain at the time. The pictures bring those two women's experiences close together before Tabitha understands the extent of that connection.

For Victor, this means his mother's story has sources beyond the things he can remember saying or seeing as a child. Henry has her paintings and his recollections of her life at home. Tabitha brings experiences from the town and, later, memories that connect her with Miranda. That can reduce the need to ask Victor for every missing piece. It can also make the family history harder to talk about, because something he remembers as a child's immediate experience now sits within events extending beyond that childhood.
