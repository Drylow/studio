The Boy in White gives Victor instructions, but he doesn't stay beside him to explain everything that happens afterward. That's an important feature of their connection. Victor can recognize him, remember an encounter with him and take what he says seriously, while still having to work out what to do when he's gone. In the remembered conversation with Christopher, the boy knows about the children and describes a way to help them. He doesn't give Victor a complete account of the place where those children are trapped.

For Victor, that knowledge comes from someone he's encountered since childhood. After the massacre, the Boy in White is there among the remains of the town's ordinary life. Victor has survived an event that has taken away the people he depended on, and this other child appears in its aftermath. Their association begins in a setting where Victor has very little human company available. That helps explain why a later appearance would carry weight for him, even when the boy offers only a short instruction.

When Victor helps Tabitha through the tunnels beneath the town, he connects his presence there with the Boy in White's guidance. Tabitha has fallen into a place where the creatures sleep, and Victor helps her find a way through it. Here, the help has an immediate, practical result. Someone who would've been alone underground has a companion who knows something about the passage. It's understandable that Victor would carry this experience into his judgment of later instructions. He's had reason to act on the boy's guidance before.

That tunnel encounter is a useful comparison with Christopher's remembered conversation. Helping Tabitha through a dangerous place has a result Victor and Tabitha can experience together. The proposal about saving the children reaches much further. It asks an adult to attempt something whose promised result lies beyond what young Victor can check. The same figure can provide both kinds of information, but the demands placed on the people receiving it are different. Christopher is being asked to accept a much larger account of how the town might be escaped.

Tabitha's later encounter with the Boy in White at the lighthouse makes his help harder to describe comfortably. He pushes her from the tower, and she subsequently wakes in a hospital outside the town. The passage succeeds in taking her somewhere the other residents haven't managed to reach. Yet Tabitha doesn't agree to the push or receive a full explanation beforehand. For someone hearing her account, the apparent benefit and the frightening way it happens belong to the same incident. Neither part can be removed without changing what happened to her.

Victor isn't present for everything Tabitha experiences. His relationship with the boy therefore can't give him a complete account of that lighthouse passage, any more than Tabitha's encounter can supply all of Victor's missing childhood details. Each of them has met the figure in a particular situation. Put those situations beside one another, and there's a recurring pattern of direction followed by separation. The boy points someone toward an action or intervenes himself, while the person left behind has to live through the consequences with little explanation.

By season four, the bottle tree gives Victor another specific instruction to bring to the adults around him. The Boy in White warns against uprooting it. This time, the message asks them to leave something in place while a plan involving the tree is going ahead. Victor objects, and Boyd restrains him. For Victor, the warning comes from a familiar figure whose earlier guidance has sometimes helped. Boyd has to respond to Victor's objection during an unfolding decision, without sharing Victor's long experience of the person who delivered it.

The tree warning also changes the kind of help being offered. In the tunnels, Victor can move toward Tabitha and help her walk out. At the tree, carrying the message requires other people to stop an action they've decided to take. Victor can't fulfill the instruction by himself. He needs Boyd and the others to give his report enough weight to alter their plan. The boy has given him a warning without giving him the means to make the group accept it. That leaves Victor trying to turn a private encounter into a public reason to stop.

A protective intention is plausible across these encounters. Tabitha gets help underground, her lighthouse passage takes her outside, and the warning concerns an action followed by danger. Calling the Boy in White wholly benevolent would require more: a clearer account of what he wants for the children and the residents, and how the harm surrounding his interventions fits that aim. Treating him as a proven villain has its own difficulty. That reading would need to explain the useful assistance and establish that he knowingly directs people toward harm for a purpose of his own.
