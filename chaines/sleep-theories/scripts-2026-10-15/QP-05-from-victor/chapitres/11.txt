Christopher used to make people laugh. That's one of the details Victor gives Jade when he describes the man in the old photograph. In a town where people have to hide from creatures after dark, someone who can still make people laugh has a place in ordinary life. Victor remembers that part of him. The Christopher who frightens him later belongs to the same remembered community as the man who once helped make its days bearable. His account includes a change, and that change is what makes Jade's interest in him so uncomfortable.

Jade approaches Christopher through something Victor can recognize: a photograph of a person who was there during his childhood. For Jade, the picture offers a possible connection to the symbol he's been seeing. Victor can supply a name and a history that the image alone can't give him. The photograph preserves Christopher's appearance, but it takes Victor to explain why finding him matters. Even then, Jade and Victor come to that image with different concerns. Jade wants to understand an experience that's happening to him now. Victor remembers what happened around another man who became occupied with it.

Victor says Christopher began seeing the symbol and changed afterward. He remembers that Christopher stopped making people laugh. It's a plain description, and it fits the perspective of someone who knew him as a child. Victor doesn't offer a detailed account of Christopher's private thoughts or describe every step in his investigation. He remembers how being around Christopher felt different. Someone associated with laughter became someone he feared. That gives Jade a warning he can understand, even though it doesn't tell him what the symbol means.

There's a particular difficulty here for Jade. The very thing that makes Christopher alarming also makes him worth investigating. Christopher encountered something Jade recognizes, and the record associated with him suggests that Jade isn't the first person to become absorbed in it. Walking away would leave Jade with experiences he still can't explain. Following Christopher's work may bring him closer to an answer, while Victor's recollection makes that work feel dangerous. Jade keeps pursuing the symbol while it continues to intrude into his life. Victor can't make that intrusion disappear by asking him to stop.

Christopher's journal gives that pursuit something concrete. Jade can examine marks left by an earlier resident rather than rely entirely on a child's remembered impression of an adult. The repeated symbol on its pages shows sustained attention. It doesn't explain every circumstance in which Christopher drew it, or tell Jade what Christopher understood by the end. Still, the journal and Victor's account fit together in a useful way. Victor remembers Christopher becoming preoccupied; the pages give Jade a surviving piece of that preoccupation to study.

For Victor, the concern reaches beyond pages. His mother told him to hide somewhere Christopher didn't know about on the night the town's people died. That instruction places Christopher inside a frightening childhood memory. Miranda considered concealment important enough to make it part of keeping her son safe, and Victor survived to remember her warning. When he encounters Jade following a similar interest, he has a reason to be afraid. He doesn't have to understand everything Christopher did to remember that his mother wanted distance between them that night.

The massacre gives that warning a weight it would never have had on an ordinary morning. Victor emerges into a town whose familiar people are dead, and Christopher's change belongs to his recollection of the period before it. But Miranda's instruction doesn't identify who carried out every death, and Victor's account doesn't make Christopher the sole perpetrator of the massacre. What stays close to Victor is the practical experience of being hidden while something terrible happens beyond his shelter. His fear of Christopher grows out of that situation, even where the full sequence remains beyond his view.

The symbol also has a visible context outside Christopher's behavior. In season two, Jade sees children lying on stone slabs beneath an opening where roots form the shape he's been pursuing. That encounter takes his investigation beyond an old resident's drawings. The shape belongs to the town's history of the children as well. Christopher's repeated attention to it may therefore have involved recognition of something real and terrible. Nothing in Victor's description requires a mechanism in which the symbol automatically takes over whoever sees it. Jade's encounter gives him a subject to investigate, though pursuing it exposes him to further distress.

By the end of season three, Christopher's connection to Jade has become much more personal. Jade and Tabitha recognize that they've been there before, through lives connected with Christopher and Miranda, and that their attempts to save the children reach back further still.  Christopher is now part of Jade's own earlier involvement in the town. That changes how his journal can be understood. Jade has been examining the work of a man whose unfinished efforts are connected to the life he's living now.

That later knowledge also changes the possible meaning of Christopher's absorption. His fixation may have included a struggle with a past he didn't initially understand, much as Jade struggles with his own experiences. Victor couldn't have known that by watching him as a child. He could notice that Christopher had stopped bringing laughter into their lives. He could become frightened of him. The explanation involving previous lives arrives through other people's experiences much later, and it adds context to the behavior Victor remembered without making that childhood fear unreasonable.
