Victor keeps pictures of things that other residents can only hear about afterward. They're drawn in a child's style, even when the person keeping them has grown into an adult. That can make them easy to underestimate. Someone looking for a written history of the town won't find one arranged in his room. What they find instead is a collection of images that Victor has kept because remembering without them is difficult. The paper gives him something he can return to, including when he isn't ready to give another person a full explanation.

There's a practical difference between asking Victor to describe his childhood and looking at a drawing with him. A spoken account has to keep moving. Someone asks what happened, and an answer is expected. A picture can sit between two people while neither of them knows quite what to say. Victor can identify part of it, stop, or come back to it later. The image remains available through those pauses. In a town where people die and newcomers arrive without knowing their predecessors, that kind of persistence has a use.

The drawings connected with the aftermath of the childhood massacre are an especially difficult example. They preserve Victor's attention to an event that the present residents didn't experience. They also come from the position of a child who survived it. Looking at such a picture can tell someone that Victor has retained an image of what he encountered. It can't supply every action that led to that aftermath. The people absent from his view don't acquire a documented history because he put the part he saw onto paper.

Even the age of a drawing needs care. Victor having an image now doesn't, by itself, tell another resident when he made it. The event represented and the moment of drawing it are separate pieces of information. A picture made after remembering something would have a different history from one made close to the event. Without that context, the viewer has an image and an association to investigate. There's no need to discard it, though there's also no reason to assign it an exact place in the town's history before Victor can help.

Then there's the question of emphasis. A person drawing a frightening event has to put some things on the page and leave other things out. Victor's pictures aren't complete views of every street, room, and person involved. A figure can matter to him without its importance being apparent to someone else. An empty area of paper doesn't tell us that nothing happened there. This is part of what makes his collection difficult for the other residents to use. They can see what he's drawn, while the reason he kept that particular image may still be unavailable.

His own account of why he draws gives the collection its most immediate purpose. He uses pictures to help him remember. That makes keeping them an ongoing activity, with consequences beyond whether another resident finds a useful clue. Victor is trying to retain access to people and events that can otherwise become difficult for him to describe. An investigator might want to remove one image from the collection and examine it in isolation. For Victor, that same image belongs among other things he's chosen to preserve, and separating it may lose associations he hasn't yet explained.

It's understandable to look for predictions in these images. FROM repeatedly brings its residents into situations that resemble earlier events, and Victor has access to a past that most of them lack. If a drawing seems to match something happening now, it deserves attention. Still, resemblance alone leaves a basic distinction unresolved: Victor may have recorded something that happened before. In that case, the picture could help identify a recurrence. Calling it a prediction would add a claim about when and how he knew the later event, which the image alone can't answer.

A partial drawing can nevertheless direct a useful search. If another resident recognizes its subject, that recognition gives them something specific to discuss with Victor. They can compare the place they know with the place he remembers. A mismatch may matter too. The current town hasn't remained unchanged throughout his life, and his long residence gives him opportunities to notice differences newcomers couldn't recognize. The drawing's value can therefore emerge through comparison, even if its first appearance leaves people uncertain. Nobody has to extract the whole answer from the page immediately.

Victor's drawings preserve parts of his past that he can't always explain when someone asks. Their meaning can become clearer later, when he or another resident recognizes something on the page.
