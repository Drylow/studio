Victor recognizes that some of the drawings he's kept were made by his sister. Until that recognition returns, Eloise has been missing from the account he can give of his childhood. The paper has survived alongside him, while his understanding of who made the pictures has fallen away. When he looks at them with Tabitha in season two, remembering their maker changes the family story. There was another child there with him, someone whose pictures he has carried without being able to explain their place in his life.

The discovery happens around the old car where Victor has stored drawings. Tabitha comes into that exchange with her own questions about the town, and the pictures seem like something she might be able to examine. For Victor, though, identifying Eloise brings back a person before it supplies an explanation. His sister drew these images. That gives the collection another author, and it means that treating every page as Victor's own account would have led Tabitha in the wrong direction before she even began interpreting what was on it.

There's a small, practical distinction here that's easy to lose among the town's stranger mysteries. Keeping a picture doesn't mean you drew it. Victor has become the person responsible for preserving these pages, so someone finding them with him could naturally assume they all came from his hand. Once he remembers Eloise, that assumption has to change. Questions about an image now include what Eloise was trying to record and what she might have heard from someone else. Victor can help identify her work without automatically knowing everything she meant by it.

The drawings associated with Eloise include the tower and the account of children who need help. Those subjects matter to Tabitha, whose search will take her toward the lighthouse. But Eloise's pictures reach her through a family: Miranda spoke about what she intended to do, Eloise made drawings, and Victor kept them. Each person contributes something different. A child's image of a place can preserve what an adult described, even if the child never visited that place. That makes the page useful while leaving room to ask how its maker came to know its subject.

For this part of Victor's story, the more immediate change is that he can remember having a sister at all. Eloise isn't introduced as a stranger who suddenly arrives in town. She's recovered within a childhood Victor has already been trying to describe. His earlier account was missing a member of his own family. That's a substantial gap, because the presence of another child changes what that childhood involved. There were experiences he shared with someone close to his own age, even though the adult Victor hasn't been able to bring her into the conversation.

His reaction makes that recognition difficult to treat as a tidy investigative success. Tabitha has helped bring something into view, but Victor has to live with what has returned. Remembering Eloise means encountering her absence again. It also means realizing that the drawings were connected to someone he had stopped consciously remembering. The exchange gives Tabitha information she didn't have, while it gives Victor a family connection he can't resume in the present. Both things happen together, and the conversation can't make the second part easy just because the first part is useful.

Eloise's place in the story also changes how we understand Victor's long habit of drawing. He wasn't the only child in that family who put things on paper. The surviving collection contains traces of a shared activity, even where the siblings' purposes may have differed. That matters because Victor's pictures can otherwise seem like a private language belonging only to him. Eloise's work places some of that paper back inside a household, with a mother whose accounts reached her children and children who left their own records of what they understood.

Tabitha's presence helps the recognition become something that can be shared. If Victor had encountered the pictures alone, the recovered association would still have mattered to him, but no one else would have heard his explanation at that moment. With Tabitha there, the pages acquire context for another person. She can connect the name Eloise to a particular part of the collection. She also sees the difficulty Victor has in remembering. Their exchange gives her a reason to handle his account with patience, especially when a question that looks straightforward to her carries an entire missing relationship for him.

Henry's later reunion with Victor adds a different kind of connection to Eloise. Henry knew the family before Victor's long life in the town. Eloise belongs to his family history too, so her existence doesn't rest entirely on an adult survivor identifying old drawings. Henry can recognize the people who were part of that earlier home. What he can't supply is an account of everything that happened after their separation. His return brings another family member into Victor's present without filling every gap in the family's missing years.

Through the season-four finale, Eloise's fate remains unresolved. Remembering her name and recognizing her drawings don't establish that she survived, and her continuing absence doesn't establish that she died. The useful distinction is between recovering her place in Victor's life and finding out where her own story went. The first has happened. The second still lacks an answer. There's no need to assign her an adult identity to make the discovery matter; her presence already changes the childhood Victor is able to describe.

For Victor, the drawings now carry a connection he can name. A page that once sat among his saved pictures can be understood as something his sister made. Tabitha can hear that distinction, and Henry's return gives Victor someone who also knew Eloise before the town separated their family. None of them can bring her back through an explanation. They can, however, speak about her as a particular person in their family, and keep her drawings without mistaking them for someone else's.
