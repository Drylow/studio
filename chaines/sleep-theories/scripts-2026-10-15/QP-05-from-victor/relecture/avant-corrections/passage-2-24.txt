Henry's survival leaves Victor with someone to care for, even though that person has just endangered him. By the end of season four, their relationship includes this difficult reversal. Henry arrived as the father who could recognize Victor from a life before the town. Victor now has to look after the father whose visions have led him toward harming his son. Caring for Henry can begin while Victor still has reasons to be frightened of him. Nothing about that responsibility requires an immediate answer to what they feel about each other.

For Henry, recognizing Victor meant accepting that his child had grown older somewhere he couldn't reach. He could bring knowledge of their family into the reunion. He couldn't supply the years he'd missed. Victor had spent those years learning how to survive in the town, preserving drawings and possessions, and carrying experiences his father hadn't shared. Their reunion therefore brought together people with different parts of the same family history. Henry could recognize his son without being able to explain the adult standing before him.

Victor's drawings can remain available even when he can't yet explain them. Keeping them gives others something to discuss with him as his account develops.

Ethan has already made room for Victor in a way that reaches beyond extracting information. Their relationship includes stories and questions, and Ethan encounters Victor as someone he can spend time with. That matters in a town where adults often need an answer because something dangerous is happening. Victor can know a place, remember a person, or recognize a warning while struggling with the conversation others want to have. Ethan's companionship allows exchanges that aren't dependent on Victor producing a complete explanation whenever someone approaches him.

Victor's response matters just as much. He disarms Henry, and afterward the father and son both remain alive. Victor can take action in a situation where his father has accepted a dangerous demand. The dependence between them has changed: Henry now needs care from the son he came to find. Victor's familiarity with danger doesn't make that an easy task. It does mean Henry's return cannot leave Victor permanently in the position of the child his father remembers. There's an adult here who can protect him.

Henry's experience also changes what it would mean to dismiss Victor whenever his account becomes difficult. The father who brought recognition from outside the town has himself been reached by its manipulation. His earlier knowledge of home remains useful, but the visions have given him something else to believe and act upon. Victor has to deal with both parts of Henry's experience. He can remember being recognized by his father and still take the gun away. Those actions belong within their continuing relationship, however strained that relationship has become.

The same patience can extend to Victor without making him responsible for solving the place. When he remembers something useful, other people can help carry it forward. When he corrects an account, they can change what they do with it. Henry's recognition, Ethan's companionship, and Victor's preserved records each contribute something different. None requires Victor to become an all-knowing guide. He has already spent much of his life with events he couldn't explain, and he still has knowledge that the people around him would lose by refusing to listen.

Protecting Victor leaves that work possible. He needs to remain a person whom Ethan can approach and Henry can learn to know, with room to speak, hesitate, and correct himself. By the finale, he also has a father who needs his care. There isn't a settled family life waiting on the other side of the confrontation. There are two surviving men whose reunion now includes something painful they can't remove. Ethan has interrupted the attempt on Victor's life, and Victor has taken the gun out of Henry's hands. Henry is still there for his son to care for.
