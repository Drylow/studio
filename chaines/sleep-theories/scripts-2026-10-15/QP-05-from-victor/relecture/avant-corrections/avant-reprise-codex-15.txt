When Tabitha embraces Victor, she experiences a memory of Miranda's last encounter with him before leaving to try to save the children. The connection reaches her through contact with the son Miranda left behind. For Victor, Tabitha is already someone he's helped and someone who has returned with his father. Now her response to him carries something neither of those relationships can easily accommodate. She's remembering his mother from within a life she hadn't previously understood as her own.

The third-season finale gives that experience a clear explanation. Tabitha and Jade have lived before, as Miranda and Christopher, and their involvement with the children reaches further back than those lives. They remember that one of the children was their daughter. Their repeated attempts to save the children have brought them back to the town. This is a revealed part of their history, carried into the fourth season's conversations. It changes what Victor's recollections of Miranda and Christopher belong to, even though he couldn't have supplied that explanation himself.

Victor knew Miranda as his mother. He knew Christopher as an adult in the town whose behavior became frightening. Those were separate people with different places in his childhood. Tabitha and Jade's discovery puts them within a shared history that began before Victor was born. It also means that the adults asking him about the past have their own connections to it. Victor can describe what Christopher was like around him, while Jade has to reckon with having lived as Christopher. The questions have become personal on both sides.

That difference matters when Tabitha talks with Victor. He remembers being a child with a mother who went away and didn't return to him. Tabitha has her life with Jim, Julie and Ethan, alongside memories that connect her to Miranda. Hearing those memories doesn't give Victor back the years he lost with his mother. It could, however, give him another person with whom to speak about her. The possibility is intimate and awkward, because Tabitha's returning knowledge concerns someone Victor has missed for most of his life.

Henry has a different difficulty. He remembers Miranda as his wife and Victor as the child who disappeared with her. After entering the town, he finds a son who has grown older without him. Tabitha's connection to Miranda adds another strain to that reunion. The woman who helped bring him to Victor also carries memories of his wife. Henry has to face this while he's still learning how to be with the adult son standing in front of him.

Tabitha hasn't lived those missing years as Henry's partner. She has a marriage and children of her own, and Henry's memories of Miranda belong to a life they shared together. The revelation doesn't make Tabitha Henry's current wife or give either of them an established way to resume that marriage. In the fourth season's conversations, this family history has to be discussed among people whose present relationships remain real. Remembering Miranda involves Henry, but it doesn't tell him what Tabitha now owes him or how he should address her.

For Victor, there's a similar problem with calling Tabitha his mother. The connection explains why her memories can reach into Miranda's experience. Victor still has to meet Tabitha as the person she is now, just as she has to meet him as a grown man. Miranda knew him as a child. Tabitha has encountered the adult who lives at Colony House, keeps drawings and struggles to explain parts of his past. Any relationship they develop has to include that long stretch of life Miranda never shared with him.

Ethan makes this especially concrete. He's Tabitha's son, and he's also Victor's friend. Victor and Ethan began exchanging stories and exploring the town before Tabitha understood her connection to Miranda. Their friendship doesn't require a new family title to remain meaningful. Yet the revelation puts Ethan's mother in an unexpected position beside the man her son spends time with. Tabitha has responsibilities to Ethan in the present, while Victor's presence can bring back the experience of having been responsible for another child.

Jade's connection to Christopher creates a useful comparison. Victor's account of Christopher remains an account of someone he knew and feared. Jade's recognition doesn't erase Christopher's actions or turn Victor's recollections into descriptions of Jade's current behavior. Jade has to hear about that earlier life from a person who experienced its consequences. Tabitha faces a gentler version of that encounter in some respects, but she, too, meets someone whose life continued after the person she remembers being was gone.

There are now several people who can contribute different parts of the family story. Henry remembers Miranda before the town. Victor remembers being with her there. Tabitha carries returning memories of having lived as her. Those accounts can be brought together without requiring Victor to explain the whole pattern. Sharing that history may leave Victor with less to explain on his own. It also asks something difficult of Henry: listening to a woman he met as Tabitha speak about a life he shared with Miranda.

The family consequence stays close to the reunion between father and son. Henry has found Victor alive, and Victor has a father who can sit with him in the present. Tabitha's connection gives them another way to approach Miranda's absence, with all the discomfort that brings. Neither man has to surrender his own relationship with her for Tabitha's memories to matter. They can speak about the same woman from different parts of her life, while Henry and Victor begin spending time together again.
