Victor leads Tabitha to a tree with bottles hanging from its branches. The entrance she's looking for is an opening in the trunk, and entering it means accepting that she won't be able to see the journey ahead. Victor connects this place with the tower his mother had hoped to reach. For Tabitha, who's been trying to understand the children she sees, that gives her a destination she can act on. She still has to step into the tree without knowing what she'll find on the other side.

By this point, Tabitha has already depended on Victor's knowledge of routes she couldn't have found alone. After the floor beneath the Matthews house gives way, she encounters him underground, where the creatures spend the daylight hours. Victor helps her move through those tunnels and get out. His familiarity has a practical value there. He recognizes danger and knows a way through a place that's unfamiliar to her. Their later journey to the bottle tree grows out of that experience of following him and surviving.

The trees themselves make ordinary directions difficult. Earlier, Victor demonstrates a faraway tree to Ethan by dropping a stone into its opening. The stone emerges elsewhere. It's a small, concrete demonstration of something that would otherwise sound like another impossible story about the town. An opening in a trunk can take an object somewhere beyond the space inside the wood. But knowing that such travel happens doesn't give Ethan, or Victor, a map of every possible destination.

That matters because Victor treats the bottle tree as a particular route. He tells Tabitha that it leads to the tower. He's giving her more useful information than a general assurance that trees sometimes transport people. There's a place to find, an entrance to use and a destination he associates with it. Tabitha can follow those directions. What she can't get from them is a full account of what the tower does, or an explanation of how reaching it will help the children.

Before she goes, Victor gives Tabitha his lunch box. It's something from his own childhood that she can carry with her on the journey. The gesture also shows how much this attempt asks of him. He's helping another mother approach a destination tied to the loss of his own. Tabitha leaves with an object he has kept, while Victor remains behind. His help carries a personal cost even though he isn't the person stepping into the opening.

Tabitha does reach the lighthouse. In the season two finale, she climbs its stairs and encounters the Boy in White near the top. He pushes her through the window, and she subsequently wakes in a hospital outside the town. Her passage therefore contains several separate events: Victor brings her to the tree, the tree takes her to the tower, and the Boy in White intervenes there. Treating the tree alone as an exit would leave out the encounter that comes between her arrival and her awakening.

There's also a difference between Tabitha getting outside and Tabitha achieving what she set out to do. Her children remain in the town. She hasn't returned with the children she hoped to help, and she can't tell her family that she's alive. The hospital gives her access to a world beyond the town, but it also separates her from everyone she was trying to protect. The journey works in one extraordinary sense while leaving her with an immediate, ordinary problem: she needs to reach her family.

Victor's lunch box then helps connect that journey with a real person outside. Tabitha finds his childhood address in it and goes to Henry's home in Camden, Maine. Directions associated with Victor have now helped her reach both the lighthouse and his father, though by very different means. The address can be read and followed through ordinary streets. The tree requires a leap into a passage whose workings remain hidden. Having both in the same journey makes it easier to see how specific each piece of guidance is.

When Tabitha is brought back to the town, her earlier passage becomes something the residents want to understand and use. That's an understandable response. She reached a hospital, found Henry and returned with knowledge from beyond their confinement. For people still trapped, the tree offers a location they can approach themselves. They don't have to wait for a distant signal or a message they can't reproduce. The opening is there, and someone they know has passed through it.

Dale's attempt gives that hope a terrible limit. In season three, he enters the bottle tree expecting a way out and emerges trapped in the concrete of the pool. The same entrance doesn't deliver him to Tabitha's destination. His death makes the problem painfully specific for the people standing nearby. Following the visible part of her journey hasn't reproduced her result. They can't safely treat the tree as a passage to the lighthouse for anyone who steps inside, whatever confidence Tabitha's earlier success might have encouraged.

Season four keeps that limitation in place: the earlier route can't simply be repeated as before. Tabitha's journey remains part of what happened, and Victor's directions remain part of how she made it. The later difficulty doesn't undo her arrival at the tower. It changes what anyone can responsibly ask the route to do again. Returning to a familiar landmark isn't enough to recover all the circumstances of an earlier passage, especially when the travelers never understood why that passage ended where it did.

For Victor, this leaves a difficult kind of usefulness. He can bring Tabitha to a place she needs to find without being able to guarantee what it will do for her. She reaches the lighthouse with his help, then reaches Henry with something Victor entrusted to her. Those are substantial consequences of listening to him. Alongside them sits Dale's failed passage and the later inability to repeat the route. Victor can point out the opening and share what he remembers. The person entering it still disappears beyond his sight.
