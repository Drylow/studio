When Victor comes out of hiding as a child, the people who had filled the town are dead. The series returns to that aftermath through his recollections, with the young Victor trying to take in a place that has changed while he was concealed. He survives the massacre without seeing everything that happened during it. That distinction matters whenever someone asks him to explain the night. He can remember coming out and finding the dead. The complete sequence of events wasn't available to the child who found them.

The hiding place gives his survival a concrete setting. Victor had been underground while the danger passed through the town above him. Being sheltered meant he was absent from events that later became essential questions. Who was where, what each person did, and how the deaths unfolded are things his survival alone can't answer. There's a difficult limitation built into his position: the place that kept him alive also kept much of what happened outside his sight.

The aftermath itself is something he does encounter. He walks into the town and sees the result, with no familiar adult arriving to explain it or take charge of what happens next. We don't need an extended description of the bodies to understand his situation. A child has emerged expecting a world with other people in it, and the people he depended on are gone. Before the town becomes a mystery for him to investigate, he's faced with the immediate problem of continuing to live there.

The Boy in White appears within Victor's recollections of that period. That presence complicates any casual description of him as completely alone, although it doesn't supply the ordinary care a surviving child would need. Seeing the boy doesn't tell Victor how to recover his family or explain the massacre. Nor does it give the audience a continuous account of his days afterward. It's a specific remembered encounter within a much larger stretch of life that the series has only shown in parts.

There's a tendency to stretch the image of young Victor in the emptied town across every year that follows. It's understandable, because the image is so closely associated with him. But Victor's adulthood takes place among other residents, and his long history includes people who arrived after his childhood. The series doesn't establish that he spent every intervening decade entirely without human company. The period immediately following the massacre and the whole duration of his residence need to remain separate when we describe what he endured.

As an adult, Victor has practical familiarity that newer residents lack. His response to changes in the town can come from having something older to compare them with. When he measures the distance between trees and worries that they're moving, he's attending to the physical place around him. Someone who arrived recently couldn't make the same comparison from personal experience. His measurements still require interpretation, but the activity tells us something useful about him: he notices differences and tries to give them a concrete form.

His knowledge can also help someone through immediate danger. When Tabitha falls into the tunnels beneath the Matthews house, Victor is there and helps guide her through them. He knows enough to move through a place she has only just discovered. That's a kind of knowledge earned within the town, with consequences for another person's safety. Even there, though, his familiarity doesn't amount to a complete explanation of the creatures or the underground passages. He can help Tabitha proceed without being able to answer every question the tunnels raise.

His age can make that unevenness frustrating for the other residents. They've arrived with urgent questions, and he's been there since childhood. It's natural for them to expect answers from him. Yet the town wasn't giving him explanations while he grew up. A child surviving its dangers wouldn't necessarily have access to whatever brought the place into existence. Years of exposure could give him repeated encounters with its effects while leaving its beginnings as inaccessible to him as they are to someone newly trapped there.

The young Victor who emerges from hiding has survived an event far larger than anything he could have understood from his shelter. The adult Victor carries knowledge from that childhood, along with knowledge acquired during the life that followed. Neither part gives him automatic access to the town's origin. What he can offer begins with something narrower and more tangible: he was there, he came out, and he remembers some of what he found. Other people can work with that account without asking him to have witnessed what happened while he was hidden.
