Victor remembers the Man in Yellow as someone who once seemed to arrive in town like the other residents. In the fourth season's fourth episode, that detail gives the figure a place in Victor's childhood account. The man associated with Jim's death has a history that reaches back into the period when Miranda was still alive. According to Victor, he was there among people whose arrival could initially be understood in the ordinary way this town allows: another person had come in.

Arriving is a familiar experience for the people trapped here. The Matthews family encounters the fallen tree, takes the road around again, and reaches a town they can't drive away from. Other residents have their own lives before the road brought them into the same predicament. Someone who appears to share that experience has an immediate explanation for being present. Victor's recollection places the Man in Yellow within that recognizable situation, although an apparent arrival doesn't tell us where he actually came from or what he already knew.

That makes the remembered detail unsettling in a particular way. Victor knew the town as a child who depended on adults to explain what was happening around him. Miranda was there, Christopher was there, and other people occupied the place before the massacre left Victor in its aftermath. An adult entering that setting could become part of a child's account of the residents without the child understanding his role. Victor's description now asks the people listening to reconsider someone who had seemed to belong to that earlier group.

The Man in Yellow's appearance at the end of the third season gives this recollection a very different context. Jim encounters him after Tabitha and Jade have begun recovering the meaning of their connection to the town. The encounter ends with the Man in Yellow killing Jim. He's capable of direct violence, and his presence connects that violence with the discoveries being made around the children. When Victor later remembers him, the audience already has a concrete reason to take his involvement in the past seriously.

Victor's account also places the Man in Yellow with the bodies after the earlier deaths, including Miranda's. This is difficult information for Victor to recover because it concerns what happened to his mother after he lost her. Miranda has already occupied several places in his remembered story: the mother who brought him here, the adult who tried to act, and the person who didn't return to him. The fourth season adds a further encounter with her absence, through his recollection of another person's actions involving her body.

There's an important sequence to keep intact here. The series has shown Smiley intercepting Miranda as she tries to reach the tree. That scene concerns the attack that kills her. Victor's later recollection concerns the Man in Yellow's involvement with her body afterward. Those events can belong to the same history without giving both figures the same action. Calling the new memory a replacement account of Miranda's killer would lose the distinction between her death and what someone subsequently did to her remains.

For Victor, both events concern his mother, so separating them in conversation may be painful even when their order matters. Miranda's attempt to save the children ended before she could return to her own children. What happened afterward belongs to a stretch of that night that Victor couldn't stop or repair. Hearing his account now means allowing him to describe that later part without folding it into the moment of the attack. The details change what the others know about the Man in Yellow's presence during that period.

Smiley and the Man in Yellow also occupy different positions in the story the audience has been shown. Smiley is among the creatures that emerge at night and threaten the residents. The Man in Yellow appears to Jim in daylight and speaks with knowledge of the discoveries that have just been made. Victor's recollection brings that second figure into an older setting. It suggests his involvement extends beyond the encounter with Jim, while leaving his precise place among the town's other threats unexplained.

The apparent arrival matters alongside that later conduct. Taken together, the two parts of Victor's account describe someone who could first be understood as another resident and who was subsequently involved with the dead. That would make his earlier presence more troubling to remember. Victor isn't merely adding a frightening stranger to a distant event. He's revisiting the place that a person already had in his childhood understanding of the town. A figure who seemed to fit one familiar category now has to be considered alongside actions Victor associates with the aftermath.

Christopher provides a useful comparison within Victor's own past. Victor remembers someone whose behavior changed as his fixation on the symbol grew. His account of Christopher includes the difference between how an adult once behaved and how frightening that adult later became to him. With the Man in Yellow, the remembered contrast concerns an apparent arrival and later involvement with the bodies. Neither account requires the child Victor to have recognized the danger at the beginning. He encountered adults before he could understand everything they were doing.

The new recollection also gives Henry something especially difficult to hear. He knew Miranda before the town and has now found the son who survived her. Victor carries experiences of Miranda's last period of life that Henry couldn't share, including this account of what happened after her death. Henry's recognition of his son can't supply those missing events for him. If he learns about them through Victor, he's receiving part of his wife's history from the child who had to remain there after she was gone.

By this point, remembering Miranda involves people with very different connections to her, but this particular account remains Victor's. It concerns a man he recalls arriving and later seeing involved with the bodies. The account adds to the Man in Yellow's history without making Victor responsible for explaining his entire nature. It also gives Miranda's story a sequence that deserves care: she tried to reach the tree, Smiley killed her, and Victor remembers the Man in Yellow's involvement afterward. Keeping those actions separate lets Victor's new memory add something specific to what was already known about his mother.
