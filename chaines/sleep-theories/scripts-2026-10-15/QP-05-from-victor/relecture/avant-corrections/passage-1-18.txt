Victor recognizes a mother, a son and a daughter in the Matthews family, and the resemblance frightens him. Miranda had a son and a daughter too. He knows what it meant to be the boy who survived after his mother left to try to save the children. When he looks at Ethan after the car visit, that earlier family arrangement gives his fear a particular shape. He worries that Ethan could be left behind, carrying an experience that the rest of his family would no longer be there to share.

Tabitha and Miranda's connection makes this more difficult than an ordinary resemblance between families. By this point, the revelation about their earlier lives has given Victor a reason to connect the women beyond their being mothers in the same dangerous town. Tabitha's involvement in trying to save the children belongs to a history that reaches into Miranda's life. For Victor, that means a danger associated with his mother has become part of the life of Ethan's mother as well.

Still, the family in front of him has its own members and its own experiences. Ethan is Tabitha's son, and Julie is his sister. Victor's recognition doesn't turn them into a complete reenactment of his childhood. The connection between Tabitha and Miranda is a released part of the story; the possibility that Ethan will suffer Victor's isolation remains Victor's fear. Nothing about a mother having a boy and a girl supplies a rule that requires the boy to survive alone.

What gives that fear weight is how well Victor knows the consequence he's imagining. He survived the childhood massacre and remained in the town as he grew older. His drawings and saved possessions carry parts of that long history into his present life. Ethan has come to know someone who can speak about being a child here, with all the interruptions and corrections that involves. Victor can look at his young friend and imagine him needing those same ways of keeping people close after they're gone.

That doesn't require Victor to believe he can predict a particular death. A person can recognize the beginning of a situation he fears without knowing how it will end. In Victor's case, the resemblance includes the family, the town and a mother's involvement with the children who need saving. Those are substantial connections. They explain why his worry grows stronger around Tabitha and Ethan, even though they don't tell him what will happen to either of them.

Ethan's friendship with Victor gives the worry another consequence. Ethan already turns to him for knowledge that the adults around him don't always have. Victor knows places and remembers events from before the Matthews family arrived. He also answers Ethan as someone who takes his interest seriously. Their exchanges can give Ethan access to information, but now Victor has to consider what that information might ask a child to carry. Explaining survival to Ethan means allowing for a future Victor would rather prevent.

In the eighth episode of season four, Ethan asks to be prepared. The request makes Victor's concern practical. Ethan is asking for help facing the possibility that has frightened his friend. He can't settle the town's history or make his family safe by understanding it, but he can ask someone who survived here what he may need to know. That puts Victor in an uncomfortable position: refusing to engage won't remove the danger, while engaging means discussing a possibility involving a child he cares about.

There's a difference between hearing that request and accepting its feared outcome as inevitable. Ethan's willingness to prepare doesn't confirm that his mother and sister will disappear, or that he'll grow into another Victor. It shows that the possibility has entered his thinking. Within their friendship, the burden has begun to move in both directions. Victor wants to protect Ethan from a familiar kind of loss, and Ethan asks Victor to help him face what being left behind could mean.

The comparison with Victor's own childhood is painful because his knowledge came afterward. Miranda told her children to hide while she went to act. Victor was a child inside an adult's attempt to do something about the town. He didn't possess a complete explanation of her purpose or the events that followed. Ethan, with Victor available to answer him, has an opportunity to ask about danger before the feared separation happens. That opportunity matters even when Victor can't give him certainty.

Preparation also has limits that are particular to this place. Victor's years in the town have given him experience, yet he's had to recover and revise parts of his account. His correction about Christopher's conversation is one clear example. Ethan can learn from him without treating every recollection as an instruction that will always work. It would be unfair to place that expectation on Victor, and dangerous for Ethan to mistake familiarity with the town for a complete account of its rules.

The care between them is easier to understand if we keep Ethan himself in view. He's the boy who asks Victor questions and shares stories with him, someone with whom Victor has formed a friendship. The fear of repetition may intensify Victor's wish to protect him, but that wish already has a person to attach to. Ethan isn't important only because his family resembles Victor's. If the resemblance disappeared, Victor would still have a young friend living among the same dangers.

For Victor, then, remembering his childhood creates a responsibility he can't discharge by announcing a cycle. Ethan needs whatever useful help Victor can offer, without being told that his life has already been decided. The season four request leaves them with that difficult task between them. Victor has survived something Ethan fears he may have to face. Ethan can ask him about it while his mother and sister are still people in his present life, and while Victor is there to hear the question.
