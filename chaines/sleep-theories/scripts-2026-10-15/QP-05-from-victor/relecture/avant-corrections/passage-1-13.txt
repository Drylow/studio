Henry has known Victor since before the town, and that gives their reunion something Victor's other relationships can't provide. He remembers a son who had a home and a family somewhere else. When he meets Victor again in season three, that child has become a grown man without him. Henry can recognize his son, but he can't fill in the years between them. The reunion brings a person from Victor's earlier life into the place where Victor has spent so much of his later one.

For the residents who've met Victor in town, his childhood usually arrives through something he says, something he draws, or somewhere he takes them. Henry comes with his own recollections. Miranda and Eloise belong to his family too. Their names refer to people he knew before they disappeared from his life. That gives Victor's account a connection beyond the town's surviving objects. Someone else remembers the family together, even though that person wasn't there when it came apart.

There's a practical difference between Henry recognizing Victor and Henry confirming a story Victor tells about the town. Henry can know that Miranda was Victor's mother and Eloise his sister. He can remember their life at home. He can't recall what Miranda did after arriving in a place he hadn't entered, or what Victor saw during his childhood there. When father and son finally stand together, their knowledge overlaps around the family. Across the years of separation, each has an experience the other hasn't shared.

Victor doesn't rush toward the reunion as though his father has arrived to settle every question. He initially avoids meeting Henry. The hesitation matters because Henry's presence brings back a relationship Victor has lived without for years. Residents can ask Victor about a tree or a tunnel and receive an answer about that particular place. Facing his father asks something much more personal of him. He has to be seen as the child Henry lost, while standing there with an adult life Henry knows almost nothing about.

When they do meet, Victor's fear that Henry will be angry with him gives the encounter a very specific hurt. He approaches his father with concern about how he's going to be received. Henry embraces him. That response gives Victor reassurance in the present, during an encounter he's been reluctant to face. It doesn't require Victor to explain the whole history of the town first. His father can welcome him before hearing a complete account of what happened to the rest of their family.

Henry, meanwhile, has to meet the person Victor has grown into. He hasn't watched the gradual changes that would ordinarily connect a remembered child with a grown son. There's no shared account of those intervening years for them to draw on together. Victor has habits, friendships and ways of getting through the town that developed in Henry's absence. Henry arrives as his father, but he's also a newcomer who needs to learn about the place his son already knows.

That makes their position different from Victor's relationship with Ethan. Ethan meets Victor as he is and begins asking questions from there. Their friendship grows inside the town, around experiences they can sometimes share. Henry brings an earlier Victor with him in his recollections. He knows things Ethan couldn't know, yet Ethan has spent time with Victor in circumstances Henry missed. Neither relationship makes the other unnecessary. They give Victor different ways to speak, with a child who befriends him and a father who remembers raising him.

The embrace can be moving without carrying every missing answer inside it. Henry's relief tells us about finding his son alive. Victor's distress tells us about facing his father after their separation. Those responses belong to the reunion itself. If Victor later describes an event involving Miranda, Henry still needs to hear where Victor was and what he actually saw. Loving recognition gives them a reason to listen closely to each other. The details of an event still have to come from whoever was there.

Henry's arrival also changes how Victor's drawings and recollections sit within the group. Until then, the people around Victor have little opportunity to compare his account of home with someone who knew it. Now there's another person they can ask about the family before the town. A remembered name or relationship can have a second source. That's useful even when Henry has nothing to add about a drawing's town setting. His contribution concerns the life from which Miranda, Victor and Eloise disappeared.

For Henry, listening means allowing Victor to be the person with experience in situations where a father might expect to guide his son. Victor has survived here. He knows places Henry hasn't visited and people Henry hasn't met. Henry can't arrive with a parent's familiarity and expect it to include that knowledge. He has to ask, observe and learn. Victor, in turn, has to make room for someone whose connection to him reaches further back than his current relationships, without suddenly becoming able to explain everything Henry wants to understand.

Their reunion leaves them with ordinary difficulties inside an extraordinary situation. They can recognize each other and still struggle to talk. Henry can want to help while misunderstanding what Victor needs. Victor can want his father nearby while finding the encounter painful. The hesitation before they meet already gives a reason to take that difficulty seriously. Reunion hasn't returned them to the point where their family was separated. They're meeting after lives spent apart, and they'll have to learn how to be together in the present.

For now, Henry's most immediate response doesn't depend on solving those difficulties. Victor comes to him afraid of anger, and Henry receives him with an embrace. The father who remembers the boy is holding the grown son who survived beyond his reach. There's room afterward for questions about Miranda, Eloise and the years Henry missed. In that first meeting, Victor is allowed to reach his father without having all those answers ready.
