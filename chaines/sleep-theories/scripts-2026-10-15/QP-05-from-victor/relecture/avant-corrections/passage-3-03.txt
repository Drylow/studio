Ethan has a way of approaching Victor that begins with something they can both talk about. In the Matthews family's early days in town, Ethan brings the stories of the Cromenockle into his attempts to understand what's happening. Victor has his pictures and his knowledge of places beyond the houses. Their conversations can move between those things without Ethan insisting that Victor first explain the entire town. For a boy trying to make sense of an unfamiliar place, someone who knows its paths is worth listening to, even when his explanations arrive in pieces.

That matters because the adults around Ethan have immediate reasons to be wary. He's been injured in the family's arrival, and Jim doesn't know Victor. When Victor takes Ethan into the woods, Jim sees an unfamiliar adult leading his son toward danger. His anger has a clear basis in the situation. Victor's long experience doesn't give him permission to decide where someone else's child should go. Yet the confrontation also shows how quickly a conversation about what Victor knows can become a confrontation about whether he should be near Ethan at all.

Ethan's interest survives that adult suspicion. He doesn't treat Victor's unusual manner as a complete explanation of who Victor is. He talks with him, asks about the things he notices, and returns to the possibility that their surroundings have a story that can be understood. That approach gives Victor a particular kind of opening. He can begin with a place, an observation, or something Ethan already recognizes. He doesn't have to begin by defending his whole account of his life to someone who has already decided that he makes no sense.

Their shared awareness of the Boy in White gives those exchanges a concrete subject. Ethan sees a figure that most residents aren't discussing as part of ordinary town life, and Victor recognizes whom he's talking about. For Ethan, that recognition means he isn't alone in having seen the boy. For Victor, Ethan can describe something familiar without needing a long introduction. Neither person's recognition settles what the Boy in White wants. It does, however, let them compare an experience instead of spending the whole conversation arguing over whether there was anything to discuss.

The trip into the woods also brings Ethan into contact with Victor's practical knowledge. Victor knows about the faraway trees and demonstrates their strange behavior with a stone. Ethan encounters something that belongs comfortably within the language of an adventure story, except that here it happens in front of him. Victor can show him a feature of the place that a newcomer wouldn't know to investigate. The exchange has an immediate limit, too. Knowing that a tree can move something elsewhere doesn't mean Victor can supply a safe destination for every person who enters one.

Ethan's stories help him remain interested when an explanation is incomplete. The Cromenockle gives him ways to talk about a journey, a difficulty, and the possibility of finding a way through. That doesn't turn the story into a set of verified instructions for escaping the town. Its usefulness here is in the conversation it makes possible. Ethan has words for continuing when the people around him are frightened or confused. Victor can meet him within that conversation without producing the orderly account an adult investigator might expect.

There's a temptation to make this connection supernatural in itself. Both Ethan and Victor have seen the Boy in White, and Victor was once a child in the same place where Ethan is now growing up. Those are genuine points of contact. They give the two characters something unusually specific to share. But their ages don't establish that Ethan has inherited Victor's role, or that children automatically understand the town better. Ethan also misunderstands things and needs protection. His willingness to listen helps a conversation happen; it doesn't make every conclusion reached within it correct.

Victor's side of the friendship matters just as much. He isn't a store of information that becomes available whenever Ethan asks the right question. He can withdraw, refuse to continue, or become distressed by what a subject brings back. Ethan has to encounter those limits as part of knowing him. Sometimes the useful development is that Victor remains in contact with someone who will speak to him again. An unfinished exchange can still leave room for a later one, especially when Victor hasn't been required to give an answer he can't yet manage.

That doesn't mean the adults should hand the work of questioning Victor over to a child. Ethan has his own fears, and the town already asks too much of him. Jim's concern about his safety remains relevant even when Victor knows something valuable. A better understanding of their friendship has to include both facts: Ethan gains access to knowledge through Victor, and Ethan can be placed at risk while pursuing it. The woods don't become safe because the conversation there is gentle or because the person guiding him means to help.

By season four, Victor's concern for Ethan has acquired a painful specificity. At the car, with Tabitha and Ethan, his thoughts turn toward the possibility that Ethan could be left as he was. The details of that discovery belong to their own part of the story. What matters for their relationship here is that Victor looks at Ethan and fears an outcome for him. His attention has moved beyond whether the boy believes him. He wants Ethan to avoid a childhood shaped by the absence of the people he needs.

Their friendship therefore changes the circumstances in which Victor can speak. Ethan gives him recognizable subjects, patient interest, and a reason to explain something small before attempting something larger. Victor gives Ethan knowledge of the place, alongside concern that can become difficult for either of them to express. Ethan's interest gives Victor a reason to keep talking, even when he can only explain part of what he knows.
