Gemma walks along the testing floor as someone who knows she's being kept there. She can speak to Dr. Mauer, ask about Mark, and want to leave. Then she passes through a testing room's doorway, and the experience available to her changes. Inside, she's placed in a situation she doesn't remember entering from that corridor. When she comes back out, the woman who wants to get home returns. Lumon can move her between these experiences while keeping the person who understands her confinement away from what happens during the tests.

In Chikhai Bardo, Gemma's life is divided more finely than Mark's familiar journey down the severed elevator. His working identity loses access to life outside; she encounters further separations as she's sent into rooms within the building.

One room places her in a dental appointment. Another puts her aboard a simulated airplane flight. These are recognizable situations, with discomfort and fear that don't require a remembered personal history to make sense. The person undergoing them can understand what's happening around her well enough to react. Severance hasn't removed language or the capacity to suffer. Lumon has arranged for the reaction to belong to an experience cut off from Gemma's knowledge of the corridor, her captivity, and the husband she wants to see again.

The room where she writes thank-you notes makes the same arrangement feel different. Here, the activity is ordinary enough to belong in someone's home. A person can understand the work of writing a polite message without knowing why this particular task has been assigned to her. Placed alongside the other rooms, it shows how varied the testing can be. Lumon can supply a local situation and let an identity encounter it as the immediate business of her life. Outside that room, Gemma doesn't carry a continuous account of having done it.

Dr. Mauer has access to the wider arrangement. He meets Gemma between these experiences and participates in the procedures that send her into them. She has to seek information from someone involved in keeping her there. Even an answer about Mark reaches her through that unequal relationship. Mauer can talk about the outside while she can't leave to check it. Her questions keep an ordinary marriage present on a floor designed to interrupt her access to it. She still has someone to ask after, however carefully Lumon divides her day.

The scenes of Mark and Gemma before her disappearance give those questions a concrete history. They meet through their work at the university, build a relationship, and experience the strain of trying to have a child. Their marriage includes affection, disappointment, and time spent together that has nothing to do with Lumon's assignments. When Gemma wants to return to Mark, she's trying to recover that shared life. Lumon is using a woman who already had work, plans, and attachments beyond the company.

That makes Ms. Casey's earlier presence on the severed floor especially painful to reconsider. Mark sits across from his wife during wellness sessions without recognizing her as Gemma. She follows the role Lumon has given her, while he receives the session as an employee. Cobel watches for signs that something might pass between them. Their encounters show how the company can bring two people with an intimate shared past into the same room and organize a conversation in which neither can openly draw on that past.

Ms. Casey's dismissal also exposes how little say that working identity has over her own continuation. She worries about her performance and speaks to Mark before being sent away. The elevator carries her toward the testing floor, a destination Mark doesn't understand. For him, a colleague has disappeared from work. Below, Gemma's confinement continues. Lumon has made those events difficult to connect even for the people living through them. A farewell upstairs can be one part of a procedure whose other stages remain inaccessible to both participants.

Cold Harbor connects this arrangement to the numbers Mark has been refining. Cobel tells him that his work creates separate consciousnesses within Gemma, and that the files correspond to the identities used in the testing rooms. That's her explanation of what his labor has been doing. Mark has spent his shifts responding to the feelings produced by groups of numbers, without knowing that the work concerns his wife. The computer presents a task he can complete. Cobel supplies the connection to the person living with its consequences downstairs.

This gives Mark's job a particular cruelty. His outie takes refuge in severed work because being there promises a break from grief over Gemma. His innie performs work that, according to Cobel, helps produce further divisions in her experience. Neither version of Mark begins with enough information to recognize that relationship. The marriage missing from his working awareness is directly involved in the work. His competence at the terminal can therefore serve a process he'd have urgent reasons to oppose if he knew whom it was being used on.

The Eagan name surrounds the technology with an account of family achievement. Gemma's days show what its use requires from someone outside that family.
