Dieter enters the Eagan family story through Kier's Fourth Appendix, a text Lumon gives the refiners during the outdoor retreat in Woe's Hollow. He's introduced as Kier's twin, which adds a close relative to a history the employees have already been taught to revere. Yet his arrival doesn't come through a family member's recollection or a record uncovered outside Lumon. The workers receive another portion of the founder's teachings under the company's supervision. Even this apparently intimate account of Kier's early life reaches them as assigned reading.

An appendix sounds secondary, something attached to a larger work to supply a little more information. For Mark, Helly, Dylan and Irving, however, this addition has immediate importance. Their employer has taken them away from their familiar desks and placed them in an activity organized around it. They aren't browsing an obscure passage because they've developed an interest in Eagan genealogy. Lumon has selected this story for them. That makes Dieter's introduction part of their employment, with the same expectation of attention that surrounds the handbook back on the severed floor.

The account gives Kier a companion who begins unusually close to him. A twin shares his generation and his family setting, without occupying the position of a later follower or descendant. Within the story, Dieter can accompany Kier before the founder has become the distant figure the refiners know. That changes the scale for a moment. The man whose teachings govern whole departments appears beside a brother, in a situation involving companionship, bodily impulses and judgment. It's a family episode made available to employees who have almost no access to family life themselves.

According to the appendix, the brothers' time in the wilderness leads into Dieter's sexual behavior and Kier's condemnation of it. The account then gives Dieter a strange, destructive fate. These details belong to the text Lumon presents, including its supernatural elements. They aren't independently witnessed events in the series' historical past. The useful thing to notice is how the passage connects a bodily act with disgrace and loss. Kier's judgment supplies the moral explanation, and the brother being judged has no comparable opportunity to explain himself.

There's something recognizable about Lumon's use of this kind of family material. In the Perpetuity Wing, employees encounter successive Eagan leaders through a company presentation that tells them how to regard those people. The Fourth Appendix reaches further back into an intimate relationship, but the route to the audience is similar. Lumon supplies the material and the setting in which workers receive it. A museum display and a reading assignment can look very different while giving the employee much the same position: someone expected to learn the approved account.

Dieter's story also brings the founder's teachings into a part of life that a work manual might otherwise leave alone. The refiners' jobs involve computers, numbers and files, yet their employer asks them to absorb a lesson about a brother's private conduct. That suggests how wide Lumon wants Kier's instruction to reach. Completing assigned work doesn't exhaust what the company asks of them. Their attitudes toward pleasure and disobedience are also available for instruction, even during an outing presented as a departure from the office routine.

The lesson also echoes Helly's earlier experience of refusing the life assigned to her and being required to continue it. At the retreat, however, Helena is occupying her place, so Helly is absent from the lesson ostensibly intended for the refiners. Placing a story of condemnation before this group may be read as another way of making departures from Kier's teaching feel dangerous. That reading follows the lesson's use at a compulsory company event; it doesn't require every employee to respond to Dieter in the same way.

The twins are also an understandable source of interest in a story about severance. Mark and his outie occupy one body while living through different relationships. Helly and Helena have sharply conflicting wishes. Dieter and Kier introduce another closely connected pair whose lives are given unequal standing. The resemblance is worth considering because the series keeps returning to people who are close in one respect and separated in another. Still, the appendix describes brothers. It supplies no demonstration that Dieter was a severed identity, a manufactured duplicate or the subject of an Eagan experiment.

The stronger connection concerns who gets heard. Mark's innie has to argue for the importance of experiences his outie hasn't lived. Helly's attempts to leave are answered by Helena, who claims the right to keep her at work. Dieter, as presented in the appendix, reaches the refiners inside someone else's moral account. These situations have different circumstances, but each makes the standing of a second person uncomfortable. A shared body or a close family connection doesn't ensure that both people will receive room to describe what happened to them.

Kier's brother becomes material for a lesson delivered under Kier's name. By the time the refiners have read about him, they know the judgment they're being asked to carry away. Dieter's own account of his life isn't among the pages Lumon has supplied.
