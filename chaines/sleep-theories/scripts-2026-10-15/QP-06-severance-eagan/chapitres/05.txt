Kier Eagan's house stands inside the Perpetuity Wing, beneath the same company roof as the severed offices. When Mark brings Helly there, the visit takes them beyond portraits and accounts of successive leaders. They encounter a reconstruction of a place presented as the founder's home. A house is a different sort of exhibit from a CEO's likeness. It invites employees to imagine Kier away from his public position, in rooms associated with the ordinary business of living.

That invitation comes from Lumon. The company has selected the domestic setting that employees can enter, just as it has selected the history surrounding it. Helly can't compare the reconstruction with an accessible world outside, or decide to spend an afternoon looking into its provenance. Her introduction happens during a company visit with her colleagues. Whatever intimacy the house seems to offer, the encounter still takes place within the conditions of her employment. Lumon supplies both the destination and the account of why it deserves attention.

For Mark, bringing the team into this space is part of introducing Helly to the institution. The house gives that introduction a scale the office doesn't have. At their desks, the refiners sit in a largely empty room with computers and prescribed tasks. Here, they're asked to pay attention to somewhere a particular man supposedly lived. The change of setting makes the founder easier to picture. It also gives the workers another place where their behavior can be measured against the respect Lumon expects them to show.

For severed employees, a domestic exhibit also arrives under unusual circumstances. Mark has a home outside Lumon, but his innie doesn't return there at the end of a shift. Helly has a life beyond the elevator that she can't remember. Both retain enough general understanding to recognize what a house is, without access to their own equivalent rooms and routines. That makes this an uneven encounter. The founder's household receives a carefully maintained presence inside their experience, while their own households remain beyond their reach.

The house acquires a further use when Dylan receives the waffle party in the first season's eighth episode. MDR has reached its quarterly target, and Mark selects Dylan for the reward. The familiar incentive language is still there: completed work, recognition, a special privilege for one employee. Yet the reward takes Dylan back into the founder's domestic setting. Lumon has made access to this space part of what a successful refiner can earn. A place introduced through reverence is now involved in encouraging performance.

Dylan eats his waffles at a table in the reconstructed house, before the reward moves into a different kind of participation.

After the meal, Dylan follows an instruction to go to the founder's bed. He puts on a mask depicting Kier, and masked performers enact the four tempers in a sexually suggestive ritual. The sequence is brief, but it changes how the house can be understood. Lumon uses its rooms for an organized performance in which a living employee takes the founder's place. The bed, the mask and the performers give Dylan a role to occupy, with the company arranging the terms of his participation.

The comparison with the earlier museum visit is useful because the setting stays the same while the employee's role changes. Helly enters as a new worker being shown something Lumon considers worthy of respect. Dylan returns as a worker singled out for achievement. He's invited further into the domestic space and into a performance built around Kier's teaching. This suggests a progression in how Lumon uses the house: familiarity with the founder can lead to rewarded participation in a company-approved version of his world.

The performance also brings together things that the office usually separates. MDR's work has its screens and numerical categories. The museum has its account of Kier. The waffle party gives the doctrine human performers and places it within a reward for completing that work. Dylan doesn't receive a technical explanation of refinement as his prize. He receives an experience arranged through the founder's imagery. Lumon can therefore celebrate his achievement without giving him any greater understanding of what the achievement has done.

Dylan leaves the reward before the performance finishes because he has agreed to help his colleagues activate the overtime contingency. His departure matters within this particular setting. Lumon has prepared a privileged encounter for him, but he has another commitment that takes him away from it. He goes to the security office and carries out the task the group needs him to perform. The reward hasn't absorbed all his attention or replaced his obligations to Mark, Helly and Irving.

Taken together, the visit and the waffle party show how much work Lumon asks this reconstructed home to do. It houses an approved account of Kier's private life, gives new employees somewhere to practice reverence, and provides a setting for rewarding their labor. Nothing shown there requires the ritual to reproduce an event from Kier's actual household. Its immediate use is visible in Dylan's visit. The company gives him a meal, directs him to a bed and offers him the founder's face to wear. Dylan eventually takes that face off and leaves to help his colleagues.
