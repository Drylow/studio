Jame Eagan comes down to the severed floor and gives Helly a kind of attention she hasn't asked for. By the end of the second season, he's speaking to her about his disappointment in Helena and a spirit he believes he recognizes in Helly. The distinction matters to him. He knows that the woman in front of him shares his daughter's body, yet he addresses something he regards as different within her. For Helly, this means being examined by a father she hasn't grown up knowing.

Their earlier meeting at the gala had depended on a mistake. In the first-season finale, Jame approached the woman he understood to be Helena. Helly was awake through the overtime contingency, surrounded by people who assumed she belonged there. He spoke with the familiarity of a parent while she tried to understand her place in the Eagan family. His later attention on the severed floor comes after that confusion has been exposed. He can now direct his remarks toward the innie whose existence the public celebration had concealed.

At the gala, Jame had described the distress he felt over what Helena's innie had done to their shared body. His account placed his daughter at the center of the injury. Helly's suffering received no comparable acknowledgment from him. That's useful context for his later interest, because his admiration doesn't arrive with an apology for the conditions she endured. He can find something compelling in Helly while continuing to speak from within the family that has required her to remain at Lumon.

In the conversations near the end of season two, Jame describes an absence he feels in Helena and a presence he believes he's found in Helly. He connects that presence to Kier's spirit. This is Jame's interpretation of the person before him, and nothing in the encounter confirms possession or the survival of Kier's consciousness. What we do hear is a father using the family's founder to explain why one identity interests him more than the daughter whose life he knows.

That leaves Helena in an uncomfortable position even though she isn't participating in the conversation. She has undergone severance as part of a public promotion for Lumon. Her family name gives that promotion its force: an Eagan is prepared to submit to the procedure associated with Eagan leadership. Jame's dissatisfaction suggests that performing this role hasn't secured the affection she might expect from her father. He speaks of her shortcomings while taking an interest in the person whose refusal made Helena's promotional work so difficult.

Helly has refused the work, challenged management and publicly condemned the treatment of severed employees. Those actions make Jame's fascination peculiar. The qualities that could make her seem forceful are also qualities Lumon has punished or contained. If he recognizes vitality in that resistance, then his admiration depends partly on conduct his company won't permit her to pursue freely. That's one possible reading of his interest. Jame doesn't give Helly a complete explanation of which qualities he associates with Kier, or why.

Helena's recorded refusal to let Helly leave gives this admiration a practical limit. Jame's praise doesn't change the arrangement in which Helena decides whether Helly must keep coming to work.

Jame also has an advantage in the conversation that ordinary family familiarity can't explain away. Helly knows him through Lumon, through the gala, and through what she's learned about her outie. She doesn't have Helena's history with him. When he speaks about his daughter's inner qualities, he draws on a relationship Helly can't consult for herself. She has to receive his assessment as someone being told about an absent person who happens to share her face. Even his disappointment brings information she didn't previously possess.

His language about Kier brings another absent person into the exchange. Employees already encounter Kier through company teachings and the Perpetuity Wing, where Lumon presents his life as an example to follow. Jame applies that same family reference to a living woman standing before him. He seems to be asking whether she contains something that belongs in his account of the Eagans. That may explain why he can sound personally absorbed without showing much interest in how Helly would describe herself.

For Helly, the immediate encounter remains a conversation with someone who can enter her workplace and discuss her existence as a family concern. Jame has found something he wants to recognize. She hasn't asked him to name it, and his interest brings no accompanying offer of release. Whatever tenderness or fascination sits inside his remarks, the practical arrangement is unchanged: Helena has a life outside, Helly is expected to work below, and their father can visit the severed floor to tell one of them what he finds missing in the other.
