At the Lumon gala, Helly finds her own face being used to recommend the life she's been trying to escape. Photographs of her time in Macrodata Refinement have become part of a public exhibition. The woman presented to the guests is Helena Eagan, daughter of Jame Eagan, and her severance procedure is the subject of a promotional campaign. Helly arrives in this setting through the overtime contingency. She's awake outside the office, surrounded by people who respond to her as Helena.

The family name changes the meaning of those photographs. Lumon hasn't asked an unrelated employee to describe a pleasant working day. An Eagan has undergone the procedure herself. For the guests, Helena's participation offers a concrete answer to a concern about severance: whether the people promoting it would accept it for themselves. Her body appears to supply that answer. She has the implant, and an identity belonging to that body has worked on the severed floor. The procedure has reached the family whose company wants others to embrace it.

Helena's recorded account helps fill that absence. She presents her participation as a personal commitment to the procedure, and describes an upbringing in which Lumon employees were spoken of as family. That language gives her decision a warm social setting. She's an Eagan joining the people associated with her family's company. Yet the person who actually spends the day beside Mark, Irving and Dylan can't hear this public account being prepared. Helena can speak about the experience while remaining separated from the consciousness that has to live through it.

The photographs make that separation especially useful to the campaign. Helly has stood beside her colleagues, taken part in office activities and had moments when her face could be photographed without visible distress. Those moments can be selected and arranged into a story of workplace belonging. The pictures don't need to be fabricated for the overall account to mislead. A photograph can preserve a real smile while leaving out what happened earlier that day, what the person wants, or how little freedom she has to leave the room.

Downstairs, Helly wakes on a conference table with no accessible account of who she is outside. Mark conducts her orientation through questions that she can't answer in the way an ordinary new employee could. She tries to get out, and the exit doesn't deliver the escape she expects. Later, she works at a terminal sorting numbers whose purpose she hasn't been told. That beginning matters beside the gala display. The smiling employee in the photographs has also experienced bewilderment and fear in the same workplace being offered as a successful personal experiment.

There are genuine connections in Helly's working life, too. Mark helps her navigate the floor. Irving and Dylan become people with whom she can share frustration, discoveries and plans. Lumon's exhibition can draw its persuasive force from that recognizable human closeness. Viewers of the photographs can see colleagues together and understand the image without needing a technical explanation of severance. But the relationships also give Helly people whose suffering matters to her. By the time she reaches the gala, she wants the outside world to hear about what happens to them.

Natalie treats the woman at the event as a participant who already knows the occasion. She guides her toward the speech Helena is expected to give. Helly has to learn her situation while other people continue responding to her as the Eagan daughter. This gives the gala a particular awkwardness. The company has built its presentation around a recognizable face, and recognition keeps working after the identity behind that face changes. Helly can move within Helena's public role for a short time because the guests have no visible reason to distinguish them.

Jame's private conversation with her makes that role even clearer. He recalls showing his daughter an early severance chip when she was a child, and says she wanted everyone to have one. That's his recollection of Helena's childhood, offered to someone who can't remember it. He connects the present event to a long-standing family enthusiasm for the technology. For Helly, however, the encounter provides another piece of unfamiliar personal history. A father is speaking to his daughter while the person hearing him has never knowingly met him before.

The gala therefore gives Helena several advantages that Helly has never had at work. Helena has a name the guests recognize, a place in the family and an account of her participation already prepared for public use. Helly has a brief opportunity created by Dylan holding the overtime contingency open elsewhere. She hasn't chosen the venue or arranged the audience. Still, the attention gathered around Helena's body gives her access to people who would otherwise have no reason to listen to a refiner from the severed floor.

When Helly reaches the microphone, she uses that access to contradict the promotion. She tells the audience that the people downstairs are suffering and rejects the happy version of their lives. Natalie intervenes, and Helly's time outside ends. The interruption leaves the guests with competing accounts from the same woman they came to hear. Their individual responses aren't settled for us. What matters within the event is that Lumon's chosen exhibit has spoken against the story arranged around her.

Helena's participation had made severance easier to present as something the Eagans would willingly share with everyone else. Helly's appearance exposes the person who has been carrying the working hours of that demonstration. Her face belongs in the photographs, but her account changes what those photographs mean. At the microphone, she gives the guests information the display has left out: the employee pictured beside Mark, Irving and Dylan wants them to know that life downstairs hurts.
