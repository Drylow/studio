Woe, frolic, dread and malice are the four tempers in the teaching Lumon attributes to Kier. In the Perpetuity Wing, the founder's account describes human character through their mixture and presents taming them as an achievement. It's quite a broad claim to place inside a company museum. A worker who arrives there to learn about Lumon's past also receives an explanation of what people are made of, emotionally speaking, and how the company's founder believed those feelings should be handled.

The names themselves cover rather different experiences. Woe suggests sorrow, while frolic allows for playfulness and pleasure. Dread brings in anticipation of something frightening, and malice involves hostility toward someone else. Within Kier's presented doctrine, they belong together as ingredients of a person. The series doesn't ask us to accept that arrangement as a validated account of human emotion. It's Lumon's inherited classification, with the authority of the founder attached to it. That distinction matters once the same classification appears in the work employees perform.

At the Macrodata Refinement desks, the four tempers have abbreviated labels in the interface. The refiners move groups of numbers into bins, and each bin tracks categories associated with those tempers. Their screens give this unusual work an orderly appearance. There are files to complete, progress to measure and a quota to meet. An employee can look at the display and see whether the task is advancing, even though the numbers don't explain whose information is being processed or why it needs this treatment.

Helly's introduction to refinement makes the difficulty easy to follow. Mark can explain the basic operation, but he can't give her an ordinary rule for identifying the correct numbers. She's supposed to recognize them through a feeling. When a group eventually frightens her, the task becomes something she can do. The numbers haven't turned into a written description of danger. She experiences a response to them and uses that response to select them. Her own feeling supplies information that the screen doesn't state in words.

That gives MDR a peculiar training problem. Mark can show Helly how to select and move a group, and the interface can register her progress. He can't hand her a list of numerical features that will replace the feeling. She has to encounter it herself. The work therefore requires an employee who can experience something, recognize it and act on it. Whatever Lumon has withheld from Helly's awareness, she retains the emotional capacities that let her learn this task, alongside the language and practical skills she uses throughout her day.

There's a limit to what that first frightening group tells us, though. Helly's response shows how refinement feels to a new worker. It doesn't explain the origin of every number or demonstrate a separate, easily described sensation for every temper. The category labels connect the task to Kier's doctrine, but the employees' knowledge remains incomplete. They can become proficient without being able to explain the whole process. Mark's ability to train Helly depends on his experience at the desk, rather than a full account of the technology beneath it.

Lumon also makes this uncertain activity into a familiar working routine. The team discusses progress, worries about finishing files and receives rewards tied to performance. A successful selection contributes to a measurable result. That structure gives the refiners something immediate to pursue while larger questions remain unanswered. For Mark, helping a colleague get the hang of the numbers is a recognizable piece of being a department chief. For Helly, completing part of a file can happen even while she objects to being there at all.

The second-season finale connects Mark's Cold Harbor file to Gemma's testing. The refiners' activity has consequences beyond their department, even though the workers at the desks haven't been allowed to see those consequences for themselves.

In Cold Harbor, Gemma is required to dismantle a crib without the emotional response that her life outside the room would lead us to expect. The narrow issue is whether pain associated with that outside life can reach the identity performing the task. We don't have to settle Lumon's ultimate purpose to see why this changes the meaning of refinement. A screen full of numbers, sorted through an employee's feelings, is linked to a procedure concerned with whether another person's feelings will cross a severed boundary.

The finale links refinement to Gemma's partitions without explaining exactly how the four categories contribute to creating them.

That leaves the refiners in an especially uncomfortable position. They use feelings they can recognize to complete work whose effects they're prevented from recognizing. Kier's four tempers give the task names, and Lumon's interface turns those names into bins and progress indicators. Neither gives Mark a sufficient account of what he's doing to Gemma. By the finale, a completed file can no longer be understood only as a good day's performance in MDR. Somewhere beyond the desks, a person with a life of her own has to enter the room that work has helped prepare.
