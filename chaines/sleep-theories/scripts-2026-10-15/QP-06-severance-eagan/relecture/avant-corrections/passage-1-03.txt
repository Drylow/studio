Irving can reach for the handbook when a conversation begins to move beyond the work in front of him. In Macrodata Refinement, Kier's teachings are part of the vocabulary employees use with one another. They give Irving a way to discuss proper conduct, the value of their work and his hopes for what Lumon means. The founder doesn't have to enter the office or issue a new instruction. His words are already available to an employee who wants to make sense of the place.

The handbook gives those words a practical home. A saying remembered on its own might invite a loose discussion. A passage in an employee handbook arrives with the employer's approval attached. Irving treats that written material with a seriousness his colleagues don't always share. When he draws on it, he brings a company document into an ordinary exchange between coworkers. That can change the conversation. Someone who disagrees with Irving may find themselves disagreeing with a teaching Lumon has told them to respect.

There's an understandable attraction here for an innie. Irving knows how to read, speak and perform his job, but he can't consult the personal history his outie carries. He can't remember the people who raised him or the experiences that shaped his outside beliefs. Lumon supplies an account of worthwhile behavior inside that gap. Its handbook gives him something he can study and return to. His attachment can be sincere even though the institution providing the teaching has also restricted his access to other sources of meaning.

Burt makes that attachment easier to see because he shares it. When Irving and Burt discuss Kier's teachings, their interest gives them a way to recognize something familiar in each other across departmental boundaries. Their exchanges about the handbook include a distinction between its earlier and later versions. That detail matters. The text employees receive has a publication history within Lumon. Even a teaching associated with the founder reaches the severed floor through editions that other people have selected, prepared and distributed.

For Irving and Burt, those passages also allow a conversation that reaches beyond quotas and office procedures. They can talk about what they admire and how they understand their place at Lumon. Their growing affection develops in a workplace that gives them very little privacy, and a shared reverence offers a permitted reason to spend time together. It would flatten their experience to treat every mention of Kier as empty repetition. The language means something to them, even as Lumon benefits from their willingness to take it seriously.

The same teachings become more restrictive when they travel through management. Mark has a department to run, Milchick supervises behavior on the floor, and Cobel can intervene from above them. These living employees decide when to offer reassurance, when to correct someone and when an infraction requires punishment. Kier's elevated position helps those decisions appear continuous with a purpose established before any of them arrived. A manager can present an instruction as part of Lumon's inherited way of doing things, without asking the employee to evaluate that manager's judgment separately.

Helly's arrival puts pressure on this arrangement because she doesn't accept the premises that make it comforting to Irving. She wants to leave. Mark's attempts to orient her take place within procedures supplied by the same employer keeping her there. Whatever Mark's intentions in a particular exchange, he has very little ability to alter her situation. The familiar language of a meaningful job cannot settle her objection. Her resistance makes the difference between learning how Lumon expects someone to behave and agreeing that Lumon is entitled to keep them working.

The break room shows how far the company carries that expectation into speech. Employees sent there must repeat a prescribed apology until the supervisor accepts their contrition. The procedure requires them to speak about fault and remorse in the company's terms. This isn't a handbook discussion between willing readers. A supervisor determines whether an employee has satisfied the demand, and the employee remains in the room until that judgment changes. Lumon has turned an account of proper behavior into a task a person must perform under supervision.

That arrangement leaves room for an uncomfortable uncertainty. Someone repeating the apology may feel remorse, may want the ordeal to end, or may experience both. The procedure gives the supervisor the authority to judge the performance without giving us a reliable view of everything the employee feels. With Kier's teachings around it, discipline can acquire a moral weight beyond correcting a workplace mistake. The employee is pressed to describe themselves as having done wrong, even when their own understanding of the incident may be quite different.

Lumon's artwork extends the teaching beyond the printed page. Irving and Burt's interest in the company's paintings shows how employees can encounter its ideas while moving through the building. An image associated with Kier invites discussion and admiration without a manager standing nearby to explain it. For Irving, looking at that art can be a meaningful part of the day. For Lumon, keeping such images in the workplace means the founder's example remains close at hand, including during moments that don't involve any direct instruction.

Season two gives a particularly clear example of how present managers can bring additional teaching into use. At Woe's Hollow, Milchick presents the workers with Kier's Fourth Appendix as part of a company-organized outing. The employees encounter material they haven't previously been working from, delivered through a supervisor who also directs their activities. Whatever they make of the account itself, its arrival depends on a current decision by Lumon. The workers aren't choosing when to expand their reading or which account deserves their attention. Milchick brings the text and sets the occasion for receiving it.

Kier's continuing authority on the severed floor therefore has a visible, everyday route. Lumon prints the handbook, places the art, trains its supervisors and supplies the procedures through which employees are corrected. Irving can find companionship in the teachings, while Helly can find that the institution surrounding those teachings won't accept her refusal. Neither response requires Kier to be secretly present. His words remain effective because people at Lumon keep putting them to use. In an ordinary workday, the person deciding what those words require is someone still employed to give instructions.
