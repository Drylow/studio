With Cobel's research in mind, the first question about the Eagans becomes quite practical: when the company gives someone credit, how closely does that account follow the work that was done?

A familiar name can make an invention seem easier to understand. With Jame Eagan attached to severance, the technology fits into a continuing family story. Kier founded Lumon, later Eagans led it, and the present leadership can present this achievement under the same name. Cobel's work interrupts that account. Once she enters it as a developer whose credit was taken, the invention has a history involving someone the family's version has pushed aside. We have to make room for her contribution before considering what Jame says the technology will accomplish.

There's a limit to the correction, and it's worth keeping clear from the beginning. Sweet Vitriol reveals Cobel's original severance work and Jame's appropriation of credit. It doesn't provide a complete account of every person who helped develop the procedure, every stage of testing, or every component of its equipment. Calling Cobel its true inventor is useful shorthand, provided we retain that distinction. Her recovered work challenges Jame's claim without requiring us to imagine her designing and manufacturing an entire system alone.

The episode also makes her long service to Lumon harder to reduce to a single explanation. Someone whose work has been taken might leave, demand recognition, or continue working within the institution. Cobel has continued serving it, with a devotion that extends well beyond ordinary employment. Her conduct suggests that attachment to Lumon and resentment toward its leadership can exist together. That tension matters because discovering the theft of credit doesn't turn every earlier expression of her belief into a performance. She's capable of having something invested in the institution that has wronged her.

It's possible to read her retained research chiefly as something she can use when her position becomes precarious. Documents that establish authorship can give her a stronger position than an unsupported accusation would. That reading fits the practical value of recovering them. Keeping them may also suggest that the work still matters to her personally, though the papers can't tell us every reason she preserved them. We can stay with the concrete fact: she has kept a record that allows her contribution to survive outside the account that credits Jame.

This is where the opening correction helps us approach the family's other secrets without treating every unanswered question as the same kind of mystery. Authorship is something Sweet Vitriol addresses with Cobel's research and the account of what Jame took. The Eagans' eventual plans for severance remain a different matter. Knowing that the invention story has been distorted doesn't supply a missing mechanism for immortality, or reveal who might inhabit a chip. It gives us a specific reason to question a specific piece of family history.

Jame's position still matters even after his inventor's credit is challenged. He's an Eagan leader with access to the institution using the technology. A person can direct the use of an invention without having originated it, and Lumon's ongoing work doesn't disappear when its account of authorship changes. That leaves a more uncomfortable situation for Cobel. The company can continue employing and developing work whose origins it has misrepresented. Restoring her name to the history wouldn't, by itself, determine what happens to the people subjected to that work.

For the employees on the severed floor, those origins aren't available as an ordinary subject of discussion. Their working world is organized around information Lumon permits them to receive. They encounter the Eagan name throughout that world, while Cobel appears to them in her managerial role. Sweet Vitriol lets us see a gap they haven't been equipped to investigate. The woman who supervises their department has a claim on the technology beneath their experience, and the celebrated family has claimed the achievement for itself.

That gap gives this account of the Eagans its starting point. We can examine their curated history, the technology credited to them, and their promises about what comes next without assuming those three things confirm one another. Cobel's research has already changed one of them. Whatever explanations remain open elsewhere, the correction here is concrete: Jame received credit for original severance work that was hers. In Salt's Neck, Cobel recovers the record of that work and takes it with her.
