Cobel keeps the research that challenges the name attached to severance. In Salt's Neck, the work survives among the belongings of a woman who has spent years serving Lumon. That makes her position uncomfortable in a very particular way. She's helped maintain the institution that celebrates Jame Eagan, even though her own retained material challenges his claim to the invention. Her devotion and her authorship have continued alongside each other. When she retrieves the research, something that the company story has pushed aside becomes available to other people.

The correction matters beyond deciding who deserves a place in a company display. Crediting Jame makes severance another achievement that appears to come from within the Eagan family. Employees encounter the family as the source of the institution's teachings, and the invention seems to extend that inheritance into their own working lives. Cobel's work interrupts that account. A person who served the family contributed the research for which an Eagan received recognition. The family's reputation has grown through work that belonged to someone outside it.

Helena's participation in the severance promotion gives the family another kind of contribution to present. At the gala, Jame's daughter can be offered as someone who has undergone the procedure herself. Her body gives the presentation an immediate persuasive force. An Eagan has accepted the intervention that Lumon wants others to accept. The photographs of her workplace experience help turn that participation into a story the company can share. Helly's working life supplies the events from which Helena's public account is assembled.

Helly has a very different understanding of those events. She wants to leave, and Helena refuses to allow it. The recorded exchange between them makes their disagreement unusually plain: the person outside the severed floor can decide that the person inside must continue returning to work. Helena's willingness to undergo the procedure has become permission to impose its consequences on someone who objects. Helly can express that objection and understand the answer. She still has to live through the next working day that Helena has authorized.

Cobel and Helly therefore have a specific grievance in common, although their circumstances are far apart. Lumon uses Cobel's work under an Eagan's name. Helena uses the life Helly experiences to support an Eagan's public endorsement. In each case, the family's account leaves the contributing person with little room to speak for herself. Cobel's authorship complicates the invention story. Helly's refusal complicates the account of willing participation. Both women possess something that can make the presentation fail if it reaches the people being asked to believe it.

There are important differences in what they can do about that. Cobel has lived outside the severed workplace, held a managerial position, and helped enforce the conditions under which Helly suffers. Her own treatment doesn't cancel those actions. Helly begins without access to Helena's life or the status that comes with her surname. She can't retrieve a personal archive from a family home. Her most direct account is the one she can give about being Helly, provided she can reach someone who isn't already committed to speaking over her.

The gala briefly gives her that opportunity. When the overtime contingency awakens Helly outside Lumon, she enters the very event that is using her experience as publicity. She discovers whose body she shares and what that identity is helping promote. Her response is to tell the audience about the suffering on the severed floor. Helena's appearance remains unchanged, so the guests are looking at the Eagan participant they came to hear. The testimony coming from her is now hostile to the account prepared around that participation.

That moment helps explain why a separate life matters even when the body belongs to an Eagan. Helly's experiences have given her reasons to oppose what Helena supports. Her friendships on the floor, her attempts to leave, and the refusal she receives all become part of how she understands Lumon. Sharing Helena's body doesn't give her Helena's commitments. The procedure the company promotes has allowed a person to develop who can stand before its guests and reject the purpose for which her experience has been collected.

The Eagans' complete endgame remains unconfirmed. What they've already taken credit for, and what they've already required other people to endure, gives this question enough substance of its own. Cobel preserves a record of work that the family story assigns elsewhere. Helly speaks about an experience Helena has agreed to keep imposing on her. Neither woman needs to solve every secret in Lumon to identify what has happened to her. In Salt's Neck, Cobel retrieves the research she kept. At the gala, Helly reaches the microphone and gives her own account.
