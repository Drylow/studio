Salt's Neck has a Lumon history that reaches beyond the offices where Harmony Cobel once supervised the severed workers. In Sweet Vitriol, she returns to a town shaped by the company's industrial presence, then enters the family home where her aunt Sissy still lives. The scale is different here. There are no visiting executives presenting the Eagan achievement to invited guests. There are buildings, family belongings, and a woman who continues to defend Lumon even when Harmony has come back with reasons to be angry.

The old ether factory gives that history a physical place. Lumon's name belongs to the town's working past, well outside the clean corridors associated with its severance program. The company has occupied ordinary lives here through work and family experience. Seeing this part of Salt's Neck changes the setting around Cobel. At headquarters, she can appear almost inseparable from her position. Back home, she's someone who came out of a place where Lumon was already present before she could have decided what kind of career she wanted.

The factory's disused condition matters because Sissy's loyalty survives it. Whatever the company once brought to Salt's Neck, its industrial remains don't provide the reassuring picture offered by Lumon's ceremonial spaces. Sissy still holds to the institution. Her attachment suggests that the company's influence can continue through people after its buildings have stopped doing their original work. Harmony doesn't have to enter an operating Lumon facility to encounter someone who expects her to treat the company with respect. She can encounter that expectation in her family's home.

Sissy also knows Harmony at a level that an executive doesn't. Their connection includes Charlotte, Harmony's mother, and a childhood that predates the professional manner Cobel uses with her staff. That familiarity makes their disagreement uncomfortable in a particular way. Harmony can challenge a superior's judgment and leave the room as an employee with a grievance. With Sissy, the grievance enters an older relationship. Her aunt can answer the adult woman through the history of the child she remembers, and Harmony can't set that history aside as easily as a job title.

Inside the house, Harmony's old room holds material from the life she had before becoming a manager. The visit brings her back among belongings that have remained while she's been away. She has a practical reason for searching there, but the search also places her in contact with the younger person whose ambitions developed in this setting. The room makes her career feel lengthy and personal. Lumon didn't first enter her life when she accepted responsibility for Macrodata Refinement. Her association with it reaches into the years when she was still preparing for an adult future.

The episode's references to her education help explain that reach. Harmony's ability and her opportunities were drawn into Lumon's world early. An institution that recognizes a promising child can give that child a path she might struggle to find elsewhere. It can also become very difficult to imagine achievement outside the terms that institution supplies. In Harmony's case, this helps explain why being pushed aside by Lumon has such force. The rejection reaches back toward a future she'd been encouraged to build around the company.

Sissy's defense asks Harmony to continue accepting that arrangement. Her aunt doesn't receive Harmony's anger as a straightforward reason to reconsider Lumon. She remains attached to the company and the account of life that comes with it. For Harmony, staying agreeable would mean leaving important grievances unanswered in a conversation with someone who knows her personally. That's a more intimate demand than following a workplace instruction. She'd have to carry her disappointment home and then make room for the expectation that she should remain grateful.

Charlotte's place in their conversation makes that demand harder. Harmony and Sissy share a family connection to her mother, yet that shared connection doesn't produce an easy agreement about the past. The home contains the history of Charlotte's illness and care, alongside Sissy's continued devotion to Lumon. There's no need to assign an unshown medical cause to see the difficulty for Harmony. Her mother's suffering belongs to the same remembered life in which her aunt continues to defend the company. Harmony has to hear those things held together by someone close to her.

Sissy's ether use further complicates the idea of home as a place where Harmony might recover a steadier view of things. The substance associated with the town's factory also appears within her aunt's daily life. The episode gives that industrial history a presence inside the house, through something Sissy does. It makes the connection between town and household unusually concrete. Harmony has returned to a family member whose habits, surroundings, and loyalties all keep bringing her back into contact with the world she has reason to question.

At Lumon headquarters, official displays select what employees should remember about the Eagans. Sissy's house preserves a different kind of company history. Here, the surviving material includes a girl's work, family pain, and an aunt's insistence that Lumon deserves allegiance. These things haven't been arranged into a public exhibition. Harmony has to search through them herself. The contrast helps explain what the company's polished story leaves outside its frame: the people who invested their ability in it, and the relatives who learned to defend it as part of defending their own lives.

What continued devotion would cost Harmony is therefore specific. She'd have to accept that her anger should yield to Sissy's familiar expectations. She'd have to keep measuring her future against an institution that has already dismissed her. And she'd have to leave her own account of what she contributed beneath the version Lumon prefers. Returning to Salt's Neck doesn't make those demands disappear. It allows her to hear them in a family voice, in rooms connected to the person she was before she learned to issue demands of her own.

Harmony doesn't leave the visit with her aunt's approval restored or her grievances settled. She takes away the material she came to recover. That action gives the return a practical consequence beyond an argument about loyalty. Something from her early life can leave the house with her, even if Sissy's allegiance remains where it was. Harmony carries her notebooks out of the place that kept them.
