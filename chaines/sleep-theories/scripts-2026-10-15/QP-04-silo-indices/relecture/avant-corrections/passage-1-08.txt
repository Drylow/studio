A mirror gives Juliette a place to look where she hadn't expected anyone to be looking back. Once she discovers the camera concealed behind it, the room around her changes. A conversation held indoors, away from the corridors and the people passing through them, may still have reached someone elsewhere. The mirror hasn't stopped doing its ordinary job. Someone standing before it can still see a reflection. Juliette now has to account for another use of the same object, one its users weren't meant to notice.

In the seventh episode of the first season, Gloria helps direct Juliette's attention toward surveillance hidden within domestic life. Their conversation puts Hanna's behavior in a different light, including the flowers she placed in front of a mirror. Flowers belong comfortably in a home. They don't require an explanation in the way a dismantled wall or a damaged fixture would. If they obstruct a camera's view, an ordinary arrangement can also give the people in the room some cover without announcing that they've discovered anything.

For Juliette, that connection reaches into her childhood. Hanna had been doing prohibited work while living with her family, and her daughter hadn't understood everything she was trying to protect. The flowers give Juliette a specific action to reconsider. Her mother could arrange something in the room that affected what a distant observer could see. Remembering a parent moving flowers is very different once there's a camera to account for. An apparently unimportant household habit acquires a practical purpose within Hanna's dangerous work.

Juliette follows the suspicion far enough to expose the equipment behind the mirror. That discovery gives her something firmer than the feeling that Judicial always seems to know too much. There's a physical camera where a resident would expect the back of a familiar fixture. She can locate the intrusion within the room. It also explains how someone might obtain information without standing outside the door, questioning a neighbor or leaving an obvious visitor for her to remember afterward. The watcher doesn't have to enter while she's there.

Elsewhere, the surveillance room supplies the other half of that arrangement. People sit before screens and examine images drawn from life inside the silo. Sims can use that operation in pursuing Juliette. The series lets us see the workers and the displays, so the surveillance has a human routine attached to it. Someone has to attend to an image, recognize a person or notice an action worth reporting. For the resident beside the mirror, all of that activity takes place out of sight.

The screens give those observers an advantage, though the image still has edges. A camera receives what falls within its view. An obstruction matters, and an action outside the frame may leave the observer with only its beginning or its result. Hanna's flowers make sense within precisely that limitation. She doesn't need to make the camera disappear to interfere with it. Something placed between the lens and the room can reduce what the people at the screens have available to examine.

Juliette's mechanical background gives this discovery a particular character. She's used to opening equipment and finding the working parts that explain its behavior. Here, looking behind a surface exposes a device whose usefulness depends on residents leaving that surface alone. The mirror is familiar enough to escape attention during an ordinary day. A resident has little reason to treat it like a machine that needs inspection. Juliette's willingness to damage it lets her reach the concealed part, even though doing so also makes her discovery visible to whoever receives the feed.

That creates a distinction between being watched and knowing that watching is possible. Before the discovery, Juliette can speak in a room without accounting for an unseen camera. Afterward, even a mirror she hasn't examined can make her pause. She may change where she stands or what she says because she can't be certain about the view. That uncertainty can affect her conduct during moments when nobody is actually attending to her image. Discovering one camera gives her a reason to be cautious elsewhere, without supplying a map of the whole system.

The observers face a different uncertainty. If they see Juliette cover a mirror or damage it, they can recognize interference with their equipment. They still have to interpret why she acted and how much she's learned. Her discovery might lead them to suspect that someone warned her. The picture alone can't identify everything Gloria told her, recover every earlier conversation or disclose what Juliette intends to do next. Sims brings his existing knowledge and suspicions to the images. What he concludes can therefore extend beyond what the lens recorded.

That matters for Hanna as well. An observer could see flowers without knowing whether their position was accidental. Even if someone suspected an obstruction, the arrangement wouldn't reveal the details of her work with the magnifying device or everything she understood about the silo. The same household object could be harmless to one person and useful cover to another. Hanna's situation makes that ambiguity practical. She was trying to investigate under restrictions while continuing to live among people who might notice a more obvious attempt to conceal her activities.

There's a useful comparison with the cafeteria display, although the two screens do different jobs. Residents gather before the large public image knowing they're looking at a view supplied to them. The concealed camera reverses the direction of that exchange: people elsewhere receive a view of residents who haven't knowingly presented themselves for inspection. Juliette can avoid discussing something in a crowded cafeteria because she sees the other people there. In a room with a hidden lens, she lacks that ordinary warning about who might receive her words and actions.

Once Juliette has found the camera, Hanna's flowers remain a particularly concrete part of the discovery. They connect a concealed device to something a person could do within reach of it. The surveillance room may have screens and staff, but its view still depends on an unobstructed path through a particular room. Hanna could put flowers in that path. Years later, her daughter can look behind the mirror and find the reason that small arrangement mattered.
