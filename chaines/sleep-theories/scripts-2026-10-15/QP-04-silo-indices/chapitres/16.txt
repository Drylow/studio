The founders describe their project as a human seed bank. In their account in season three's eighth episode, the silos are meant to preserve people so that human life can continue. That description gives the underground rooms a purpose beyond keeping their present occupants alive. It asks us to think about the children born there, and about a future those children may never see. It also leaves a very practical matter to consider: what did the people being preserved understand about the lives arranged for them?

A seed bank stores something for later use. Applied to the silos, the comparison makes births, food supplies and the maintenance of habitable space part of a long survival plan. A broken generator threatens everyone who needs its power. A failed harvest would reach beyond the people tending the crops. The founders' description helps explain why preservation occupies so much of the silo's official language. They're describing a project whose success would have to extend beyond the working life of any individual engineer or doctor.

Inside Silo 18, that long project is experienced through ordinary responsibilities. Juliette works on machinery that people above her depend on, though most of them never visit Mechanical. Pete treats patients whose lives unfold within the same enclosed settlement. Porters carry goods along the stairs, and families raise children with no everyday access to the world beyond the cafeteria display. These people keep the refuge habitable through specific work. Whatever the founders intended, its continued existence requires the inhabitants to do things, learn skills and care for one another.

The distance between those two views matters. The founders can speak about preserving humanity across generations. Juliette has to get a machine working for people who are alive now. A survival plan may depend on both perspectives, but the people carrying it out still need enough information to judge what they're being asked to do. Juliette can examine a mechanical problem. She can't examine the whole founding rationale with the same freedom. The account offered in the Before Times belongs to people with access to a world she hasn't been allowed to investigate.

The Pact gives Silo 18's residents rules for living together, and references to the founders give those rules an authority older than anyone enforcing them. Billings takes the Pact seriously as a guide to his duties. Bernard has access to information unavailable to most residents. Both men live within the same installation, yet they don't approach its requirements with the same knowledge. Once the founders describe a human seed bank, the familiar language of preservation sounds connected to an original undertaking whose details have been distributed very unevenly.

Agreement is difficult to describe under those conditions. A resident can accept a job, help with a repair or support a particular official without having agreed to every purpose behind the silo. Juliette's willingness to maintain the generator doesn't tell us what she'd accept if someone laid out the entire project before her. Billings's commitment to his duties doesn't mean he's been shown everything Bernard knows. Their actions have immediate meanings within their lives. Treating each useful contribution as approval of the founders' whole design would assign them an agreement they haven't expressed.

Allison and Holston's attempt to have a child makes that problem especially concrete. They go through an authorized process and expect the medical procedure to match what they're told. Allison later discovers that her contraceptive implant remains in place. The permission they received hasn't given them the opportunity they understood it to give. Seen beside the human seed-bank rationale, reproductive permission has an obvious place in the administration of a population. For Allison and Holston, though, it concerns a child they hope to raise, and a procedure whose outcome they were entitled to understand.

The same issue appears in the information available to residents. George and Allison recover forbidden material from a hard drive, and Juliette follows questions that the authorized history doesn't answer. Their investigations expose them to danger within the place supposedly preserving them. From the founders' perspective, restrictions may belong to the maintenance of their project. For the residents, those restrictions affect whether they can assess its story at all. Calling the silo a refuge doesn't supply George with an account he can check or give Allison a way to inspect the promises made to her.

The founders' stated threat remains their account of why such a refuge was needed; the seed-bank description alone doesn't verify every part of it. What it does give us is a clearer view of the scale on which they're thinking. They're discussing the continuation of humanity, while later inhabitants are making decisions about work, illness and children within the resulting system. Those different scales can explain some of the distance between the founders' language and life in Silo 18. They don't make the inhabitants' immediate concerns disappear.

Allison agreed to a procedure she was told would let her try for a child. The founders' plan doesn't turn that agreement into consent to keeping the implant in place.
