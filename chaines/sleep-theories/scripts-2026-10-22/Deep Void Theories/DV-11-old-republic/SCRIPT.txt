Coruscant is under attack while Republic delegates are trying to negotiate peace with the Sith Empire. The Jedi Temple burns, and the government on the capital finds itself bargaining with an enemy that has already reached its home. The settlement becomes the Treaty of Coruscant. We'll begin with that damaged capital, then follow the older history behind it: how scattered worlds formed a Republic, how the Jedi became its protectors, and how successive wars changed the civilization both were trying to preserve.

This history follows Star Wars Legends, the older continuity, with spoilers for Dawn of the Jedi, Tales of the Jedi, both Knights of the Old Republic games, The Old Republic's core stories and major later expansions, and the Darth Bane trilogy. In the period surrounding The Old Republic, Coruscant is already the seat of an ancient government. Its senators represent worlds separated by immense distances. Its survival depends on ships being able to cross those distances, carrying officials, supplies and military help where they're needed.

The novel Deceived brings this situation close through Darth Malgus and the assault on the Jedi Temple. Malgus takes part in an operation that reaches into the Republic's capital while peace talks are underway on Alderaan. The timing gives the Empire a powerful advantage at the negotiations. The Republic's representatives are dealing with an enemy whose forces have already demonstrated that distance from the front offers no reliable shelter. The treaty follows that attack, and the Republic accepts a settlement that leaves it weakened.

For the Jedi, the damage reaches into a place where their work has been gathered and organized. The Temple belongs to an order whose members travel far beyond Coruscant, but those journeys still connect back to shared teaching and leadership. Aryn Leneer, a Jedi in Deceived, feels the death of her former master, Ven Zallow, through the Force. Her response takes her away from the negotiations and toward Coruscant. Through Aryn, the attack has a personal consequence alongside the diplomatic one: someone she knows has died, and the talks continue around that loss.

The Republic survives the sacking. The Empire doesn't replace the Senate with a permanent government of its own on Coruscant. Instead, the treaty leaves the Republic and Empire facing each other across a divided galaxy. That distinction matters for the history we're following. Coruscant can suffer a defeat without the Republic's entire history coming to an end. Its member worlds, officials and armed forces remain, though they must work within the conditions imposed by the peace. The government continues with fewer safe assumptions about its position.

The Jedi also have to decide where their work can continue. In The Old Republic's core setting, they have withdrawn to Tython after the destruction of the Temple. There, new students train and experienced Jedi conduct the business of an order that still has obligations beyond its own grounds. Tython connects this late crisis to a much older part of the story. Long before the Republic's familiar partnership with the Jedi, the world had been home to the Je'daii, whose approach to the Force developed under very different conditions.

Returning to that earlier period changes the scale of what Coruscant has lost. The Jedi weren't founded as a department of the Senate, and the Republic didn't begin with all the territory it would later claim. Each acquired its familiar shape over time. Worlds had to become reachable before they could participate in a shared political life. Force traditions had to develop, divide and change before the Jedi of the Temple could exist. The partnership visible at Coruscant was the result of a long history, with obligations accumulated across generations.

A record of such wars can sound orderly once someone has assembled it afterward. In The Old Republic's Galactic History, Jedi historian Gnost-Dural examines the Republic's conflicts with the Sith and the events that preceded them. His account belongs to a person within this world, working from the Jedi's position and concerns. That gives us a useful companion to the experiences of people such as Aryn. He looks across campaigns and generations; she has to live through the consequences of a particular assault. Both scales belong in a connected history.

From Coruscant, then, the route backward leads beyond the latest Empire and beyond the Temple that Malgus attacked. It reaches a period before there was a Republic capital for the Jedi to defend. On Tython, communities brought together through the Force lived within a system they hadn't yet learned to leave as the later Jedi would. Their difficulties concerned their own world, their teachings and the dangers of losing balance. This earlier history of the Jedi begins on Tython, before their order became a protector of the Republic.


The Tho Yor carried their passengers to Tython long before the Jedi had a temple on Coruscant. These great vessels gathered people from different worlds, people who felt a summons through the Force and entered structures whose full purpose they didn't understand. In Dawn of the Jedi, that gathering brings separate peoples into the same history. They arrive with different bodies, customs and inherited beliefs, and find themselves on a world where the Force can affect the conditions of life around them.

The Legends account of those arrivals has the character of a sacred history. The Tho Yor are physical vessels, yet the call that draws their passengers aboard belongs to the Force. Their journey gives the Je'daii a shared beginning without making them descendants of a single species or culture. Tython becomes a home where those differences have to be lived with. The tradition that develops there includes humans and other peoples, among them members of the Sith species, long before that name takes on its familiar association with an enemy order.

Tython itself helps explain why balance becomes so important to the Je'daii. Disturbances in the Force can produce violent storms on the planet. A struggle within a Force user therefore has consequences beyond that person's private thoughts or conduct. The world responds to imbalance, and the people who live there have to take that response seriously. Their teaching grows around the need to recognize both light and dark, understand their pull, and keep either from drawing a practitioner too far away from equilibrium.

The planet's moons give that teaching a visible setting. Ashla is associated with the light, while Bogan is associated with the dark. Je'daii who become unbalanced can be sent to one of the moons to meditate and recover their balance. Someone drawn too far toward darkness goes to Bogan; someone drawn too far toward the light goes to Ashla. That second possibility can feel unfamiliar beside the later Jedi. Within this earlier tradition, excessive attachment to either side calls for correction, and the moons are places where that work takes time.

Bogan is therefore part of Je'daii discipline as well as a place associated with danger. Daegen Lok's exile there gives the practice a particular human cost. Lok has seen a vision connected with a great chasm on Tython, and his insistence on what he believes it means places him at odds with the order. On the moon, he remains separated from the community whose judgment he disputes. His experience suggests how difficult this system can become when a practitioner believes an alarming vision deserves action and the other Je'daii see a person who needs to regain balance.

For younger members, learning involves movement between communities and kinds of work. Je'daii Journeyers travel among Tython's temples rather than completing their education within a single building. The temples have different fields of study, so a journey exposes a student to more than one master's habits and concerns. Akar Kesh is the Temple of Balance. Mahara Kesh concerns healing. Stav Kesh teaches martial skills. Together, these places make Je'daii training a broad education in how to live and act through the Force, with physical practice alongside contemplation.

Shae Koda's work at Anil Kesh gives that education a more unusual form. Anil Kesh is the Temple of Science, and Shae studies the alteration of living creatures through the Force. Such work belongs within the Je'daii's accepted range of inquiry. Their world includes people who study life by attempting to change it, just as other practitioners study healing or combat. Shae's impatience and curiosity give these activities a personal character. She's a young learner working within a demanding tradition, with interests that don't fit neatly into the later image of a Jedi carrying out a diplomatic assignment.

Tasha Ryo encounters another difficulty. Her mother, Kora Ryo, is a Je'daii master, while her father, Baron Volnos Ryo, has obligations and ambitions beyond the temples. Tasha's place between them brings family and political demands into her training. The Je'daii don't live apart from every other concern in their system. A student can belong to a family whose disputes continue while the temples pursue their own work. For Tasha, attending to the Force doesn't remove the need to respond to people who expect different things from her.

Settlement has also spread beyond Tython. People without sensitivity to the Force live on other worlds in the system, where they aren't exposed to Tython's dangers in the same way. Over generations, those communities develop their own affairs and tensions. The Despot War, fought before the main events of Dawn of the Jedi, draws the Je'daii into armed conflict within that system. Its aftermath is part of the world the younger Journeyers inherit. Their teachers' experience includes warfare, and the temples' martial instruction has uses beyond an exercise between students.

The arrival of Xesh interrupts that existing life. He comes to Tython aboard a vessel that crashes, carrying a Force-empowered weapon unfamiliar to the Je'daii. Shae Koda, Tasha Ryo and Sek'nos Rath are drawn into the consequences of his arrival. Xesh has been trained under conditions very different from theirs, and violence has shaped his understanding of what he can do. Meeting him gives the Journeyers a difficult encounter with another use of the same Force they study. Their teachings have to guide their response to a dangerous person whose past they don't initially understand.

The Je'daii carry swords rather than the familiar lightsabers of the later Jedi. Xesh's forcesaber introduces a different kind of weapon into that martial tradition.

Tython's importance begins with that particular way of life. The Tho Yor brought strangers together, and their descendants built places where the Force could be studied through healing, science, meditation and armed practice. Shae, Tasha and Sek'nos receive that inheritance while finding that it doesn't make every encounter easy to understand. Around them, masters disagree, families make demands, and an exile on Bogan holds to his troubling vision. The Je'daii have an established tradition, with enough experience to teach their young and enough unresolved difficulties to keep those teachings under strain.


Xesh carries a forcesaber, a weapon whose blade depends on the Force and whose use draws on the dark side. In Dawn of the Jedi, that weapon belongs to the military world of the Rakata. They train Force-sensitive slaves called Force Hounds to find other inhabited worlds, making a captive's abilities useful to further conquest. Xesh has served as one of those hunters. His arrival near Tython brings the Je'daii into contact with an empire that has already made the Force part of its machinery of expansion.

The Rakata call their dominion the Infinite Empire. Its ships can cross the distances between star systems, while their subjects supply labor and endure enslavement. Force sensitivity helps determine which worlds the Rakata can reach through their technology. That gives their expansion a particular shape. Worlds rich in the Force attract an empire equipped to recognize and exploit them. Tython, with its powerful and sometimes dangerous connection to the Force, is the kind of destination their hunters are trained to discover.

Xesh serves the Rakatan master Tul'kar, who treats him as a possession despite the abilities that make him useful.

The Rakata's technology also gives their armies advantages that the Je'daii have to confront in battle. Dawn of the Jedi brings invading Rakatan forces into the Tython system, where the defenders face weapons and methods developed through earlier conquests. The Je'daii's familiarity with the Force doesn't make those encounters easy. Their opponents have built a military practice around compelling others to serve them. For the people of Tython, resistance means learning what that practice can do while fighting to keep their own worlds outside it.

Much later, Knights of the Old Republic encounters the empire through what remains on conquered planets. Beneath the surface of Kashyyyk, a Rakatan computer survives in the Shadowlands. On Dantooine, ancient ruins contain functioning machinery and guardian droids. These places have outlasted the society that built them. They can still respond to visitors, defend their interiors and preserve information, even though the people living above them have very different concerns. The old occupation has left structures that don't disappear when its rulers do.

Kashyyyk offers a particularly uncomfortable example. The computer encountered there describes ancient work involving the planet's environment and the growth of its vegetation. Its account links Rakatan activity to the immense forests through which the Wookiees now live and travel. That information comes from a surviving machine with a limited task, rather than a complete planetary history. Even so, it gives the Shadowlands another layer. Beneath a living forest, something from the empire continues carrying out procedures established long before the present inhabitants encountered it.

The Star Forge is the largest surviving expression of that technical ambition. Built near the Rakatan homeworld, known as Lehon, it draws energy and material from a nearby star to manufacture warships and other military resources. Its operation is bound up with the dark side. A ruler who can use it gains access to production on an extraordinary scale. The forge explains how Rakatan engineering could sustain conquest across distant systems, supplying the vessels and equipment needed to carry armed forces beyond their homeworld.

Yet the Star Forge also brings dangers to those who rely on it. In Knights of the Old Republic, the Rakatan accounts associate it with the corruption and destructive habits of their ancestors. The machine remains usable long after their empire has vanished, and its output can equip another war. Its survival separates the fate of the builders from the fate of their creation. The Rakata lose the civilization that made such construction possible, while the enormous installation continues to threaten people who never lived under Rakatan rule.

On Lehon itself, that loss has become part of daily life. The Rakata encountered there live among the remains of their ancestors' achievements, without the old ability to operate their Force-dependent technology. Their accounts describe a plague, the loss of their connection to the Force, and rebellions among enslaved populations as parts of the empire's collapse. With their technological advantages failing, the Rakata could no longer hold their subjects as they once had. The ruins on their homeworld belong to a people whose circumstances have changed beyond recognition.

The surviving Rakata don't all respond to that inheritance in the same way. The One leads a violent tribe and seeks a way into the Temple of the Ancients. The Elders preserve more knowledge of the past and regard their ancestors' empire with shame. Their disagreement has practical consequences for visitors, who find rival groups making demands and treating the temple differently. Sharing an ancestry hasn't given them a common purpose. Ancient Rakatan achievements have become objects of struggle among descendants who can no longer reproduce them.

The Elders' interest in recovering their species' connection to the Force adds another difficulty. For their ancestors, that connection helped ships travel and weapons function. Recovering it could restore abilities lost during the collapse, but the Elders also understand what their people once did with those abilities. Their research therefore sits beside their judgment of the past. Lehon holds both the desire to recover something lost and the surviving testimony of people who consider the old empire's treatment of other beings a source of disgrace.


A ship leaving Coruscant needs a usable course to its destination. Having a hyperdrive doesn't make every direction safe. Stars, planets and other masses affect navigation, and a journey depends on calculations that carry a vessel past those hazards. A known hyperspace route gives travelers a course that others have already surveyed and used. As those courses connect inhabited systems, a world can exchange goods, receive visitors and maintain regular contact with governments far beyond its own sun. That regular passage helps explain how separate planetary societies begin to form a Republic.

In the early Republic history of Star Wars Legends, worlds of the galactic Core build a shared government around connections like these. Coruscant becomes its capital, while Corellia and other early member worlds retain their own identities and local affairs. The early chronology is sparse compared with the wars recorded in the games and comics. Its broad development is clear enough, though: travel makes sustained diplomacy possible, and diplomacy gives connected worlds a way to settle common business without treating every arriving ship as the representative of a foreign power.

Hyperspace routes aren't tunnels constructed between planets. They're navigable courses through hyperspace, discovered and maintained through exploration and the collection of navigational information. A chart can therefore have considerable practical value. A captain needs to know where a course leads and whether the ship can follow it safely. A government needs reliable passage for couriers and supplies. Two systems may look close on a map while remaining awkward to reach, because the useful route between them follows the available navigation rather than the shortest visible line.

Corellia belongs among the early Republic's influential worlds. Later accounts also associate it strongly with shipbuilding, an industry on which regular interstellar travel depends.

The Corellian Run and the Corellian Trade Spine become major arteries of galactic travel. Other great routes, including the Perlemian Trade Route, connect the Core with more distant regions. Their importance grows out of repeated use. Merchants can plan around established destinations, and worlds along the way gain access to traffic that might otherwise pass beyond their reach. The Republic's expansion consequently follows an uneven pattern. A distant system with good access to a major route can be more closely involved in galactic affairs than a nearer system reached by a difficult journey.

Coruscant's position gives those journeys a political destination. The planet becomes the place where member worlds send representatives and where the Senate conducts the Republic's business. The office of Supreme Chancellor provides leadership within that larger government. These institutions allow disputes and shared concerns to be addressed above the planetary level. Membership doesn't erase a world's own government. Across the Republic's history, planets continue to have rulers, councils and local laws, while their representatives participate in decisions that affect the wider community.

Alderaan's royal tradition shows how a world's local institutions can persist through its relationship with the Republic. In The Old Republic's class-story era, the struggle among its noble houses also affects its ties to the competing galactic powers. That later situation gives some substance to an institution that can otherwise sound remote. A senator's seat belongs to a world with internal politics, influential families and arguments of its own. Decisions made on Coruscant have to operate through those existing societies, whose disagreements don't disappear when they join a larger government.

Goods move through the same network as envoys. Food, industrial materials and manufactured equipment can cross between systems with different resources and needs. Coruscant's growth into a planet-spanning city makes that dependence particularly visible in the Republic's later history. Its inhabitants rely on supplies arriving from elsewhere. The routes that allow a capital to gather people and institutions also have to sustain them. Keeping ships moving is therefore part of governing the Republic, even when the work involves cargo schedules and safe passage rather than a vote in the Senate.

Knights of the Old Republic also shows why a connected galaxy can't be treated as a single political territory. Manaan's government regulates access to its kolto and maintains neutrality between the Republic and the Sith. Republic personnel can travel there and negotiate, yet they still have to deal with the Selkath authorities. A route makes Manaan accessible without giving Coruscant the right to command it. Throughout Republic history, inhabited space includes member worlds, independent governments and regions where other powers hold influence. Travel crosses those boundaries more readily than institutions do.

The Republic's survival doesn't mean uninterrupted peace. The same routes carry merchants and military forces, and worlds connected by trade can later find themselves on opposing sides.

The early Republic takes shape through the continuing work of keeping worlds in contact. Ships carry representatives to Coruscant, charts guide them along established courses, and the Senate gives their governments a place to conduct shared affairs. Local societies remain recognizable within that arrangement. Alderaan doesn't have to become Coruscant to participate, and Corellia's shipyards remain part of a world with its own interests. Across the distances between them, regular journeys make a common government workable. A decision reached in the capital can travel outward aboard a ship and become business for another world.


On Ossus, Jedi learning gathers around teachers who have responsibilities far beyond a single student's lessons. Odan-Urr maintains a great library, preserving writings that other Jedi can consult and study. A journey to a distant world may separate a Jedi from the teacher who first guided them, but the Order has places where knowledge can accumulate across generations. That matters for the Republic's growing association with the Jedi. The people it calls upon for help belong to a community with its own education and obligations.

In Tales of the Jedi, that community still has a distinctly local shape. Arca Jeth teaches on Arkania, Vodo-Siosk Baas trains students on Dantooine, and Thon lives on Ambria. Their students don't all begin in the same building or follow their teachers through identical circumstances. A master takes responsibility for particular people, observes their difficulties, and decides when they're ready to undertake work beyond their training ground. Across these separate settings, the Jedi develop the skills that make their service useful to worlds whose inhabitants may never visit Ossus.

Arca's pupils include Ulic Qel-Droma, his brother Cay, and the Twi'lek Tott Doneeta. Their preparation involves learning to act together despite different temperaments and abilities. Ulic is confident and eager to intervene, while Cay's technical skill gives him another way to solve practical problems. Tott can communicate with creatures whose behavior the others struggle to understand. A mission can require all of those abilities. Sending several students together allows Arca to give them experience without expecting one young Jedi to possess every answer.

When Arca sends these pupils to Onderon, their assignment concerns a conflict within an inhabited world. They arrive among people who already have rulers, enemies and explanations for why fighting continues. The Jedi have to encounter that situation before they can help resolve it. Their teacher's decision puts them in the role of mediators as well as combatants. This is one recognizable form of early Jedi service: traveling to a troubled society and trying to bring a dispute to an end, with the possibility that their first understanding will need correction.

Arca remains responsible for the pupils he sends. When the assignment exceeds what they can manage, he intervenes himself. His use of battle meditation shows that Jedi assistance can affect an entire confrontation, helping allies coordinate while weakening an opponent's effectiveness. Yet the expedition also depends on the judgment of its teacher. The students' difficulties don't end Arca's responsibility at the moment they leave Arkania. Training and public service remain connected through a master who must decide when to send help and when to go personally.

Nomi Sunrider approaches this same community from a very different starting point. Her husband, Andur, is traveling to continue his Jedi education when he's killed during their journey. Nomi survives with their daughter, Vima, and eventually reaches Thon on Ambria. Her arrival carries grief and the practical responsibility of caring for a child. Before she can become someone other people turn to during a crisis, she needs a teacher willing to work with her reluctance and help her understand abilities she hasn't yet accepted.

Thon's instruction takes place on a world marked by dangerous dark-side influences. Nomi's lessons therefore involve responding to real danger, alongside developing her use of the Force. Her resistance to taking up a lightsaber has a particular cause in Andur's death and her own experience of violence. Thon can't address that difficulty by treating her as an interchangeable recruit. 

Vodo-Siosk Baas provides another view of how much depends on the teacher. On Dantooine, he instructs Exar Kun alongside Crado and Sylvar. Vodo can meet a lightsaber with his wooden staff through his command of the Force, giving his students a direct demonstration of how much their understanding still lacks. His lessons also bring him into conflict with Kun's pride and impatience. The same training community that produces capable defenders has to deal with students who resent limits, including limits imposed by someone more experienced.

These teachers have standing within the Order, and Jedi also gather in councils to consider problems together. A master's judgment belongs within that wider community of other masters, shared teachings and accumulated experience. Ossus gives this arrangement a lasting home for study, while gatherings allow Jedi from separate places to deliberate face to face. Their service to the Republic develops alongside these internal practices. The Senate doesn't conduct Nomi's training, decide how Vodo should teach Kun, or replace Arca's responsibility for his pupils.

A later comparison appears in Knights of the Old Republic, where the Jedi Council on Dantooine receives the central character and oversees their instruction. The enclave has teachers, students and grounds used for training, even while the Republic faces a military emergency. Individual lessons continue because an unprepared Jedi would bring additional dangers into that emergency. The setting makes an older arrangement familiar: public demands reach the Order, and the Order responds through people whose preparation remains the responsibility of Jedi teachers.

Across these eras, the Jedi provide public service while retaining responsibility for their own training and deliberations. Their association with the Republic grows through that continuing work, rather than through a single settlement that fixes every later relationship.


The defeated Dark Jedi were sent away from the territory they'd fought to change. After the Hundred-Year Darkness, the survivors of their struggle with the Jedi departed into exile and eventually reached Korriban. There they encountered a people who already had their own rulers, religious practices and command of the dark side. These were the Sith. The name belonged to a people before it became the title carried by generations of dark side masters, and the newcomers' arrival began the long process that joined those meanings together.

The conflict behind that journey had begun within the Jedi. In the older Legends history, the dissidents pursued uses of the Force that the Order rejected, including dark side experiments involving living creatures. Their ambitions brought them into a prolonged war with the Jedi they'd separated from. The name Hundred-Year Darkness describes that extended struggle, rather than the whole history of disagreement within the Order. By its end, the defeated faction had lost its attempt to prevail in the world of the Republic. Exile carried the surviving participants beyond that familiar setting.

Their defeat at Corbos brought the war to its close. 

Korriban wasn't an empty refuge waiting for settlers. The Sith had a civilization of their own, and their use of the Force hadn't begun with instruction from the exiles. Their traditions included sorcery and a social order in which strength in the dark side could carry religious and political weight. For the newcomers, that created an opening. They'd arrived after losing a war, but among the Sith they could present powers and knowledge that helped them claim a position above the people who received them.

The exiles gained ascendancy over the native Sith and became their rulers. The newcomers were treated as beings of extraordinary power, and their conquest established a lasting hierarchy. Ajunta Pall is remembered among the first Dark Lords associated with this foundation. The title attached a ruling outsider to the Sith people beneath him. A former Jedi could therefore become a Lord of the Sith without having been born a member of the Sith species, a distinction that later uses of the name can easily obscure.

That change didn't happen through a simple replacement of the local population. The exiles and the Sith became connected through intermixture as well as domination, and their descendants inherited elements of both communities. Across generations, ancestry and teaching became difficult to separate. A ruler might belong to the population descended from the native Sith while also inheriting the traditions of the fallen Jedi. The emerging Sith civilization carried both histories.

The physical Sith remained recognizable within that civilization. In The Golden Age of the Sith, red-skinned figures with distinctive facial features belong to the society that has developed from this encounter. Yet appearance alone doesn't explain someone's place within its ruling order. Sith identity has acquired associations with descent, service and knowledge of the dark side. When later stories call a human dark side adept a Sith, they're using a meaning that this early history helped make possible. The word has traveled beyond its original use for a people.

Korriban's tombs give that ruling tradition a durable presence. The Valley of the Dark Lords contains monuments to figures whose standing survives their deaths, and the burial places remain important to those who seek ancient Sith knowledge. A tomb can hold dangerous objects and teachings as well as the remains of a ruler. Within this culture, approaching the dead can become part of a living adept's pursuit of power. The authority claimed by the first exiles consequently outlasts the people who established it.

Knights of the Old Republic returns to that inheritance through Ajunta Pall's tomb. The player character encounters Pall's spirit, who speaks about his fall and the ruin associated with his former companions. His account is the testimony of a dead participant looking back on his own life. The encounter allows different responses, including an attempt to help him turn away from the darkness. Those player choices vary, but the setting consistently places an ancient fallen Jedi within the burial ground of the Sith.

Pall's presence also gives the early schism a personal consequence that the broad account of conquest can conceal. The exile who became a revered lord remains attached to the place where his power was commemorated. In the game, his recollection contains regret rather than the confidence his title might suggest. The monument and the person encountered inside it give different impressions of the same career. Someone approaching the tomb for an ancient weapon meets a figure troubled by what he became.

Beyond Korriban, the Sith realm developed apart from the Republic. The exiles' defeat had removed them from the institutions and territories where their rebellion began, while their success among the Sith gave their successors a separate society to inherit. The Republic didn't absorb this community, and the Jedi didn't oversee its teaching. Over time, that separation allowed a dark side tradition descended partly from Jedi dissidents to become established across an unfamiliar region, with its own rulers and competing claims to rank.

By the era depicted in The Golden Age of the Sith, those beginnings lie behind an established empire. Its lords inherit a civilization that neither the original exiles nor the native Sith had possessed before their encounter. The Hundred-Year Darkness ended with the rebels expelled from the Republic's world, but their departure carried their teachings to Korriban. There, conquest and generations of shared descent gave the surviving tradition a new name and a home among the Sith.


Gav and Jori Daragon reach Korriban while the Sith lords are gathered for the funeral of Marka Ragnos. Their ship has crossed into a region unfamiliar to the Republic, and its arrival interrupts a dispute already forming around the dead ruler's succession. Naga Sadow and Ludo Kressh both seek leadership. The strangers give that rivalry a new subject. Someone has found a passage into Sith territory, which means the empire's long separation from the Republic may be ending whether its rulers welcome the contact or fear it.

In the Star Wars Legends comics The Golden Age of the Sith and The Fall of the Sith Empire, the Daragons are hyperspace explorers whose search for usable routes has brought them into danger. A successful discovery could make their voyages worthwhile, but an unknown destination can also leave them without protection or a way to explain themselves. On Korriban, they encounter rulers who treat their arrival as a matter of imperial survival. Gav and Jori have reached an inhabited civilization, yet neither sibling can negotiate with it as an equal.

Kressh regards the outsiders as a threat and wants them killed. Sadow sees an opportunity to reach the worlds beyond the empire. Their disagreement grows out of different expectations about what contact will bring. Kressh fears the destruction of the Sith way of life through dealings with the Republic. Sadow believes the Republic can be conquered. The siblings' ship gives his ambition a practical direction, because its journey demonstrates that the distance between the two societies can be crossed.

Sadow doesn't wait for the other lords to agree. He arranges an attack that appears to involve enemies from the Republic, then uses the disturbance to remove the prisoners from captivity. The deception makes his warnings about an outside enemy seem more urgent. It also places Gav and Jori in his hands. They understand that he has saved them from execution, while he has created circumstances in which their gratitude and uncertainty can help him. The argument over succession now includes an apparent emergency that Sadow himself has manufactured.

The siblings are then drawn along different paths. Sadow begins teaching Gav to use the Force, giving him attention and instruction at a moment when he's stranded among strangers. Jori remains concerned with getting away and returning home. Sadow allows her escape to become useful to him by tracking her ship's passage back toward Republic space. Her flight carries a warning to the people she knows, but it also supplies the Sith with a route for their invasion. The same passage can carry an explorer home and bring a fleet after her.

Back in the Koros system, Jori has difficulty persuading others of what she's encountered. Her background and the trouble surrounding the Daragons' earlier voyages don't make her an obvious authority on an unknown empire. Empress Teta nevertheless takes the warning seriously, alongside the Jedi Odan-Urr, whose visions have already disturbed him. Teta has been fighting to unite the Koros worlds. She now has to consider an enemy beyond that local struggle, arriving by a route that her own people have only just discovered.

Sadow assembles his invasion while his rivalry with Kressh continues. He has gained the initiative through deception and military action, but the lords haven't become a settled community behind him. That matters because he is committing Sith forces to distant targets while leaving enemies within his own society. His decision brings the Great Hyperspace War into Republic territory. For those facing the attack, the danger arrives as ships and soldiers. They have little opportunity to untangle the succession dispute that helped send those forces toward them.

The invasion reaches worlds including Coruscant and Koros Major. On Coruscant, the Republic and its Jedi defenders face Sith troops and the creatures fighting alongside them. The capital's importance doesn't spare it from direct assault. Sadow also expands the apparent size of his forces through Sith illusion, projecting images that make the attacks seem more overwhelming. From his meditation sphere, he can influence battles far from his own position. Republic defenders are therefore fighting actual invading forces while also confronting appearances sustained by a distant Sith lord.

Gav's part in the campaign brings the war back to people he recognizes. On Koros Major, he encounters Aarrba, the Hutt who had helped the Daragons during their earlier difficulties. Sith warriors kill Aarrba, and Jori finds her brother amid the consequences of an invasion he has helped bring home. Gav's instruction under Sadow has placed him among the attackers. Whatever hopes he attached to that instruction, he now has to confront the suffering caused by the forces traveling under his new master's command.

Gav turns against Sadow and attacks his meditation sphere. The interruption breaks Sadow's concentration, and the illusions supporting the invasion disappear. The defenders can see that the attacking strength is smaller than it appeared. Their resistance has already mattered, and this disruption gives them a further opening. On the Republic side, Teta's forces help drive the invaders back. Canderous's admiration for a difficult battle also has limits as an account of what the war meant. Canderous can describe a campaign as a demanding military undertaking. Juhani has to explain why her family fled. Cathar suffered one of the war's most terrible attacks. The Mandalorian assault devastated the Cathar people, and the destruction remained part of how survivors understood their scattered lives. For Juhani, Cathar wasn't a distant name in a military history. On Taris, Juhani's parents encountered prejudice against aliens as well as poverty. Her father struggled after the loss of Cathar and was eventually killed. Those events weren't all Mandalorian military actions. They were consequences within a refugee family's life, as the loss of a home placed them somewhere they had few protections. Leaving the battlefield hadn't brought their suffering to an end.

The Republic's difficulty was partly a matter of deciding when these expanding attacks demanded a wider military response. The Mandalorians pressed through the Outer Rim before the war became a full confrontation with the Republic. Communities in their path experienced that progression locally, through attacks and conquest. A government farther from the fighting could still weigh commitments while a frontier world was already being overrun. That difference helps explain the bitterness surrounding the Republic's response. The decision to fight a major war took time, and threatened populations bore the cost of that time.

The Jedi faced growing pressure as reports of the war spread. Their Council remained reluctant to commit the Order, concerned about dangers beyond the immediate fighting and cautious after earlier conflicts involving fallen Jedi. To people seeking protection, that caution could be difficult to accept. The Republic had defenders in uniform, but the Jedi were also associated with intervention when ordinary defenses failed. Every conquered world made their absence harder to explain to those who believed they should be helping. The disagreement would become especially painful among Jedi who felt the suffering demanded action.

For this part of the war, Cathar and Taris explain why the Mandalorian advance couldn't remain a remote strategic concern. Cathar had survivors who carried news and grief beyond the planet. Taris received refugees without giving all of them safety. Soldiers such as Canderous remembered the campaigns as tests of military strength, while Juhani remembered a family displaced and then broken. By the time the Republic faced the Neo-Crusaders in open war, the consequences already extended beyond the systems under attack. People who'd escaped those systems were trying to build lives elsewhere, with losses that followed them into the Republic.


Revan called for Jedi to join the Republic's defense while the Council still counseled restraint. Its soldiers were facing an expanding enemy, and Jedi training offered abilities that could help them. Revan gave that conviction a practical direction. A Jedi who disagreed with the Council now had someone to follow, and a war in which to serve.

The disagreement ran through an Order whose members shared much of the same education. Revan and Malak had learned to protect others, to recognize suffering, and to accept responsibilities beyond their own lives. Those teachings helped make intervention persuasive. Remaining apart from the fighting could feel difficult to reconcile with them. The Council, however, also expected Jedi to examine consequences that weren't immediately apparent. The dispute concerned how those obligations should guide action when the Republic was under attack, and whether an individual Jedi could decide that the Council had waited long enough.

In Knights of the Old Republic, Bastila describes Revan's departure as defiance of the Council. Her account carries the perspective of a Jedi who accepts the Order's authority and regards its warnings seriously. The volunteers had broken with a decision made by their own teachers. Bastila's concern makes sense within an institution that gives its members dangerous abilities and expects discipline in their use. A Jedi entering a war would face decisions about killing, obedience, and the lives of soldiers. The Council couldn't regard that commitment as an ordinary difference of opinion.

Revan's appeal reached Jedi who thought those risks had to be accepted. The Republic was already fighting, so refusing to participate didn't remove the danger faced by its people. Before exile became the condition of that character's life, there had been a decision to leave the Order's protection and accept responsibility within a military campaign.

The Exile's conversations allow the player to express different reasons for that decision. Compassion for people under attack can be central, while other responses give the past a different emphasis. Those are local player choices, rather than a single fixed confession. Across those variations, the shared history remains that the Exile went when the Council wouldn't commit the Order. That matters because Revan's movement contained individual Jedi with their own reasons for joining. Following the same leader didn't require every volunteer to have reached the battlefield through exactly the same reasoning.

Republic soldiers had another reason to welcome them. Carth Onasi remembers the Mandalorian Wars as a veteran, and he credits Revan with helping turn the Republic's military fortunes. Canderous Ordo supplies a view from the opposing side. He remembers Revan as a formidable commander whose methods challenged the Mandalorians. Together, their accounts help explain the standing Revan acquired during the war. Republic service and Mandalorian resistance both brought people into contact with his military ability. The Council could withhold its approval, but it couldn't prevent that reputation from growing among the people doing the fighting.

Malak was beside Revan in this break with the Council. The games remember them together as the Jedi who committed themselves to the Republic's cause and drew others after them. Revan receives most of the attention in later recollections, especially when characters discuss command. Malak's participation still matters to the division within the Order. The volunteers were following leading Jedi who'd publicly taken a different course from their teachers. The Council's reluctance also needs its own setting. The Jedi remembered earlier wars in which members of their Order had fallen to the dark side and carried destruction into the Republic. That concern didn't give the Council an easy answer for someone facing a Mandalorian assault. Revan could direct a willing Jedi toward service with Republic forces. This difference helps explain the movement's appeal without requiring the Council's members to be indifferent to suffering. Bao-Dur built the Mass Shadow Generator, and at Malachor V the general who would become the Jedi Exile gave the order to use it. Bao-Dur had joined the Republic's war effort after the Mandalorians attacked his homeworld, Iridonia. For the Exile, the deaths at Malachor also arrived through the Force. The Jedi had formed bonds with others during the war, and the loss of those lives became unbearable. That break happened before the Council's sentence of exile. The Masters' later examination of the returning Jedi belongs to the aftermath of an injury already sustained at Malachor. They encountered someone whose condition disturbed their understanding of what a Jedi could survive. The player can shape how the Exile speaks about wartime service and its consequences, but the central history remains: the order was given, the battlefield was destroyed, and the Exile came away severed from the Force.

Other Jedi who survived Revan's campaign carried a different experience away from the same war. They'd fought under his direction and remained with forces whose victories had strengthened his position. Malachor destroyed an enemy army, while the surviving Republic fleet still had a commander and a military organization. The Exile's departure wasn't the common ending for everyone who had followed Revan. Kreia later gives that survival a darker interpretation. She describes Revan as someone who understood how war could change Jedi and draw them away from their earlier loyalties. Canderous remembers the defeat from the Mandalorian side. He respects Revan's ability even while describing the destruction of the cause he'd served. Bao-Dur's later work on Telos gives another survivor a specific occupation after Malachor. He helps the effort to restore a world devastated during the conflicts that followed the Mandalorian Wars. The Mandalorian Wars ended with Revan victorious and the Mandalorian campaign broken. Malachor remained ruined. After the Mandalorian defeat, Revan and Malak had disappeared into the regions beyond familiar Republic space. Knights of the Old Republic introduces this history through people living with the invasion's consequences. Republic soldiers know the names of the commanders opposing them. Its factories make the planet valuable to military powers, and its people have reasons of their own to fight over who governs it. Republic involvement with the resistance allows the struggle to continue without beginning with a formal declaration of war. For the Empire, suppressing that resistance requires soldiers and resources long after conquest. For Balmorrans, the diplomatic peace leaves fighting in their home settlements and industrial facilities.

Alderaan offers another way for both powers to compete. After withdrawing from the Republic, the planet is divided by a struggle among its noble houses. The Republic backs House Organa, while the Empire supports House Thul. Local succession and allegiance give outside powers an opening, but the houses have their own histories and ambitions. Neither side can treat Alderaan as an empty position on a military chart. Support has to pass through rulers who want a future for their families, and those rulers must consider what accepting foreign help will cost.

Nar Shaddaa is useful for different reasons. Under Hutt rule, its crowded districts give Republic and Imperial representatives room to pursue interests outside their own territory. Criminal networks, private businesses, and armed groups complicate those efforts. An official mission can depend on arrangements with people who owe neither government loyalty. The moon's importance comes partly from that freedom of movement. Goods and information can pass through channels that a Republic senator or Imperial officer doesn't wholly command, giving non-Force users a considerable part in the rivalry.

These competing societies also depend on specialists who move outside the training grounds of Jedi and Sith, carrying supplies, gathering intelligence and accepting private contracts.


On Ord Mantell, a new recruit to Havoc Squad discovers that the Republic's elite soldiers are preparing to abandon it. Commander Tavus and most of the existing squad defect to the Empire, leaving the recruit to help rebuild the unit and pursue former comrades. The trooper's story begins with a military failure that can't be solved by winning a battle against separatists. People entrusted with Republic weapons and operational knowledge have decided to take their skills elsewhere. Their replacements have to recover both the missing personnel and the dangerous equipment moving with them.

Tavus explains his betrayal through grievances against the Republic leadership, including its treatment of soldiers sent into difficult operations. Those complaints are his account of why he leaves. His defection still places Republic lives at risk, and the trooper encounters former Havoc members working for the enemy. General Garza directs the response through Special Forces, where sensitive missions often involve decisions kept away from public scrutiny. Serving her means dealing with the consequences of military secrecy while relying on classified orders to reach the next objective.

Rebuilding Havoc also brings an Imperial defector into Republic service. Elara Dorne is an experienced medic whose knowledge makes her useful, although her background attracts suspicion. Aric Jorgan arrives through a different kind of institutional injury, after the Ord Mantell disaster damages his career. Together, these companions give the rebuilt squad practical strengths and personal difficulties that a recruitment roster wouldn't explain. Later operations against the Gauntlet, an Imperial superweapon capable of disrupting travel through hyperspace, show why the Republic needs such a unit. Ships carrying soldiers and supplies must be able to reach their destinations.

The smuggler begins on Ord Mantell with a much smaller transportation problem. Skavak steals the smuggler's ship, taking away the vessel on which the character's livelihood depends. Recovering it leads through criminal dealings and brings Corso Riggs into the crew. For a military officer, a ship belongs within a chain of command and supply. The smuggler has to negotiate passage through a world of theft, personal obligations, and opportunities that can disappear as soon as someone else gets hold of the cargo.

Risha's business aboard the recovered ship opens a further route into that economy. Her connection to the pirate Nok Drayen draws the smuggler into the search for his hidden wealth, with deliveries and exchanges carrying the journey across several worlds. Risha also has a claim tied to the royal family of Dubrillion, so the woman helping manage the voyage has concerns beyond its immediate profit. The crew's work passes between private fortunes, criminal reputations, and political inheritance. A cargo run can become part of a dispute that began far away from the landing pad.

Republic officials eventually recruit the smuggler for privateering, giving independent work an official military purpose. Darmas Pollaran and Senator Dodonna help draw the character into these arrangements, though their loyalties make the relationship dangerous. The Imperial commander known as the Voidwolf seeks to bring criminal forces into his own campaign. That gives the smuggler a place in the war through contacts and access that a uniformed Republic commander would struggle to reproduce. The player can shape how generously or selfishly that position is used, but the struggle over independent ships remains central to the story.

Imperial Intelligence approaches those same crowded networks through observation and deception. On Hutta, the agent works under a cover identity to influence Nem'ro the Hutt's dealings in the Empire's favor. Kaliyo Djannis joins the agent there, bringing skills and priorities that don't fit neatly into official service. The assignment depends on entering Nem'ro's organization and making Imperial interests look useful to him. Intelligence officers need people who can work among criminals without arriving as an occupying force, and they accept considerable personal risk to obtain that access.

Back within the Imperial hierarchy, the agent discovers how vulnerable the intelligence service is to Sith intervention. Darth Jadus imposes his own designs, and the struggle surrounding the terrorist network led by the Eagle reaches into the service itself. Keeper and the other handlers have procedures, surveillance, and trained personnel, yet a Sith superior can place all of them in danger. The agent's work requires discretion while the people above the agent can make decisions that expose entire operations. Professional competence offers little shelter when a powerful Sith turns against fellow Imperials.

An assignment inside the Republic's Strategic Information Service makes that vulnerability intimate. Arden Kothe uses conditioning implanted in the agent to compel obedience, leaving the operative unable to act freely at moments when cover and survival depend on it. Watcher X's earlier involvement becomes relevant to the effort to break that conditioning. Later, the pursuit of the Star Cabal uncovers a conspiracy working across political boundaries. The agent's possible endings differ, but the released story repeatedly puts the operative between intelligence institutions whose demands reach into the character's own mind.

The bounty hunter enters this world through sponsorship rather than enlistment. On Hutta, Braden prepares a promising hunter for the Great Hunt, supported by Mako and Jory. Tarro Blood's attack kills Braden and Jory, leaving Mako and the hunter to continue after the loss of their team. Entering the competition requires securing Nem'ro's backing. Reputation begins with completed work and someone willing to sponsor it, while rivals can use violence outside the formal contest to remove a competitor before the hunt has properly begun.

Success in the Great Hunt brings the hunter into contact with Mandalorian leadership and more prestigious contracts. It also attracts enemies with the resources to pursue a famous professional. Jedi Master Jun Seros drives a campaign against the hunter, and Darth Tormen later employs the hunter in an Imperial offensive. The player can make different decisions when that service reaches Supreme Chancellor Janarus. By then, a career built around accepting targets has carried the hunter into Republic leadership. Mako's surviving partner, Havoc's replacement commander, a privateer, and an intelligence operative all reach the war through jobs that keep its campaigns moving.


Lord Scourge turns against the Emperor after serving as his Wrath, the Sith charged with carrying out his master's will. He comes to the Jedi Knight with a warning that reaches beyond the Republic's military position. According to Scourge, the Emperor intends to consume life across the galaxy through a vast dark side ritual. Imperial soldiers and Sith would perish along with their enemies. The threat gives Scourge and the Jedi a common enemy, despite their opposing allegiances.

The original Jedi Knight story in Star Wars Legends gives that alliance a difficult beginning. Before Scourge joins the Knight's crew, Jedi Master Tol Braga leads an attempt to capture the Emperor and turn him toward the light. Braga's expedition reaches the Emperor's fortress, where the Jedi discover that they've underestimated him. He overwhelms their minds and bends them to his service. The Knight eventually breaks free with help from the spirit of Master Orgus Din, but the mission leaves other members of the expedition trapped in the Emperor's influence.

That experience changes the practical meaning of confronting him. Braga had approached the fortress with a plan for redemption, supported by capable Jedi. Afterward, the Knight has to reckon with an enemy who can make those same Jedi carry out his commands. Kira Carsen has already brought this danger close to the crew. Raised among the Emperor's Children, she carries a connection through which he can reach her. She resists him, and her survival gives the Knight a companion who understands his intrusion as something she's endured herself.

Scourge's defection also carries an older burden. He explains that he once accompanied Revan and Meetra Surik against the Emperor, then betrayed them after receiving a vision of a future Jedi who could defeat him. That's Scourge's account of why he waited and why he now helps the Knight. His presence aboard a Jedi's ship doesn't erase what he did. It gives the crew an ally with knowledge of the Emperor's service, and a reason to accept assistance from someone whose earlier betrayal helped their enemy survive.

The Emperor's preparations send the Knight after servants who are working to produce devastation on inhabited worlds. On Belsavis, the danger involves releasing the destruction contained within the prison planet. On Voss, the pursuit reaches Sith activity tied to the planet's dangerous spiritual sites. These missions make Scourge's warning tangible. The Knight spends this part of the campaign interrupting preparations, pursuing agents, and preventing deaths that would help bring the Emperor's intended ritual closer. Reaching the Emperor himself would accomplish little if his servants could complete their work elsewhere.

On Corellia, that pursuit returns to Tol Braga. The Jedi who organized the expedition against the Emperor has become one of the people advancing his plans. The Knight confronts him amid a battle already consuming the planet, where further destruction can be folded into an existing war. Braga's fall gives the earlier defeat a continuing cost. The Emperor has taken a Jedi's experience and determination and directed them against the people he meant to protect. The player can shape the encounter's resolution, including whether to attempt to bring Braga back.

Inside the Empire, another struggle concerns who can speak in the Emperor's name. Darth Baras claims to be the Emperor's Voice, a position that would place his pronouncements above ordinary Sith disputes. The Voice is an actual vessel through which the Emperor acts, so the claim has consequences for everyone expected to obey it. The Sith Warrior, formerly Baras's apprentice, survives Baras's attempt to dispose of them and is recruited by the Emperor's Hand. These servants appoint the Warrior as the new Wrath and direct that office against Baras.

The Warrior's journey to Voss reveals why Baras has room to make his claim. The Emperor's true Voice is trapped in the Dark Heart, caught through Baras's plotting and the influence of Sel-Makor. The Warrior must kill the trapped vessel to release the Emperor from that confinement. Here, service to the Emperor requires striking down the body he's using. The episode also explains how an ambitious Sith can obstruct an apparently supreme ruler: Baras exploits the Emperor's reliance on a vessel and keeps that vessel from leaving.

Baras still has to be confronted before the Dark Council. The Warrior returns with the authority of the Wrath and challenges the master who tried to have them killed. Their confrontation ends Baras's bid to rule through his claim to be the Voice, although the player determines whether he's killed or spared. The Council allows the contest to settle the matter. While Republic forces are fighting Imperial armies, Sith leaders are also dealing with a struggle in which orders issued under the Emperor's name may have come from a rival pursuing his own advancement.

The Sith Inquisitor reaches the same Council through a separate conflict with Darth Thanaton. After surviving Darth Zash's attempt to take over their body, the Inquisitor inherits enemies and obligations attached to Zash's position. Thanaton pursues the former slave who has risen through the Sith ranks, and their feud becomes a Kaggath, a contest fought with the resources of their respective power bases. On Corellia, that rivalry draws Imperial personnel and assets into a private Sith war while the wider campaign continues around them.

When Thanaton finally retreats to the Dark Council, the Inquisitor follows and defeats him there. Darth Mortis kills the fallen Thanaton, and the Inquisitor receives a Council seat. The Empire's ruling body can absorb the victor after a feud that has damaged its own war effort. That helps explain the atmosphere surrounding the Knight's advance on Dromund Kaas. The Emperor has servants committed to him, yet the Sith hierarchy beneath him is occupied with succession, retaliation, and competing claims to rank.

The Knight reaches the Dark Temple with T7-O1 and defeats the Emperor in their core-story confrontation. The immediate ritual threat is stopped. Later stories confirm that the Emperor survives this defeat, but that continuation lies beyond this encounter. For the people living through the original campaign, the consequence is substantial: the worlds targeted by his preparations remain alive, Scourge's break with his master has helped the Jedi reach him, and the Empire continues under Sith leaders who have survived their own internal wars.


The Emperor's defeat in the original class story doesn't remove him from the history of the Republic and the Sith. The later expansions of Star Wars: The Old Republic carry his existence into another imperial court, on Zakuul, where he rules as Valkorion. That revelation changes the scale of the older conflict. While the Republic and Sith Empire have been fighting each other, the being behind the Sith throne has also held power over a separate civilization. Zakuul has its own institutions, its own understanding of its ruler, and the military strength to force both older states into submission.

In Knights of the Fallen Empire, Zakuul enters the war through the Eternal Empire and its fleet. Its forces attack Republic and Sith alike. The familiar division between those two sides no longer explains who can command the galaxy's major powers. Each now faces an enemy capable of imposing terms on it. The Eternal Fleet gives Zakuul a reach that neither government can comfortably resist, and both are reduced to paying tribute. Their governments survive, but they have to conduct their affairs beneath the threat of another empire's intervention.

Valkorion's household also becomes part of galactic politics. Arcann, Thexan and Vaylin are his children with Senya, a former Knight of Zakuul. Thexan dies at Arcann's hands before the Outlander's struggle against the Eternal Empire takes shape. Arcann's anger toward his father and his place within this family become immediate concerns for everyone living under Zakuulan rule. Senya eventually joins the resistance against her own children. Her involvement gives the opposition someone who knows the imperial family, while leaving her to face the personal cost of fighting it.

The player character enters this upheaval as the Outlander. During the encounter with Valkorion, the Emperor's physical body is killed, and his presence takes up residence within the Outlander's mind. Arcann seizes the throne, while the Outlander is imprisoned in carbonite. This removes the player from public events while Zakuul consolidates its position. When Lana Beniko helps free the Outlander, the Republic and Sith Empire are already living with the consequences of defeat. Returning to the war means dealing with a different political situation from the one the player left behind.

Lana's rescue leads toward an Alliance based on Odessen. Its membership crosses boundaries that once determined nearly every military allegiance in the conflict. Republic soldiers, Sith, smugglers and specialists can work toward the same campaign against Arcann. Theron Shan and Lana are especially useful examples of that arrangement. Their backgrounds lie on opposing sides of the old war, yet both help build the organization around the Outlander. Odessen gives these recruits a common headquarters away from either capital, where cooperation can become part of daily military work.

The Alliance has to assemble the means to challenge Zakuul while the Eternal Fleet remains a threat. Its campaign therefore involves recruitment, intelligence and finding weaknesses that conventional Republic or Sith forces haven't overcome. The Gravestone, an ancient warship capable of fighting the Eternal Fleet, gives the resistance an unusual military asset. Its importance helps explain why the Alliance can grow beyond a gathering of fugitives. It has leadership, a base and a means of confronting the fleet that made Zakuul's demands enforceable across the older powers' territories.

Arcann's reign eventually gives way to Vaylin's, bringing another member of Valkorion's family onto the Eternal Throne. The succession doesn't settle the conflict with the Alliance. Vaylin continues the struggle, and the danger posed by the imperial household remains bound up with Valkorion's treatment of his children. Arcann's later place in events depends on the player's decisions, including whether he survives and has an opportunity to change. Across those different paths, the broad historical movement remains the same: the Alliance challenges Zakuul while its ruling family fractures around the throne.

Throughout this campaign, Valkorion speaks to the Outlander from within. He offers power and advice, and the player must continue acting while carrying the presence of the former Emperor. His apparent assistance belongs to a larger attempt to possess the Outlander's body. In Knights of the Eternal Throne, that attempt becomes the central confrontation. The fight reaches into the Outlander's mind, where Valkorion tries to make his domination complete. The Outlander defeats him there, with members of his family participating in the struggle against him.

Victory leaves the Alliance in possession of the Eternal Throne and the power associated with the fleet. For a time, Odessen is the center of an organization whose reach exceeds what its beginnings might have suggested. The Outlander can approach that position as a ruler or as a protector, according to player decisions. Either way, the Republic and Sith Empire still exist. Their officers and citizens haven't all transferred their loyalties to the Alliance, and cooperation against Zakuul hasn't erased the governments, military traditions or territorial interests that divided them.

The Alliance's command of the Eternal Fleet also fails to provide a lasting settlement. During the events surrounding Iokath and the later struggle involving Zildrog, the machinery behind its military advantage becomes vulnerable, and the fleet is destroyed. That loss changes what Odessen can demand and what it needs from potential allies. The Alliance continues, but it can no longer rely on overwhelming fleet strength to keep the older powers beneath it. The Republic and Sith Empire recover room to pursue their own campaigns.

Onslaught returns the Republic and Empire to open war. Fighting over places such as Onderon and Corellia again makes their competing military needs central to the story, with the Alliance drawn into those alignments. Later, Echoes of Oblivion brings another confrontation with an Emperor remnant threatening Satele Shan and her students. The struggle takes place within their connected minds and includes help from people drawn from the Emperor's past. His persistence reaches beyond the fall of Zakuul, even as the Republic-Sith conflict continues around the people confronting him.


Much later, in the age of the New Sith Wars, the man who will become Darth Bane works in the cortosis mines on Apatros before he ever enters a Sith academy. The mining company keeps its workers dependent through debt, and the war reaches them through visiting soldiers and the demand for valuable ore. His name is Dessel, and his route into the Sith begins with a killing and a need to escape. By this stage of the Republic's history, armies can draw recruits from people whose most immediate concern is getting away from the life they've already got.

A long stretch of Star Wars Legends history separates Dessel's lifetime from the Republic and Empire of the earlier class stories. The New Sith Wars belong to a later age, with different leaders, different territories, and repeated changes in the fortunes of both sides. The centuries between weren't one uninterrupted campaign carried forward by the same Imperial government. Wars ended, Sith movements fractured, and new enemies emerged. When the fighting finally produces the Brotherhood of Darkness, the Republic has been dealing with recurring Sith challenges for generations.

The beginning of the New Sith Wars is associated with Darth Ruin, a former Jedi who gathers followers and establishes a new Sith movement. His break with the Jedi begins another period in which knowledge carried out of the Order helps create an enemy for it. Ruin's followers eventually turn against him, and his death leaves the conflict without its founding leader. Sith ambitions survive him. Across the wars that follow, different rulers try to gather enough strength to defeat the Republic while also defending their positions against other Sith.

That competition makes the later wars difficult to describe as a simple contest between two stable governments. Sith lords hold territory and command forces, but they also fight one another. A victory against the Republic doesn't settle who will inherit the conquered worlds or command the next offensive. The Republic, meanwhile, loses its reach across parts of the galaxy as prolonged warfare disrupts the connections between worlds. Keeping a government seated on Coruscant doesn't mean that every distant system can expect its fleets to arrive.

The Jedi become increasingly involved in military leadership during these struggles. Some take on the responsibilities of governing and defending territories, and Jedi commanders gather forces around their own leadership. For people living far from dependable Republic assistance, a Jedi able to organize a defense has immediate practical importance. That arrangement also changes the Order's daily work. Campaigns require commanders, recruits, and supplies, and Jedi who might otherwise have taught or mediated disputes spend their lives directing soldiers against Sith armies.

Lord Kaan attempts to overcome the divisions among the Sith by gathering them into the Brotherhood of Darkness. He brings rival leaders into an alliance that recognizes them as equals, with Kaan directing their common war effort. The arrangement gives Sith who have spent their lives competing for supremacy a place in the same military enterprise. Kaan's personal influence helps hold them together. His persuasion also draws on the Force, so the apparent agreement among his followers owes something to his ability to press upon their minds.

The Brotherhood trains new Sith at academies, including the one on Korriban where Dessel eventually arrives. Before that promotion, he serves as an ordinary soldier in the Brotherhood's forces. In the Gloom Walkers, his perception of danger and his effectiveness in combat help distinguish him from the recruits around him. The army gives him opportunities that the mines never offered, although it also expects obedience to officers whose decisions can get their soldiers killed. His abilities bring him to the attention of those who recognize his potential in the Force.

At Korriban, Bane studies under instructors who are preparing students to serve the Brotherhood. The academy includes practice with weapons, contests between apprentices, and instruction in the dark side. Bane's strength attracts attention, but his progress isn't steady. After killing another student during training, he struggles with guilt and loses confidence in his abilities. Sirak, a powerful rival at the academy, defeats him. For a time, the former miner who seemed unusually promising becomes someone the other students can dismiss.

Bane begins spending more time with the academy's archives, where the teachings of earlier Sith give him a way to question what Kaan's followers are building. The Brotherhood discourages the title Darth, treating it as a reminder of the rivalries that once divided the Sith. Bane sees value in the older insistence on individual supremacy. His disagreement develops through study and through his experience among the apprentices, where claims of fellowship coexist with humiliation, jealousy, and lethal competition. Kaan's arrangement starts to look to him like a settlement that conceals those ambitions.

Lord Hoth gathers the Army of Light to oppose the Brotherhood. His forces include Jedi who answer his call alongside the soldiers fighting with them, and the campaign places him under the strain of repeated losses and difficult decisions. His friendship with Lord Farfalla suffers as the commanders disagree over the conduct of the war. Both remain committed to defeating the Sith, yet that shared purpose doesn't spare them resentment. The Jedi army has its own exhausted leaders, and their disagreements affect how they work together in the field.

The campaigns converge on Ruusan, where Hoth and Kaan commit their forces to a succession of battles. The world becomes the focus of a struggle that draws Jedi away from other duties and Sith away from their separate holdings. Kaan needs the Brotherhood to maintain its unity under pressure. Hoth needs enough support to finish the campaign without losing the army he's assembled. Neither commander has an unlimited reserve of people who can replace the dead, and each battle leaves fewer experienced fighters available for the next one.

By the time Bane rejects the Brotherhood's teachings, both armies are concentrated around leaders carrying the burden of those losses. Kaan has brought competing Sith into a common force, and Hoth has gathered Jedi into a dedicated army. On Ruusan, those efforts have placed the opposing orders within reach of one another, committed to a battlefield neither leader is prepared to abandon.


Kaan gathers the Brotherhood inside a cavern on Ruusan, preparing a weapon that requires the Sith to join their strength in the Force. Outside, Lord Hoth's Army of Light has brought the campaign close to its end. Kaan still believes he can destroy the Jedi approaching him. Bane understands what the ritual will do to its users, and he's already arranged to be elsewhere when they complete it. The Brotherhood's remaining leaders are about to commit themselves to a weapon that will leave none of them alive.

In the Legends novels Darth Bane: Path of Destruction and Rule of Two, this ending grows out of Bane's rejection of Kaan's Brotherhood. Bane supplies Kaan with the knowledge needed to create the thought bomb, drawn from older Sith teachings. Kaan accepts it as a way out of military defeat. Bane intends the ritual to remove the Sith gathered under Kaan, whose shared leadership he considers a failure. Their disagreement has moved beyond the academy and its arguments. Bane is helping the Brotherhood destroy itself.

Before the final ritual, Bane has already demonstrated how dangerous their combined power can be. He leads the Sith in an attack through the Force that sets the forest ablaze. Maintaining it requires concentration, and the Brotherhood's leaders eventually break away to pursue the battle by more familiar means. To Bane, their inability to sustain the effort confirms his contempt for them. Kaan returns to a plan built around his assembled followers, even though those followers are frightened, exhausted, and increasingly close to defeat.

The thought bomb requires those same Sith to surrender themselves to a collective ritual. Its effects reach beyond the destruction of their bodies. The beings caught within it have their spirits trapped, leaving a lasting wound beneath Ruusan. Kaan approaches the ceremony with confidence in his own ability to direct it. Lord Qordis has already died at Bane's hands, and Githany, who recognizes the danger too late, cannot escape what Kaan has begun. The Brotherhood has gathered its surviving strength into a place from which there will be no recovery.

Hoth learns enough about the weapon to understand that confronting Kaan will cost him his life. He enters the cavern with Jedi who accept that danger, while Farfalla remains outside with the surviving forces. Hoth's decision brings his long pursuit of the Brotherhood to its end. When Kaan releases the thought bomb, the Jedi who have come to face him die along with the Sith. Farfalla survives to deal with the aftermath, but the commander around whom the Army of Light formed won't return from the cavern.

Bane survives because he stays beyond the weapon's reach. His survival gives him the opportunity to begin again without Kaan's followers, their ranks, or their claim to equality. He retains the title Darth and sets out the arrangement that will govern his new Sith line: a master who possesses power and an apprentice who seeks it. There will be no Brotherhood to recruit replacements or settle disputes. Bane expects the apprentice eventually to challenge the master, and he intends that struggle to strengthen the succession.

His reasoning comes from the rivalries he found among the Sith. Several weaker followers could cooperate to kill a stronger leader, then divide what remained among themselves. Bane believes that process allows Sith knowledge and ability to decline. By limiting the line to a master and an apprentice, he means to require a successor capable of overcoming the person who trained them. It's a demanding and dangerous design. The master has to teach someone who may become a lethal rival, while the apprentice has to survive long enough to learn.

On Ruusan, Bane finds the child who will become that apprentice. Zannah has been caught in a war brought to her world by armies far larger than anything she could resist. After Jedi kill her companion Laa, one of Ruusan's native bouncers, she lashes out through the Force and kills them. Bane recognizes the strength in her reaction. He takes her as his pupil, giving the new Sith line a future at the very place where the Brotherhood has ended.

Zannah's cousin Darovit complicates that departure. He wants to rescue her from Bane and tries to intervene, but Zannah maims his hand through the Force. Her action keeps Bane from killing him. Darovit remains alive on Ruusan, separated from the cousin he hoped to bring back. For Zannah, entering the Sith doesn't erase the people she knew before the war. It leaves her making a brutal decision about how to protect one of them while remaining beside the man who will train her.

Beyond Ruusan, the Republic begins reorganizing after the wars. Under Chancellor Tarsus Valorum, the Ruusan Reformation curtails the military arrangements that have grown around the conflict. The Republic steps away from the great standing forces of the war era, and the Jedi relinquish their military authority. Hoth's Army of Light belongs to the conflict that has just ended. Jedi service will continue within a Republic whose civilian government is reasserting its place, rather than through Jedi commanders maintaining armies and governing territories of their own.

Those changes answer problems the war has made visible. Jedi drawn into prolonged campaigns have become generals, and the demands of holding territory have carried them into political rule. Reform redirects the order toward its duties as peacekeepers under the Republic. Worlds emerging from the fighting can begin rebuilding without supplying another campaign against Kaan. With the Brotherhood destroyed, that seems an achievable settlement. Bane's survival, however, means the Sith danger has taken a form the Republic's victorious armies weren't organized to pursue.

Bane has no intention of immediately replacing Kaan's armies. He and Zannah will need secrecy, knowledge, and time to establish their succession. The Republic's recovery can proceed without a Sith fleet appearing over another member world, and the Jedi can return to responsibilities the war displaced. On Ruusan, Farfalla and the survivors are left with the dead and the damage of the final campaign. As the Republic rebuilds its institutions, Bane leaves the battlefield with Zannah, carrying the Sith teachings into the life of a master and a single apprentice.
