In the Jedi Temple on Coruscant, a group of younglings practices with training lightsabers while Yoda watches over them. Obi-Wan Kenobi comes looking for help with a missing planet, and their lesson makes room for his problem. These children live and learn in the same Temple that clone soldiers will later attack. Following the life of this building, we can begin to see how the Order's ordinary work, its responsibilities to the Republic, and its political decisions come together in the catastrophe that destroys its central home.

Before the attack, the Temple contains several parts of Jedi life within one institution. Children receive instruction there, Jedi consult the archives, and the Council considers requests and reports from across the Republic. A Jedi returning to Coruscant can bring a problem into a community with teachers, records, and experienced colleagues. Obi-Wan's visit to Yoda's class gives us a particularly clear example of that community at work.

The building itself places that community within the enormous city of Coruscant. Its towers rise above the surrounding structures, and inside are broad corridors, council chambers, and spaces for study. The films return to these interiors as Jedi arrive from assignments or prepare to leave on new ones. Coruscant is also the seat of the Republic's government. The Order's home stands on the same world as the Senate and the Chancellor, close to the officials whose decisions repeatedly enter Jedi discussions. That proximity makes visits and consultations possible throughout the prequel films.

The Council chamber gives those consultations a familiar setting. Jedi sit in a circle, with the city visible beyond them, and listen to accounts brought by members of the Order. In The Phantom Menace, Qui-Gon Jinn brings Anakin Skywalker before them after returning from Tatooine. The Council questions the boy and considers whether he should be trained. For Anakin, the Temple is a place where strangers can decide the course of his education. For Qui-Gon, it's where he must explain a conviction that his fellow Jedi don't share.

By Attack of the Clones, Obi-Wan and Anakin return to Coruscant as master and apprentice and receive an assignment to protect Senator Padme Amidala. Their work draws them into her attempted assassination and then into a larger investigation. The Temple remains a place where Obi-Wan can consult others as that investigation develops. He doesn't have to carry everything he knows alone. He can ask the archives for information, speak with Yoda, and report his discoveries to the Council. The films show an institution whose members travel widely while continuing to seek advice from home.

The archives give another part of that home a concrete form. Obi-Wan searches their records with Jocasta Nu's assistance, expecting to locate a world described to him elsewhere. Jocasta treats the collection as comprehensive and regards the absent entry as grounds for rejecting the planet's existence. Obi-Wan continues searching beyond her answer. Within the Temple, then, the same investigation encounters both confidence in an established record and a classroom willing to consider a different explanation. Those encounters help explain how Jedi work through problems before any lightsaber is drawn.

As the Clone Wars continue into Revenge of the Sith, the Council chamber also becomes a place for military reports and assignments. Jedi who once traveled as negotiators now discuss campaigns. Anakin brings news of the Chancellor's wishes into their deliberations, and the Council gives him responsibilities connected to Palpatine. The details of that conflict belong to the wartime story, but the change in the Temple's daily business is already visible. Decisions about battles and the Chancellor now occupy the room where the Council once questioned a newly arrived child.

Anakin's appointment to the Council makes those overlapping duties personal. Palpatine wants him there as his representative. The Council accepts his appointment while withholding the rank of master, and Anakin reacts with anger. He takes a seat among Jedi who've helped direct his life, carrying expectations from the Chancellor into their circle. The Temple hasn't ceased to be his home or the center of his vocation. Yet returning to it now brings a dispute over his standing and demands that pull against his closeness to Palpatine.

Elsewhere in the same film, Yoda receives Anakin in private when he seeks advice about fearing someone's death. Anakin doesn't identify the person whose loss he dreads. Yoda responds within the Jedi teachings available to him, while Anakin holds back the circumstances that make the fear so immediate. Even during the war, the Temple still has room for this kind of conversation. A troubled Jedi can approach an elder away from a Council meeting. The help offered depends, though, on what Anakin is willing to tell him.

By the time soldiers enter the Temple, the building contains children, teachers, and Jedi whose work has sustained the Republic. The assault reaches into all those activities. The younglings' earlier lesson helps give that loss a familiar shape: children practicing, a teacher listening, a returning Jedi asking for help. Before its fall, the Temple is where those people can find one another, and where the Order gathers the experience it carries home from the wider galaxy.


Luke can't see the training remote through the helmet Obi-Wan has asked him to wear. Aboard the Millennium Falcon in A New Hope, he has to respond to its attacks without watching it. Han Solo treats the exercise with skepticism. Luke has barely begun to learn what a Jedi does, and the lesson asks him to set aside the sense he's accustomed to relying on. Obi-Wan is introducing a way of perceiving the world that will shape how Luke acts within it.

Earlier, in Obi-Wan's home on Tatooine, Luke hears the Force described as an energy field created by living things, surrounding them and binding the galaxy together. This gives Jedi abilities a setting much larger than a duel. Luke has grown up handling machinery and working on a moisture farm. Obi-Wan introduces him to something he can't examine like an engine, yet which connects him to the life around him. Learning to become a Jedi begins with recognizing that connection.

Obi-Wan also tells Luke about his father and gives him the lightsaber that belonged to him. The weapon makes the conversation immediate, but Luke can't become a Jedi merely by taking possession of it. On the Falcon, he initially struggles against a small training device. When he follows Obi-Wan's guidance and stops depending on his eyes, he begins to intercept its attacks. His first progress comes through attention and receptiveness. The exercise gives him a modest experience of what Obi-Wan has been describing, something he can begin to recognize for himself.

On Dagobah in The Empire Strikes Back, Yoda starts addressing those difficulties before Luke recognizes him as his teacher. Luke is impatient with the unfamiliar creature who interrupts his search. He wants to find a great warrior and proceed with his training. Yoda has already had an opportunity to observe how he behaves when an encounter seems inconvenient. When Yoda questions Luke's readiness, his concerns include anger and impatience. Luke's desire to become useful is genuine, but his expectations leave him poorly prepared to listen to someone who doesn't look important.

During the training that follows, Yoda directs Luke toward the life of the swamp. The Force is present around them, between living beings and throughout their surroundings. Luke's instruction takes place among trees and water, far from the Temple's classrooms. He runs with Yoda on his back and practices moving objects through the Force. Physical effort and concentration belong to the same education. Yoda wants him to notice what surrounds him while becoming less distracted by his own frustration, a demanding task for someone eager to measure his progress.

The sunken X-wing makes that frustration visible. Luke can move smaller objects, but he regards the ship as an entirely different problem. Yoda challenges the importance Luke gives to its size. Luke attempts to raise it, then gives up when he can't complete the task. Yoda lifts the fighter out of the swamp himself. Luke's disbelief becomes part of their exchange afterward. He has accepted some of what he's been taught, while still carrying assumptions about what should be possible. Those assumptions affect what he's willing to attempt and how soon he stops.

Yoda's teaching also places limits on what a Jedi should seek from the Force. When Luke asks about the dark side, Yoda describes it as easier and more seductive, while rejecting Luke's suggestion that it's stronger. He associates that danger with fear, anger, and aggression. Alongside this warning, he teaches Luke to use the Force for understanding and defense. These instructions give restraint a practical meaning. A frightened Jedi may want a quick means of overcoming an enemy, and Yoda wants Luke to recognize the danger in letting that desire direct him.

Luke's visit to the cave brings the warning into his training. Yoda tells him he won't need his weapons, but Luke takes them anyway. Inside, he encounters the appearance of Darth Vader and strikes off its head. The face revealed beneath the mask is Luke's own. The experience connects the enemy he fears with a danger within himself. Luke entered armed despite his teacher's advice, and his response to the apparition was to fight. For a pupil who wants to confront Vader, the image gives Yoda's warnings an uncomfortable, personal form.

His concern for Han and Leia tests him differently. During his training, Luke sees them suffering at Cloud City and wants to leave Dagobah to help. Yoda cautions him that the future is difficult to perceive, and Obi-Wan urges him to continue preparing. Luke won't accept the risk of leaving his friends without help. His departure exposes a difficulty within the Jedi vocation: caring about other people doesn't make the right action easy to identify. His teachers fear what will happen if he faces Vader unprepared. Luke fears what will happen if he stays.

At Cloud City, Luke discovers how badly he's underestimated the encounter. Vader defeats him, and Luke eventually needs rescue himself. Hanging beneath the city, he reaches out to Leia through the Force, and she has the Falcon return for him. The connection Obi-Wan described now has a very immediate place in Luke's life. He's injured, unable to get himself to safety, and dependent on others answering his need. Through these lessons, Jedi service takes a practical shape: perceive the people around you, prepare to protect them, and learn to recognize when fear is pushing you beyond your judgment.


On Ahch-To, the first Jedi Temple survives on an island reached by sea and steep stone steps. When Rey arrives, Luke Skywalker is living among its old buildings, far from the Republic the Jedi once served. The place gives the Order's distant beginnings a physical location. There's a temple to enter, a space for meditation, and a collection of books preserved in an ancient tree. Yet Rey finds no community of apprentices there. The Jedi she's crossed the galaxy to find has withdrawn from teaching.

The island changes the scale of the story. Coruscant's Temple belongs to a crowded capital, where Jedi receive assignments and deliberate within reach of the Senate. Ahch-To has porgs nesting among its structures and sea creatures living along its shores. Luke fishes and gathers milk as part of his daily routine. Reaching him means following him across the island while he goes about these tasks. During Luke's retreat, the ancient site is far removed from the machinery of galactic government.

Even here, Luke isn't the only resident. The Caretakers tend the island's buildings and maintain its spaces. Rey's arrival disrupts their work, especially when her lightsaber practice sends a rock crashing down onto their cart. That small encounter gives the ancient site an ordinary present. Someone still has to look after the structures while visitors wrestle with the fate of the Jedi. The Caretakers' presence also keeps Ahch-To from feeling like an abandoned monument that began to matter only when Luke chose to settle there.

Inside the temple, a mosaic depicts a seated figure with light and dark arranged around it. It's a very different image from the Council chamber of the prequel era, with its circle of chairs and windows overlooking Coruscant. Here the surviving image centers on meditation. Taken together with the temple's location, it suggests an early tradition concerned with contemplation and the Force. The film doesn't turn that figure into a named founder whose entire life we can follow. Ahch-To gives the beginning a setting without supplying a complete founding biography.

The ancient Jedi texts provide another kind of connection to that beginning. They're physical books, kept within the hollow of a great tree. Rey is drawn to the place where they're stored, and Luke identifies them with the Jedi tradition. Their survival matters because the Order has taught through more than personal instruction. It has also preserved teachings that another generation can return to. On Coruscant, that work takes the form of extensive archives. On Ahch-To, Rey encounters a small collection whose age reaches beyond the institution she knows through stories.

Those books don't offer Rey an immediate solution to the crisis that brought her there. She needs Luke to return and help against the First Order, while Luke regards the Jedi's history through his own disappointment. During their conversations, he points to the Order's failure to prevent Darth Sidious from rising and destroying it. That's Luke's judgment, shaped by the losses he's carrying. Standing near the first temple hasn't made him eager to begin again. He has come to an ancient Jedi place while intending to let the Jedi end with him.

His decision makes the island an unusual retreat. Luke has sought out a location closely connected to the tradition he's rejecting. That may suggest he still needs some relationship with its beginnings, even while refusing its future. In practical terms, though, he has cut himself off from the Force and resists Rey's request for instruction. The temple's presence can't make that decision for him. Rey has to persuade a living teacher to speak, despite having already reached a place filled with the remains of teaching.

When Luke finally gives Rey a lesson, the island supplies the example. He asks her to reach outward through the Force, and she becomes aware of life around her, of death and decay, and of new life growing. The experience includes the land and sea beyond the temple walls. It's easy to understand why a place like Ahch-To could support a contemplative tradition. A pupil can sit near the old stones and attend to things that continue without the Jedi's involvement. Luke uses those surroundings to challenge Rey's assumption that the Force belongs to the Order.

The same island also contains a place that draws Rey toward darkness. Beneath it, she enters a cavern and encounters a succession of reflected images of herself. She wants an answer about her parents, but the experience leaves her facing her own reflection. Ahch-To's connection to the Jedi doesn't make every part of it reassuring or easy to interpret. Rey's experience there brings her private longing into the middle of her search for instruction. Even at the first temple, her questions remain personal, and Luke's account of Jedi history can't answer them all.

Later, Luke approaches the tree with the intention of burning it. Yoda appears, and their exchange returns the distant tradition to a relationship between a teacher and his former pupil. Before Luke can carry out his intention, Yoda calls down lightning that sets the tree ablaze. Luke reacts with alarm, particularly at the apparent loss of the texts. His response exposes how much he still cares about what the tree contains. He has spoken of ending the Jedi, yet watching an ancient part of their inheritance burn is painful for him.

Yoda directs Luke's attention toward teaching, including the failure a teacher might wish to hide. The conversation takes place beside burning wood rather than before a council or a class. Ahch-To has brought Luke back into the position of a pupil who needs help understanding what he should pass on. The books, meanwhile, are safe aboard the Millennium Falcon, where Rey has taken them. The oldest Jedi place remains on its island, while its preserved teachings leave with someone who still wants to learn.


The Legacy Run is traveling through hyperspace when its journey ends in a disaster that reaches far beyond the ship itself. In Light of the Jedi, the vessel breaks apart, and fragments emerge into ordinary space at terrible speeds. The people of the Hetzal system have little time to understand what's approaching them. Some of the incoming wreckage carries passengers who are still alive. Other pieces threaten inhabited worlds. A route that usually connects distant communities has brought an emergency that no planetary rescue service can manage alone.

Hyperspace travel makes the Republic's expansion possible. A settlement can be distant from Coruscant and still receive supplies, exchange goods, and welcome travelers, provided ships can reach it reliably. That connection matters especially in the Outer Rim, where the Republic is extending its presence. The Legacy Run belongs to this ordinary movement of people across the galaxy. Its destruction interrupts journeys that began with the expectation of arriving somewhere else, and the fragments carry that interrupted civilian life into the path of whole populations.

Chancellor Lina Soh's Great Works include Starlight Beacon, a station intended to help connect and support these farther regions. Its location gives travelers and settlements a Republic presence closer than the capital. Communications, medical assistance, and Jedi service are all part of its purpose. The station is approaching its dedication when the disaster strikes. The emergency gives immediate weight to the reasons for building it. The frontier needs somewhere that can receive a call for help and send assistance across the surrounding systems.

The Jedi already work beyond their Temple on Coruscant. Outposts allow them to live closer to communities they serve, with smaller groups taking responsibility for places far from the Council. Elphrona, where Loden Greatstorm trains Bell Zettifar, is one such posting in the novel. A master and apprentice can continue their teaching relationship while responding to local needs. That arrangement gives the Order a presence people can encounter without traveling to the capital, and it gives pupils experience among the people their training is meant to help.

At Hetzal, the scale is quite different. Avar Kriss and other Jedi join a rescue effort that includes Republic personnel, local authorities, and civilian pilots. Warnings have to reach people on the ground while ships attempt evacuations and rescuers work out which fragments can be intercepted. Every action depends on information arriving quickly enough to matter. A pilot needs somewhere to take survivors. A rescue crew needs to know where survivors are. Jedi abilities help with these problems, though the response still requires vessels, communications, and people willing to fly into danger.

Avar experiences the Force as music. Other living beings have distinct presences within that experience, and she can bring Jedi together through it. During the rescue, this allows separated participants to act with an awareness of one another that ordinary messages couldn't provide. They're working across a vast emergency, often unable to see what another group is facing. Through Avar, their efforts can be coordinated. Her particular gift becomes useful because so many other Jedi are present and prepared to respond when she reaches for them.

The novel gives that shared effort a personal scale through Burryaga, a Wookiee Padawan whose sensitivity to emotion helps him recognize living people amid the wreckage. From a distance, a fragment can look like another dangerous object to divert or destroy. The presence of frightened survivors changes what rescuers must do with it. Burryaga's perception helps direct attention toward people who might otherwise be lost. A pupil's contribution matters here, even among experienced Jedi, because finding someone who needs help is an essential part of saving them.

Meanwhile, the danger to Hetzal grows as the rescuers identify a shipment of liquid tibanna among the fragments. Its course toward the system's sun threatens a catastrophe far beyond a single impact on a settled world. The rescue now includes the need to prevent that collision while evacuations and other interceptions continue. There's no opportunity to put every other task aside and concentrate on one object. People are still scattered through the wreckage, and the system's inhabitants remain exposed while the Jedi attempt something that demands an extraordinary combined effort.

Avar reaches outward through the Force, joining the strength of Jedi who answer her. Together they work to alter the tibanna's course. The action grows out of the coordination already taking place throughout the rescue, with each participant contributing to an effort too great for any of them alone. It also places a heavy burden on those involved. The Jedi can attempt this intervention because they've learned to listen and respond to one another, and because Avar can sustain a connection across the distances separating them.

Alongside that effort, pilots such as Joss and Pikka Adren help make the wider rescue possible. Their work keeps the emergency grounded in the practical business of reaching endangered people and carrying them away. The Jedi aren't the only people accepting risk. Civilian vessels and their crews become part of a response that the Republic couldn't conduct through official resources alone. For those aboard a damaged fragment or waiting for evacuation, the ship that reaches them matters as much as the decisions being made elsewhere in the system.

The disaster also leaves consequences outside Hetzal. More wreckage emerges elsewhere, and uncertainty about further arrivals disrupts travel. Authorities restrict hyperspace movement while specialists try to predict where the fragments will appear. The same network that allows aid to cross the Republic now has to be treated with caution. A distant settlement may escape the wreckage and still lose the dependable passage on which its supplies and journeys rely. This helps explain why Soh's projects place such emphasis on connection: distance becomes much harder to live with when familiar routes can no longer be used safely.

When Starlight Beacon is dedicated, Avar becomes its marshal, giving her an ongoing responsibility in the region where the Order is extending its service. The station provides a place to gather resources and send Jedi outward, while smaller outposts keep teachers and apprentices near particular communities. Hetzal has shown what that presence can ask of them. Avar must coordinate across distances, Burryaga must attend to lives others haven't detected, and rescuers must keep finding ways to reach people. Starlight gives that work a permanent base farther from Coruscant.


The Nihil can take routes through hyperspace that other ships can't follow. They call these routes Paths, and their raids depend on arriving where ordinary navigation gives defenders little reason to expect them. In Light of the Jedi, this makes a scattered band of raiders dangerous across an enormous region. A patrol can guard a familiar approach and still fail to intercept the ships that matter. The Republic's effort to connect distant settlements runs into an enemy whose movements don't fit the network it's trying to protect.

Marchion Ro holds the means of supplying those Paths. Mari San Tekka, an elderly woman kept aboard his ship, can perceive extraordinary routes through hyperspace. He exploits that ability and distributes access to the Nihil. Within the organization, his position as the Eye gives him a role distinct from the leaders who command its raiding forces. Those leaders have ships and followers, but their freedom of movement depends on something Marchion provides. Their apparent independence leaves him with a powerful way to shape what they can attempt.

The destruction of the Legacy Run brings that hidden advantage into civilian life. The transport encounters a Nihil ship in hyperspace and breaks apart while trying to avoid it. Its fragments emerge into inhabited systems, carrying the emergency far beyond the original encounter. The Jedi face wreckage, endangered passengers and threatened worlds before they have a clear account of the people responsible. For the Nihil, unusual access to hyperspace makes an encounter possible that conventional travelers haven't prepared for. Everyone along the fragments' paths has to live with the consequences.

The Nihil also exploit the emergency through raids and demands for payment. A settlement frightened by approaching destruction is vulnerable to anyone claiming they can provide safety. Jedi and Republic personnel have to divide their attention between immediate rescue and finding the attackers. That division matters because the Nihil don't need to defeat every defender in a direct engagement. Each evacuation, damaged ship and endangered community creates work that the defenders can't responsibly abandon. Even a successful rescue can leave the people conducting it with another crisis already underway.

Loden Greatstorm encounters this danger personally. During the fighting on Elphrona in Light of the Jedi, he saves his apprentice, Bell Zettifar, but is captured by the Nihil. Bell survives without knowing the full circumstances of his master's imprisonment. Marchion keeps Loden alive and subjects him to captivity, turning a Jedi who'd been helping people into a prisoner beyond the reach of his companions. For Bell, the loss interrupts the ordinary continuity of apprenticeship. The person who guided his training is missing, while the work expected of the Jedi continues around him.

In The Rising Storm, the Republic Fair on Valo gives the Nihil a different kind of target. Chancellor Lina Soh intends the gathering to bring worlds together and display what cooperation can accomplish. Visitors and representatives assemble around that promise. The Nihil attack the fair, placing civilians and the Chancellor in danger and forcing the Jedi present into a sprawling rescue effort. Stellan Gios, Elzar Mann and Bell face an assault whose consequences spread through the gathering. Protecting a public celebration requires them to respond wherever people are trapped or under attack.

Valo makes the cost of disruption especially clear through Elzar. Faced with the destruction around him, he draws on the dark side during the battle and is disturbed by what he's done. The emergency brings his fear and anger into the way he uses the Force. Stellan, meanwhile, carries the responsibility of a Jedi closely associated with the Republic's public presence. The fair's destruction reaches beyond buildings and vessels. It places individual Jedi under pressures that follow them after the immediate fighting ends, even when they've managed to save lives.

Marchion's plans also reach into a vulnerability that ships and security arrangements can't address. He brings a Nameless creature against the Jedi. These beings affect Force users with overwhelming dread and distorted perception, making their familiar awareness of the Force frightening and unreliable. A Jedi who normally senses nearby life may struggle to understand what's happening in the creature's presence. Training that helps during a firefight offers little preparation for this experience. Marchion has acquired a weapon that attacks the capacities his opponents usually depend on when other means fail.

Loden's fate reveals the danger in The Rising Storm. After his imprisonment, his reunion with Bell offers a brief return of the bond the Nihil interrupted. Then the Nameless kills Loden, leaving his body calcified and breaking apart. Bell has found his master only to lose him again in circumstances he can scarcely comprehend. The creature makes the encounter difficult to perceive as well as difficult to survive. Bell's experience gives the threat a particular human cost: the rescue he wanted doesn't restore the life and teaching he'd expected to share with Loden.

In The Fallen Star, that danger enters Starlight Beacon. The station is already a refuge and a working center for Jedi service when sabotage puts it in peril. Damage compromises the station, and the people aboard have to organize evacuations while trying to prevent further catastrophe. Nameless creatures aboard the Beacon interfere with the Jedi attempting that work. Stellan, Bell and their companions can't approach the emergency with their usual confidence in what they sense. They must help passengers through a failing station while facing a threat that can leave a Jedi terrified, disoriented or dead.

Starlight brings these methods together in a single place. Sabotage damages the structure, the station's danger creates an urgent need for rescue, and the Nameless weaken the rescuers who would ordinarily be among its greatest resources. Marchion's attack exploits the Beacon's purpose: people have gathered there because it offers help, and the Jedi remain because those people need them. As the station falls toward Eiram, Stellan stays aboard to help avert worse destruction. Evacuations save people, but Starlight is lost. Bell survives carrying the loss of Loden into another disaster, alongside others whose work must continue without the station that once supported them.


Sol sees Osha beneath a tree on Brendok before he knows her name. She's a child living beyond the settlement he and his fellow Jedi have been studying, and he follows her back to a fortress occupied by a coven of witches. Inside, Osha and her twin sister, Mae, are preparing for a ceremony. Sol's concern for the children grows out of what he observes there, though he doesn't understand the community he's entered. That gap between his concern and his understanding follows him through everything that happens on Brendok.

The Jedi haven't come to the planet looking for children. Indara, Sol, Kelnacca and Indara's Padawan, Torbin, are investigating how life returned to a world devastated by a hyperspace disaster. They suspect a vergence, an unusual concentration of the Force, may explain the growth around them. Their work involves searching the terrain and collecting samples. Torbin is weary of the assignment and wants to return to Coruscant. When Sol finds the twins, the investigation acquires a possibility that draws each Jedi toward a different response.

Mother Aniseya leads the coven, while Mother Koril is especially determined to keep the Jedi from taking the girls. The witches have their own practices and their own understanding of the power they use. During the Jedi's first confrontation with them, Aniseya enters Torbin's mind and addresses his desire to go home. He can't keep her out. The encounter gives the visitors a direct experience of the coven's abilities, while the witches face armed outsiders who have entered their home and begun asking about their children.

Osha wants to leave. She's drawn to the Jedi and to a life beyond the coven, while Mae wants her sister to remain with her. Aniseya allows the girls to be tested, and their responses reveal how differently they regard the opportunity. Osha eventually admits that she wants to become a Jedi. For Sol, her wish strengthens his conviction that he should help her. For Aniseya, it creates a painful decision about letting a daughter depart. Mae sees the same possibility as the loss of the person closest to her.

At their camp, the Jedi receive instructions from the Council to leave the coven alone. Indara accepts that direction. She doesn't share Sol's certainty that taking Osha away is the right course, and she challenges how quickly he's formed an attachment to the child. The blood samples, however, reveal something extraordinary about the twins: their symbionts are identical in a way the Jedi consider impossible for two separate people. Torbin treats the discovery as a way to complete the assignment and return home. He heads back toward the fortress, and Sol follows.

Their return brings the fears inside the coven into a confrontation. Mae has already tried to prevent Osha from leaving, and a fire begins after she burns Osha's book. As the danger spreads, the Jedi and witches face one another again. Aniseya begins transforming into a dark, smoke-like form, with Mae also affected. Sol responds by driving his lightsaber into Aniseya. As she dies, she tells him she intended to allow Osha to leave. 

The violence widens after Aniseya's death. Koril fights, and the witches take possession of Kelnacca, turning him against Sol and Torbin. The Wookiee Jedi attacks his companions with a force they struggle to withstand. When Indara arrives, she breaks the witches' hold on him. The women linked to that possession collapse and die. Each attempt to deal with the immediate danger has now added to the deaths inside the fortress, and the children are still somewhere within a building being consumed by fire and structural failure.

Sol reaches the twins as they stand on opposite sides of a collapsing walkway. He tries to hold the structure through the Force, but he can't sustain both sides. He saves Osha, and Mae falls out of sight. Afterward, Indara insists they conceal what happened, arguing that a full confession would also destroy Osha's chance to enter the Order. The account Osha receives places responsibility on Mae's fire. She leaves Brendok believing her sister caused the catastrophe, while Sol becomes her teacher without telling her that he killed Aniseya.

Mae survives with a different understanding. She knows Sol killed their mother, and she grows up seeking the Jedi who were present. Osha spends years training within the institution that took her in, then leaves without becoming a Jedi. Neither sister knows the other is alive. Their separation gives Sol's silence a continuing consequence: Osha's relationship with him develops around an account of her childhood that he knows is false. His care for her afterward can't repair something she hasn't been allowed to understand.

When Mae's attacks bring the surviving Brendok Jedi back into the same investigation, their responses expose how differently they've lived with that night. Torbin has withdrawn into the Barash Vow and accepts poison from Mae after apologizing for what happened. Kelnacca lives apart on Khofar. Sol continues trying to reach Osha while withholding the part of her past that would change how she sees him. Indara's death begins the investigation, but the search for her killer gradually uncovers the earlier actions of the Jedi being targeted.

Back on Brendok, Osha hears Sol admit to killing her mother. She kills him by constricting his throat through the Force, and the crystal in the lightsaber she holds turns red. The concealed account has finally broken apart between them. Vernestra Rwoh later presents Sol to the Senate tribunal as the Jedi responsible for the murders, while withholding her discovery of her former apprentice's involvement. The Council's earlier instruction had been to leave the coven alone. Yet the failure of the Jedi on Brendok has reached the Order's public record, where another incomplete explanation now takes the place of the one Osha was given as a child.


Dooku finds a village suffering under the senator who's supposed to represent it. In Justice, he and his apprentice, Qui-Gon Jinn, arrive to secure the release of that senator's kidnapped son. The assignment places them between a frightened captive and villagers who've resorted to kidnapping because ordinary appeals have failed. Dooku listens to their complaints. Their surroundings give those complaints weight: the people are struggling while their representative enjoys the benefits of office. Before he meets the senator, Dooku has already begun questioning whose interests the Jedi have been sent to protect.

Qui-Gon approaches the captive as someone who can still help resolve the dispute. The senator's son hears what the villagers have endured, and he doesn't respond with his father's contempt. That difference matters when the senator arrives with armed forces. His response to the kidnapping threatens to turn the village's desperation into a massacre. Dooku stands against him, refusing to treat his office as permission to mistreat the people around him. The mission has become a direct confrontation with a Republic official whose authority the Jedi would usually recognize.

Dooku's anger then carries him beyond defending the villagers. He catches the senator by the throat through the Force and begins choking him. Qui-Gon intervenes by bringing the senator's son into the confrontation, giving the father a reason to stop and drawing his master's attention back to the people at risk. Neither has restored the Order they served. They have changed a dangerous signal, confronted the Sith, and placed two children in homes beyond the ruined Temple. Their work now continues at that smaller scale, with Leia held by her new parents and Luke in Beru's arms beside Owen.


Cal Kestis is working as a scrapper on Bracca when he reaches out through the Force to save his friend Prauf from a fall. He's spent years concealing the abilities he learned as a Jedi Padawan. A single rescue exposes him, and the Empire sends Inquisitors to investigate. In Jedi: Fallen Order, the hunt begins among workers dismantling the remains of the war. Cal has found employment and kept his lightsaber hidden, but helping someone in immediate danger brings the life he's concealed back into view.

The Second Sister and Ninth Sister arrive with Imperial troops. They gather the workers, and Prauf speaks against the Empire before the Second Sister kills him. Cal draws his lightsaber and reveals himself. The confrontation leaves him fleeing through the scrapyard, pursued by soldiers and a hunter trained to fight someone like him. His old teacher can't come to his aid. Jaro Tapal died protecting him during the clone attack, and Cal has carried both that loss and an interrupted education into hiding.

The Inquisitors make that unfinished education especially dangerous. They wield lightsabers, use the Force, and answer to Darth Vader. Their work includes finding surviving Jedi and pursuing children whose abilities might bring them to Imperial attention. Cal can escape ordinary soldiers and still encounter someone who understands the skills he's trying to conceal. The Second Sister also speaks to the fear surrounding his survival. She knows that a fugitive Jedi may be isolated, ashamed, and reluctant to endanger anyone who helps him.

Cere Junda and the pilot Greez Dritus rescue Cal aboard the Stinger Mantis. Cere has been seeking a way to rebuild the Jedi Order, and Cal's arrival gives her a possible partner. She directs him to Bogano, where the late Jedi Master Eno Cordova left a route toward a hidden holocron. The device contains information about Force-sensitive children. For Cere, those children could become the beginning of a new teaching community. Reaching them would allow the Jedi's knowledge to pass to pupils who haven't yet been found by the Empire.

Cordova's preparations take Cal well beyond a single hiding place. With the droid BD-1, he follows recordings and explores ancient Zeffo sites to gain access to the vault on Bogano. The journey draws him into places already disturbed by Imperial occupation and excavation. On Kashyyyk, his search intersects with the struggle of the Wookiees and brings him into contact with Mari Kosan and the Wookiee leader Tarfful. Even a mission intended to recover a teaching legacy requires help from people facing their own immediate dangers under Imperial rule.

Cere's history explains why she hasn't approached Cal as a confident master with an intact school to offer. She survived capture and torture, and under that pressure she revealed where her Padawan, Trilla Suduri, and a group of younglings were hiding. Trilla was captured and became the Second Sister. When Cal learns who has been pursuing him, the hunt becomes tied to the woman who rescued him. Cere's former apprentice is now using her Jedi training in the service of the power that broke their community apart.

That history gives the holocron a danger Cal can't solve by finding a secure building. The names would help Cere locate children, but the same information would give the Empire a route to them. On Bogano, Cal experiences a vision in which he teaches young pupils, Imperial forces discover them, and the children are captured. The vision also shows Cal becoming an Inquisitor. It's a possible future that frightens him, rather than a fixed account of what must happen. It brings the children themselves into a mission previously guided by Cordova's recordings and Cere's hope.

The Second Sister takes the holocron, and Cal and Cere follow her to the Fortress Inquisitorius on Nur. Recovering it now means entering the institution that imprisons and turns Jedi survivors against one another. Cere takes an active role in the rescue, while Cal confronts Trilla. After her defeat, Cere apologizes to her former apprentice and accepts responsibility for failing her. Trilla has a brief moment in which that apology can reach her. Vader's arrival ends it. He kills her, then forces Cal and Cere into a desperate escape.

Back aboard the Mantis, the recovered holocron leaves Cal with the decision the journey has brought into focus. He destroys it. The children won't be gathered through Cordova's list, and the Empire won't be able to use that device to find them. Cere's wish to teach again has led them toward something precious, but Cal has seen what can follow when the hunters reach a teacher's pupils. He leaves those children's futures to the Force. His decision ends this attempt to rebuild through a collection of names.

The Obi-Wan Kenobi series shows another response to the same pursuit through the Hidden Path. On Mapuzo, Tala Durith helps people escape Imperial attention while serving within the Imperial military. A concealed refuge contains messages from those who've passed through it, including a mark Obi-Wan recognizes as Quinlan Vos's. Tala tells him that Quinlan has been helping young people reach safety. The refuge is a stopping place in an escape route, with people arranging passage and shelter while trying to keep their work concealed.

That work remains vulnerable to the Inquisitors. On Jabiim, Reva and Imperial troops reach a Path refuge where fugitives are waiting to leave. Their presence puts the people sheltering there in danger alongside Obi-Wan and Leia. Tala dies during the attack, and those escaping depend on others holding the troops back long enough for a departure. The Path gives hunted people practical help, though the series doesn't make it a common destination for every surviving Jedi. Cal and Cere's search has its own companions and circumstances.

For a surviving teacher, gathering pupils now carries consequences that reach beyond the lesson itself. Cere has to face an apprentice the Empire captured, while Cal gives up a list that might have helped him find new students. Tala and the Path keep people moving toward safety without a public Temple to receive them. Teaching can continue only where someone survives long enough to offer it. On the Mantis, Cal leaves the holocron in pieces, with the children's names beyond the reach of anyone who might take it from him.


Ezra Bridger's first invitation to learn comes through a recording hidden inside Kanan Jarrus's Jedi holocron. Aboard the Ghost, Ezra encounters a message from Obi-Wan Kenobi warning surviving Jedi away from the Temple. Kanan has also left him a way to discover something about himself. Ezra can open the device because he's sensitive to the Force. For a boy accustomed to stealing supplies and escaping across Lothal's rooftops, the message introduces a scattered community he hadn't known he could belong to. Kanan offers to teach him, though his own education ended before he was ready.

The Ghost already has a community aboard it. Hera Syndulla pilots the ship and organizes missions, while Sabine Wren, Zeb Orrelios and Chopper bring their own skills and arguments into its cramped shared life. Ezra doesn't arrive at a school built around his needs. He joins people stealing Imperial supplies, helping fugitives and deciding how much danger they can accept for strangers. His training has to fit among those obligations. Hera gives him a place in the crew, and Kanan has to learn how to be responsible for someone who can follow him into danger.

One of Kanan's early difficulties is that Ezra already knows how to survive. On Lothal, the boy has relied on quick reactions, concealment and an instinct for escape. Kanan asks him to slow down enough to notice what those instincts have been drawing upon. In an exercise aboard the Phantom, Ezra tries to balance while reaching out through the Force. The lesson becomes tangled with frustration and interruptions. Kanan knows practices passed down by Jedi teachers, but recalling an exercise doesn't automatically make him good at explaining it to this particular pupil.

That uncertainty nearly persuades Kanan to hand the task to someone else. When the crew hears of a Jedi Master named Luminara Unduli imprisoned by the Empire, he hopes to rescue her and find Ezra a more qualified teacher. The prison contains a trap. Luminara is dead, and the Grand Inquisitor uses her remains to draw surviving Jedi into reach. After their escape, Kanan has to face why he'd wanted her there. Ezra, meanwhile, wants the teacher who has already taken responsibility for him. Their arrangement continues with Kanan's doubts acknowledged between them.

The dangers of their lessons are especially clear at Fort Anaxes. Surrounded by fyrnocks and confronted by the Grand Inquisitor, Ezra reaches through the Force to an enormous creature that attacks their pursuer. Fear and anger help drive what he does, and the effort leaves him unconscious. Kanan has seen his apprentice accomplish something powerful under pressure, with a cost that concerns him. Ezra needs help recognizing what happens inside him when survival seems to require every ability he possesses. Kanan can't provide that help through lightsaber drills alone.

The Jedi Temple on Lothal gives them somewhere to bring those concerns. Its entrance requires master and apprentice to work together, and inside Ezra faces visions involving the loss of Kanan and his companions. Yoda reaches him through the Force and asks him to consider why he wants to become a Jedi. Ezra's attachment to his new companions gives the question an immediate meaning. He wants to protect people he cares about. He emerges with a kyber crystal, which he later uses in a lightsaber whose design includes a blaster. His equipment reflects the fighting life he's already living.

Kanan's own development continues alongside Ezra's. During a later visit to the Lothal temple, he confronts a Temple Guard who challenges his ability to protect his apprentice. Kanan eventually accepts that he can't guarantee Ezra's safety or determine every part of his future. The guard knights him within the encounter. By then, Kanan has been teaching through Imperial pursuit and missions with the rebellion. The recognition comes after he's already struggled with the responsibilities involved. Ezra's education has required Kanan to examine fears that survived his own interrupted apprenticeship.

On Malachor, Ezra encounters another kind of instruction through Maul. Maul offers encouragement and presents himself as someone who understands the strength Ezra needs. He draws the boy toward a Sith holocron while exploiting the group's danger. During the confrontation that follows, Maul blinds Kanan. Afterward, Kanan withdraws, and Ezra continues turning to the holocron. Their separation leaves room for teachings Kanan would have opposed. When Kanan begins working with the mysterious Bendu on Atollon, he also begins finding his way back into his responsibilities, including the difficult work of reaching Ezra again.

Teaching aboard the Ghost extends beyond Ezra. When Sabine needs to learn how to wield the Darksaber, Kanan initially struggles to adapt his methods to someone who isn't a Jedi. Hera urges him to understand what Sabine is carrying. Sabine's resistance is tied to her family, her past at the Imperial Academy and the weapon she helped create for the Empire. Training brings those wounds into the open. Kanan listens as she speaks about them, and their practice changes. His experience with Ezra has brought him another pupil whose needs can't be answered by repeating an inherited exercise.

Kanan's last rescue places Ezra among the people who must live without him. At the Imperial fuel depot on Lothal, Kanan holds back an explosion so Hera, Ezra and Sabine can escape. He dies after pushing them clear. Later, inside the World Between Worlds, Ezra sees a passage through which he might reach Kanan at that moment. Ahsoka helps him recognize that pulling Kanan away would expose the others to the blast. Ezra has to leave his teacher's action intact, despite having found an extraordinary way to approach him again.

By the liberation of Lothal, Ezra can organize a plan that asks others to act without knowing every part of it. He confronts Thrawn aboard the Chimaera and brings the purrgil into the battle, accepting that he'll disappear along with the Imperial commander. The Ghost crew helps carry the operation through. Kanan's pupil has become someone they follow, and their shared work frees Ezra's home. No Council appoints him to that responsibility. He reaches it through the years spent learning with Kanan and living among people who gave those lessons somewhere to take effect.


Obi-Wan places Anakin Skywalker's lightsaber in Luke's hands on Tatooine before Luke knows what happened to its owner. In A New Hope, the weapon connects him to a father he's been told was a navigator on a spice freighter. Obi-Wan describes a Jedi Knight who fought beside him, then attributes Anakin's death to Darth Vader. Luke receives a family inheritance and an account of the Jedi together. The account leaves out the fact that Vader is his father, a silence that will shape his apprenticeship.

Luke doesn't enter a surviving school. His first teacher is an old man living beyond the farm where Owen and Beru have raised him. Obi-Wan explains the Force as an energy field created by living things, surrounding them and connecting the galaxy. For Luke, these ideas arrive alongside the possibility of leaving Tatooine. He initially refuses the journey to Alderaan because he has obligations at home. Only after discovering the destruction of the homestead and the deaths of his aunt and uncle does he commit to going.

Aboard the Millennium Falcon, Obi-Wan introduces Luke to perceiving through the Force with a training remote. Luke begins to respond without sight, but has little time to develop that experience before the Falcon reaches the Death Star.

Obi-Wan's death ends their time together before Luke has learned much about the Order itself. Luke sees Vader strike his teacher after Obi-Wan raises his blade and stops defending himself. Soon afterward, Obi-Wan's voice reaches him during his escape. At the Battle of Yavin, that voice urges him to rely on the Force. Luke turns off his targeting computer and fires the shot that destroys the station. His success gives the teaching enormous personal weight. He has acted on something his teacher can no longer explain face to face.

By The Empire Strikes Back, Luke has learned to draw his lightsaber to his hand with the Force, but he still needs instruction. Obi-Wan appears to him on Hoth and directs him to Yoda on Dagobah. Luke arrives expecting a great warrior. Instead, he grows irritated with the small stranger who interrupts his search, handles his belongings and questions his expectations. Yoda is examining the pupil before revealing himself. Luke's impatience is already part of the difficulty the teacher will have to address.

On Dagobah, Yoda challenges Luke's impatience and his assumptions about what the Force can do. Luke finally receives sustained instruction, although his education still depends on a single living teacher far from any school.

Yoda also directs him into the cave where Luke encounters a vision of Vader. Luke takes his weapons despite Yoda's advice, strikes the apparition and sees his own face inside the severed helmet. The experience places Luke's fear of Vader alongside the possibility of becoming like him. Later, visions of Han and Leia in pain pull his attention toward Bespin. Yoda warns that the future remains difficult to read. Luke nevertheless leaves before completing his training, convinced that his friends need him.

On Bespin, Vader defeats Luke and severs his hand. Luke's attempt to rescue his friends has brought him into a confrontation for which he isn't prepared. Then Vader tells him that he's his father and offers him a place at his side. Luke rejects the offer and falls away from him, eventually calling to Leia for help. The revelation leaves him with questions about Obi-Wan as well as Vader. His teacher's account of Anakin can no longer explain the man Luke has just faced.

When Luke returns to Dagobah in Return of the Jedi, Yoda is dying. He confirms that Vader is Luke's father and tells Luke that he must confront him. After Yoda's death, Obi-Wan explains his earlier account through the change Anakin underwent when he became Vader. Luke doesn't accept that his father is beyond recovery. He also learns that Leia is his sister. His inheritance now includes a family history his teachers withheld, and he has to decide how to face Vader with that knowledge.

Luke surrenders to Imperial forces on Endor because he believes he can reach his father. Speaking with Vader, he appeals to the person Anakin once was. Vader takes him to the Emperor, who has arranged for Luke to witness the danger facing the Rebel fleet. Sidious encourages his anger and invites him to attack. When Luke finally strikes toward the Emperor, Vader blocks the blow. Their duel places Luke between his wish to save his father and the rage Sidious is trying to cultivate.

Vader's discovery that Luke has a sister breaks Luke's restraint. When Vader threatens to turn Leia, Luke attacks with enough fury to drive him back and cut off his hand. Looking at the damaged machinery, Luke then looks at his own artificial hand. He stops. With Vader beaten and the Emperor urging him to finish the killing, Luke throws away his lightsaber and declares his allegiance to the Jedi. Sidious answers with Force lightning, and Luke is left calling to his father while Vader watches.

Anakin intervenes, lifting Sidious and casting him down the shaft, suffering fatal injuries as he saves Luke. Before dying, he asks to see his son without the mask. Luke's teachers gave him exercises, warnings and a path toward confronting Vader. Luke carried those teachings into the throne room while continuing to believe his father could respond to him. On Endor, after Anakin's funeral, Luke sees Obi-Wan and Yoda beside the restored figure of his father. Obi-Wan, Yoda, and Anakin now appear together through the Force, leaving Luke to carry their different legacies forward.


Luke enters Ben Solo's hut while his nephew is asleep. By Luke's later account, he's already sensed darkness during Ben's training, but what he finds that night frightens him more deeply. He looks into Ben's mind and sees the destruction of people and things he loves. For a brief moment, he ignites his lightsaber. The impulse passes, and shame follows. Ben wakes before Luke can explain. From the bed, he sees his teacher standing over him with a weapon drawn.

The Last Jedi approaches that encounter through accounts that change as Rey learns more. When Luke first describes the loss of his school, he says Ben turned against him, destroyed the Temple and took some of the students away. Luke places himself at the scene as someone who came to confront an apprentice whose darkness had grown. His own part remains partly concealed. Rey hears the story of a teacher who failed to recognize the danger soon enough, without yet hearing what Ben saw when he opened his eyes.

Before that night, the school had grown out of Luke's decision to teach a new generation. Leia entrusted Ben to him, hoping Luke could help her son. Ben was Luke's nephew, the child of Han Solo and Leia Organa, and the grandson of Anakin Skywalker. That family connection brought expectations into the training. Luke recalls believing his own reputation and assuming that his standing as a Jedi would help him succeed. He'd become a figure people looked toward, and he accepted responsibility for students whose futures he couldn't guarantee.

The Force Awakens first describes the school's collapse through the people left behind. Han tells Rey and Finn that Luke had been training a new generation when a pupil turned against him and destroyed what he'd built. Han says Luke blamed himself and walked away. At that point, Luke's absence is a problem stretching across the galaxy. Leia wants her brother found, while the First Order wants to prevent his return. The failed school has left no visible teaching community for Rey to approach.

For Han and Leia, Ben's fall also belongs to the history of their family. Leia believes Snoke drew their son toward the dark side, and she still hopes Han can reach him. Han approaches Ben on Starkiller Base with that hope, calling him back toward home. Ben kills him. By the time Rey reaches Luke, she's already seen how far Ben's actions have carried him from his parents. Her questions about Luke's school concern someone who has become dangerous, and someone whose family hasn't stopped trying to recover him.

Ben gives Rey a different account through their connection in the Force. He remembers waking to find Luke above him with an ignited lightsaber, and he understands the encounter as an attempt to murder him. In this version, Ben raises his own weapon to defend himself against his uncle. The detail changes Rey's understanding of the ruined school. Luke had allowed her to picture him confronting an apprentice who was already lost. Ben describes being threatened in his sleep by the person responsible for teaching him.

When Rey confronts Luke, he gives the fuller admission. He acknowledges the moment in which he considered killing Ben to prevent the suffering he'd foreseen. He also says the impulse vanished almost immediately. The film shows Ben responding with his lightsaber and bringing the hut down around them. There isn't a sustained duel in which Luke pursues his nephew. The disaster grows out of a brief, terrible action and Ben's response to it. Luke's regret arrives before he can repair what Ben has seen.

Luke's description of the vision matters because he reacts to suffering that hasn't yet happened. He believes he can see what Ben will bring about, and for an instant he treats that future as something he can stop with a blade. His earlier training had included warnings about the difficulty of reading the future. Here, the threatened people are those he loves, and that knowledge doesn't steady him quickly enough. The audience is given Luke's frightened experience of the vision, rather than an assurance that every part of it was inevitable.

When Luke emerges from the wreckage, his school is burning. In his account, Ben has vanished with a handful of students, while the others have been killed. The films leave the individual students largely unnamed, so the loss reaches us through Luke's account and the ruined training site. These were pupils he'd gathered and undertaken to teach. Their deaths and disappearance turn his private failure with Ben into the destruction of a community. Luke survives, but he no longer believes he should continue the work that brought them together.

On Ahch-To, Luke has withdrawn to the site of the first Jedi Temple. Rey arrives carrying his old lightsaber, expecting to find a teacher willing to return. He throws it away and refuses the role she offers him. His account of the Jedi's failures connects his own school to the earlier Order, whose members couldn't prevent Darth Sidious from rising. That history helps explain his refusal, though Rey challenges the conclusion he draws. She's facing an immediate struggle, and Luke's retreat leaves her seeking help from a man determined to withhold it.

When Yoda visits Luke on Ahch-To, he urges him to pass on what he has learned, including failure itself. Luke has treated Ben's fall as the end of his right to teach. Yoda asks him to consider what a student might learn from an honest account of where a teacher went wrong.

Luke doesn't rebuild his school before his death. On Crait, he faces Ben through a projection in the Force and apologizes for failing him, while giving the Resistance time to escape. Ben remains hostile, and their encounter doesn't restore the relationship broken at the hut. Luke's final act nevertheless helps people beyond the island where he'd withdrawn. His school has fallen, and its students can't be brought back. He returns to service carrying that loss, without claiming that he's repaired it.


While Kylo Ren concentrates on Luke outside the Resistance base on Crait, the people inside have time to find another way out. Poe recognizes that Luke's appearance has given them an opportunity, and the surviving Resistance members follow the crystal foxes through the tunnels. At the blocked exit, Rey lifts the fallen rocks with the Force. Luke's intervention and Rey's arrival meet in that escape. He holds the First Order's attention, and she opens the passage through which Leia and the others can leave.

The Millennium Falcon carries something else away with them. Aboard the ship are the ancient Jedi books Rey took from Ahch-To. Luke's withdrawal hasn't left those writings beyond anyone's reach, and the burning tree hasn't consumed them. They're now traveling with a young woman who still has questions about the Force and has received only a brief, difficult introduction to Jedi teaching. The books can accompany her wherever she goes. Reading them, though, will require the work of someone willing to put their lessons into practice.

The Last Jedi gives another example of a teaching reaching beyond its original setting. On Canto Bight, the stable children retell Luke's confrontation with the First Order. One of them draws a broom into his hand with the Force, then looks toward the stars. He hasn't entered a Jedi school, and the film doesn't give him a teacher. What has reached him is an account of someone standing against the forces that dominate his world. Luke's action has become a story that children can share even where the Resistance has little practical reach.

By The Rise of Skywalker, Rey has a teacher in Leia. At the Resistance base on Ajan Kloss, Leia oversees her training, and Rey practices both meditation and physical exercises. Her attempts aren't effortless. She struggles to connect with the Jedi who came before her, and troubling visions interrupt her concentration. This gives the surviving tradition a recognizable daily shape again. Someone practices, loses focus, tries to understand what happened, and returns to a teacher who expects her to continue. There isn't a restored Temple around them, but instruction is taking place.

Leia brings her own unfinished Jedi training to that relationship. On Ahch-To, Luke later tells Rey that Leia trained with him and surrendered her lightsaber after a vision concerning her son's death. The film shows the siblings practicing together. Leia's instruction of Rey therefore draws on something she learned and carried through the years, alongside her responsibilities in the Resistance. Luke is gone, yet his pupil can still teach another person. For Rey, that means the loss of one teacher hasn't ended the possibility of learning from someone who knew him.

The books also help Rey with a practical problem. Luke's notes describe his search for Exegol and point her toward Pasaana, where he had been looking for a Sith wayfinder. Rey follows that unfinished investigation with Finn, Poe, Chewbacca, and the droids. Written knowledge gives them somewhere to begin, though it doesn't spare them the dangers of the journey. Luke couldn't complete the search himself. By preserving his findings, he leaves Rey a route she can investigate with people whose abilities and experience differ from her own.

On Pasaana, Rey encounters a wounded serpent in the tunnels beneath the sinking fields. Rather than kill it to clear their path, she approaches and heals its injury through the Force. She explains to BB-8 that she transferred some of her own life energy to the creature. Once healed, it moves away and reveals an exit. The action belongs to the Jedi work of helping a living being in front of them. Rey and her companions need to escape, and attending to the serpent's wound allows everyone to move on.

Rey uses that ability again after fighting Kylo Ren on the wreckage of the second Death Star. When Leia reaches out to her son and Rey wounds him, Rey then heals the injury she has inflicted. Their encounter leaves her frightened by her own capacity for violence, and she retreats to Ahch-To intending to remain there. Luke appears to her and challenges that retreat. He also gives her Leia's lightsaber and raises his old X-wing from the water. His help leaves her with the means to travel, as well as a reason to return.

At Exegol, Rey finally hears the past Jedi she had struggled to reach during her training. Their voices address her while she lies injured before the Emperor. Among them are Obi-Wan, Yoda, Anakin, Ahsoka, Kanan, and Luke. They encourage her to rise and draw strength from the Jedi who came before. The help answers a difficulty introduced in her exercises with Leia. She couldn't summon that connection when she wanted it at the base. Here, in danger and exhaustion, she receives it.

Rey gets back to her feet and faces Palpatine with the lightsabers of Luke and Leia. She crosses the blades against his lightning and turns it back upon him, destroying him. The effort leaves her dead until Ben Solo reaches her and gives his life to restore hers. Rey's survival therefore includes help from people with very different histories: the Jedi whose voices reach her, the siblings whose weapons she carries, and the man who has returned from his allegiance to the dark side.

The films leave Rey with the Jedi writings and the experience of being taught by Luke and Leia. They don't show her opening a school or assembling a new Council. Her inheritance already has uses within the story: she can follow an unfinished investigation, heal an injured creature, and accept help when she can no longer stand alone. On Tatooine, she buries Luke's and Leia's lightsabers near the Lars homestead. She then reveals a lightsaber of her own, its blade yellow. The old weapons remain together beneath the sand, while Rey carries one she can use herself.
