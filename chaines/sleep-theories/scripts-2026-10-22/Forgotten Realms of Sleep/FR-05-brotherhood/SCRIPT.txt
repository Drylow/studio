At Mariposa Military Base, Roger Maxson discovers that the researchers his soldiers are guarding have been experimenting on human beings. The work carries the authority of the United States military, the same institution he's sworn to serve. His response begins the history of the Brotherhood of Steel. We'll follow that history through its bunkers and expeditions, as different chapters inherit the task of containing dangerous technology and disagree over what protecting people requires.

This is a games-only history, with full spoilers for Fallout, Fallout 2, Fallout 3 and Broken Steel, New Vegas, Fallout 4, and Fallout 76 through Steel Dawn and Steel Reign. The television series and the separate Tactics and Brotherhood of Steel games sit outside this account. Where the games leave a chapter's fate to the player, we'll keep those different outcomes separate. Here, at the beginning, Roger Maxson is still an Army officer inside a functioning military installation. The Brotherhood's familiar ranks and customs haven't yet taken shape.

Mariposa places soldiers and researchers within the same guarded facility, but their responsibilities give them very different access to what's happening there. Maxson's men provide security for a project whose human cost becomes clear to them only after they're already involved in protecting it. That arrangement matters. The soldiers haven't arrived to investigate a hostile laboratory. They discover the experiments inside their own chain of command, among people working under government authority. The research concerns the Forced Evolutionary Virus, usually shortened to FEV. In Fallout, this is a substance capable of changing living organisms far beyond the limits of ordinary medical treatment. Mariposa's researchers are using human subjects in their work. For Maxson, learning the purpose of the project changes what his assignment means. Keeping the installation secure also keeps its experimental program secure. The soldiers' everyday duty has helped shelter something they find intolerable, even though they weren't the people conducting the experiments.

The surviving records in the original Fallout give this beginning a particular texture. Maxson's diary follows an officer trying to respond to events within the base. It doesn't begin with a completed set of Brotherhood principles or a settled explanation of how civilization should handle advanced machinery. He has people under his command, scientists to question, and a military hierarchy above him. His later importance can make each early action seem planned around founding an order. Within the account itself, he's dealing with an immediate crisis at his post.

The later Brotherhood will approach old technology with a mixture of technical interest and suspicion. Here is advanced research whose custodians have already accepted human experimentation. Read in light of those later practices, Maxson's experience helps explain the Brotherhood's insistence that technical knowledge needs restrictions on its use. At the base, the people with the knowledge are also the people carrying out the work.

There's also a useful difference between Maxson's position here and the position of an outsider entering Mariposa long afterward. When the NCR's demand becomes relevant, the Courier can bring the possibility of cooperation to him. McNamara can accept a truce that commits Brotherhood soldiers to assist the republic, rather than forcing the Courier to carry out Moore's original order.

The available agreement has concrete terms. The NCR returns the suits of power armor it took from the Brotherhood, and Brotherhood forces support the NCR at Hoover Dam. Each side must give something the other considers valuable. For the Brotherhood, recovering its armor addresses a material grievance from the fighting. For the republic, the chapter's commitment supplies armed assistance at the decisive battle and removes an immediate threat in its rear. The arrangement gives both commanders something they can actually use.

Moore doesn't welcome the Courier's diplomatic success with enthusiasm. Her resistance keeps the truce from feeling like a friendship that was waiting to happen. The Courier has found an option she hadn't wanted to pursue, and the agreement asks the NCR to accommodate an organization it has fought. Yet the NCR route permits that result. A player who has preserved McNamara's leadership can bring the two forces into a limited partnership without pretending their history has ceased to matter.

Hardin's appointment closes that particular route. If the Courier helped remove McNamara and made Hardin elder, the same negotiated settlement isn't available. The earlier dispute over leadership therefore reaches beyond the bunker. It affects what the Courier can offer the NCR when Moore demands action. Hardin's determination to restore a more assertive Brotherhood leaves less room for the compromise McNamara can accept. A change that seemed internal to Hidden Valley alters the chapter's possible relationship with the republic outside.

If the truce is arranged and the NCR wins, the Brotherhood's ending describes its members patrolling the highways and assisting with the protection of travelers. The chapter acquires a place in the region's security rather than remaining entirely concealed beneath it. That outcome is conditional on the Courier's decisions and the final result of the war. It gives the Mojave Brotherhood a possible future alongside the NCR, with duties that put its soldiers among the people using the roads.

Mr. House takes a different position. He requires the Brotherhood's destruction as part of his plans for the Mojave. In his explanation, its beliefs and its approach to advanced technology make it an enduring threat to the future he intends to build. House depends on systems the Brotherhood would have strong reasons to covet or oppose. He isn't seeking an accommodation comparable to McNamara's agreement with the NCR. Continuing his quest line requires the Courier to resolve Hidden Valley on his terms.

Caesar likewise demands action against the bunker. For a Courier serving the Legion, the Brotherhood is another armed group to remove before the struggle for the region is settled. Caesar's judgment of the order belongs to his own account of it; the practical requirement is clear enough. The chapter can't expect its isolation to protect it from a power that has identified its location and wants it gone. Its sealed entrance delays contact, but it doesn't give its inhabitants a permanent exemption from war.

Destroying Hidden Valley means destroying an inhabited headquarters. The game allows different ways for the Courier to accomplish the assignment, including activating the bunker's self-destruct system. The consequences extend to the soldiers, scribes, and other members whose lives are organized around that underground home.  The same rooms where the chapter maintained equipment and argued over leadership can become the place where its Mojave presence ends.

An independent Courier has another degree of freedom through Yes Man. The Brotherhood can be left alone rather than destroyed, although leaving it intact doesn't automatically create the NCR truce or turn it into a cooperative public service. Its ending can include renewed efforts to take technology from travelers. That possibility matters when weighing independence against the negotiated NCR arrangement. Simply surviving the war doesn't settle how the Brotherhood will treat its neighbors, and declining to attack the bunker doesn't supply a new policy for its elder.


Before the Courier's journey through the Mojave, Owyn Lyons leads another Brotherhood expedition east. The Pentagon gives his force a headquarters large enough to sustain a presence far from the western bunkers. In the Capital Wasteland, its surviving structure becomes the Citadel. Soldiers train there, scribes work there, and the expedition develops into a chapter with responsibilities that extend beyond a traveling mission. The building still carries the remains of the military world the Brotherhood inherited. Around it, the people of the ruined Washington region face dangers that make a narrowly defined recovery mission increasingly difficult for Lyons to maintain.

Lyons had come east under orders from the Brotherhood's western leadership. The expedition was to recover advanced technology and investigate the region, with the search for information about super mutants among its concerns. Those objectives fit an organization accustomed to sending trained members into dangerous ruins. A distant military headquarters offered obvious reasons to investigate. The expedition's arrival didn't begin as a declared rejection of Brotherhood practice. The disagreement grew as Lyons decided what his soldiers should do with the strength they had brought.

The journey itself had already exposed the expedition to suffering beyond the western territories. Its passage through the Pitt is remembered through the Brotherhood action called the Scourge. That episode also connects the expedition to the child who would become Paladin Kodiak. The Pitt belongs to the chapter's history before the Citadel became its permanent home. It gives the eastward journey a human consequence: the people Lyons led didn't cross an empty map between bunkers. Their movement brought them into damaged communities and left lasting connections behind.

In Washington, super mutants present an immediate threat to the population. They occupy parts of the city, attack humans, and take captives. Ruined streets and buildings provide them with places to establish positions, while ordinary residents have limited means to resist them. Lyons commits Brotherhood forces to fighting that threat. Power armor, disciplined squads, and maintained weapons make his soldiers unusually capable of entering areas where a scavenger or a lightly armed settlement resident might have little chance of returning.

The commitment draws the chapter into repeated fighting rather than a single recovery operation. Brotherhood troops hold positions in the city and work against super mutant activity even though Lyons hasn't found a final solution to the problem. His soldiers can win encounters without stopping new attacks across the region. That continuing demand consumes ammunition, equipment, and lives. Members who crossed the continent to locate technology now spend their strength defending ground and confronting enemies whose defeat doesn't necessarily produce a recovered machine or a useful archive.

Sarah Lyons and Lyons' Pride make that commitment visible in the field. Sarah leads an elite unit that operates outside the Citadel, and her service puts the elder's family inside the dangers of his policy. At the Galaxy News Radio building, the Lone Wanderer encounters Brotherhood soldiers fighting super mutants. The battle brings an outsider into direct contact with their work. Whatever arguments are taking place within the chapter, the soldiers there are dealing with an immediate attack in a city people still try to inhabit.

Three Dog's presence at Galaxy News Radio adds another practical connection. His broadcasts reach listeners beyond the building, and the Brotherhood's position there ties its military activity to a voice used throughout the wasteland. Protecting that location has effects outside the squad defending it. A resident who never enters the Citadel can still hear news carried over the station. Lyons's decision to spend soldiers in the city therefore reaches people who have no place in the Brotherhood's ranks and no access to its stored technology.

Inside the Citadel, the expedition retains the working divisions of a Brotherhood chapter. Scribes study and maintain technology while soldiers prepare for operations. Elder Lyons hasn't abandoned technical research. The enormous Liberty Prime project remains under examination there, with Scribe Rothchild directing scientific work. The difficulty is one of resources and priority. Every commitment to defending residents draws on the same chapter that must repair equipment, train recruits, and carry out research. Its technical mission continues alongside the growing demands of local warfare.

Lyons also permits recruitment from the surrounding population. That helps an isolated eastern force sustain itself, and it changes who can belong to the chapter. Paladin Gunny's work with trainees places local recruits within the Citadel's military routine. The Brotherhood still demands discipline and training; a welcome into its ranks doesn't make someone an experienced soldier overnight. Yet opening that path ties the expedition more closely to the region. The people it protects can become the people who carry its weapons and maintain its positions.

For Lyons, assistance to the Capital Wasteland becomes a responsibility he won't set aside merely because the original mission was narrower. His dialogue presents that conviction as his own judgment after encountering the region's condition. Other members don't have to share it simply because he remains elder. Some believe he's spending their strength on work they were never sent to perform. Their objection grows from the same material facts he confronts: the expedition is distant from home, and its supplies and trained personnel aren't unlimited.

The western leadership eventually withdraws support after learning how far Lyons has departed from its priorities. He retains his position in the east, but the chapter can no longer rely on the relationship that originally sent it across the continent. That separation makes local recruitment and the Citadel's resources more consequential. Lyons has chosen obligations that his superiors won't continue financing. The decision also leaves dissatisfied members with a grievance that reaches beyond an argument over a single patrol or an individual rescue.

By the time the Lone Wanderer reaches the Citadel, Lyons commands a settled eastern chapter whose daily work has been reshaped by Washington. Its scribes still study military technology, and its soldiers still wear the order's armor. Those soldiers also fight in streets where local people are trying to survive. Lyons has answered the expedition's immediate question by committing to those people despite the cost. Within his own ranks, however, some members remain convinced that the expedition should have kept its attention on the technology it was sent to recover.


At Fort Independence, Henry Casdin leads Brotherhood soldiers who refuse to follow Elder Lyons's changed priorities. Their armor identifies them as the Outcasts, the name associated with their separation from the Citadel. They retain the training and technical interests they brought east, and they regard their work as faithful to the Brotherhood's original assignment. To Casdin, the soldiers serving Lyons have been diverted into a local war. His own group intends to recover technology and preserve it while the dispute with their former commander remains unresolved.

The separation comes after disagreement within the expedition has become too deep to manage through ordinary orders. Lyons continues directing soldiers toward the defense of Capital Wasteland residents. Casdin and those who leave with him reject that use of the mission's resources. They depart with equipment and establish a separate base. The loss hurts Lyons's chapter because the departing members are trained personnel taking useful military assets with them. The argument has acquired a physical shape: separate headquarters, separate patrols, and soldiers who no longer answer to the same immediate commander.

Calling Casdin's people impostors would miss the source of the conflict. They emerged from the same expedition as the Citadel's defenders. Their knowledge of Brotherhood procedure and their commitment to technological recovery didn't appear after their departure. Likewise, Lyons's chapter doesn't stop being the Brotherhood because its elder has accepted responsibilities the Outcasts reject. Both groups carry part of the institution's history into their present work. Their quarrel concerns what that inheritance requires them to do in the Capital Wasteland.

Fort Independence gives the Outcasts an operating base distinct from the Pentagon. Its occupants can store what they recover and organize activity beyond its walls. The Lone Wanderer encounters a guarded military site rather than an open settlement offering general assistance. Casdin is cautious about outsiders and dismissive of their technical understanding. His attitude makes practical sense within the role he has chosen for the fort: access to Brotherhood equipment and knowledge is something to restrict, and visitors must demonstrate value before expecting much cooperation.

An outsider can nevertheless become useful to him. Casdin accepts particular kinds of recovered technology and offers supplies in exchange. Energy weapons, power armor, and other accepted technical items can be brought to Fort Independence instead of sold through an ordinary wasteland merchant. The transaction gives the Lone Wanderer a repeatable relationship with the Outcasts. A person without Brotherhood membership can carry equipment out of the ruins and help fill their stores, provided the material matches what Casdin is seeking.

The rewards are practical supplies, including ammunition and medical items. That exchange reveals a limited form of cooperation with the surrounding world. Casdin doesn't need to invite the Lone Wanderer into the group's councils to recognize the usefulness of an outside scavenger. The arrangement extends the Outcasts' reach beyond their own patrols. Technology found during someone else's travels can end up inside Fort Independence, while the person who delivered it leaves with resources useful for surviving another journey through the wasteland.

Outcast patrols also take their mission onto the roads. Their armor and weapons give them considerable strength, though the wasteland still exposes them to hostile creatures and armed opponents. The patrols make the split visible beyond either headquarters. A traveler can encounter Brotherhood-trained soldiers who have no intention of returning to Lyons's command. Their presence means the technological search continues even while the Citadel commits much of its attention to super mutants. The original expedition's work has divided between forces following different priorities.

For the soldiers at the Citadel, that division is more than an argument over terminology. Fewer experienced fighters remain available for the operations Lyons has chosen. The Outcasts' departure also leaves an uncomfortable challenge to his judgment standing nearby. Casdin can point to their recovery work as the mission Lyons should have continued. Lyons can point to the people endangered by the fighting his soldiers confront. Neither headquarters has to imagine the other's position in the abstract; both can observe how the other spends personnel and equipment.

Casdin hopes to make contact with the western Brotherhood and explain what happened. That hope places Fort Independence within a larger institutional dispute. He doesn't see separation from Lyons as the final definition of his group's future. Recognition from the west could affirm his claim that he has preserved the expedition's proper purpose. The distance between Washington and the western elders makes that ambition difficult to realize, leaving the Outcasts to carry on their work without an immediate outside judgment settling the quarrel.

Their stance also affects what local residents can expect. Finding an Outcast patrol doesn't carry the same implication as finding a force committed to defending a settlement. The patrol's assignment centers on technological recovery. It may fight threats it encounters, but Casdin hasn't adopted Lyons's broader undertaking on behalf of the region. A recovered weapon has a clear destination at Fort Independence. A community asking for sustained protection is making a different kind of request, one that the Outcasts left the Citadel partly to avoid accepting.

Fort Independence therefore preserves a functioning alternative within the eastern expedition's history. Its members maintain equipment, search for recoverable technology, and reward outsiders who bring suitable discoveries. Their separation weakens Lyons's force while allowing them to pursue work they believe he has neglected. The disagreement remains visible in ordinary transactions: the Lone Wanderer arrives carrying an energy weapon, Casdin assesses whether he wants it, and useful supplies change hands. Behind the fort's guarded entrance, another piece of the old technological world enters Brotherhood custody.


James returns to the Jefferson Memorial because Project Purity offers something the Capital Wasteland lacks: a way to provide clean water on a large scale. The work requires scientific knowledge, a suitable facility, and protection for people who can't complete it while fighting for their lives. Elder Lyons's Brotherhood becomes involved in all of those surroundings. Its commitment to helping the region now has a specific installation to defend, with a result that could reach far beyond a single neighborhood or a squad's patrol route.

The project already has a history with the Brotherhood. Before James left to raise his child in Vault 101, Lyons supported the researchers working at the memorial. James's departure contributed to the effort's collapse, and Doctor Madison Li eventually established herself in Rivet City. When the Lone Wanderer finds James and helps bring him back into the work, the old undertaking becomes possible again. The scientists return to a facility whose promise hasn't disappeared, even though years of abandonment have left practical problems to overcome.

Their return also attracts the Enclave. Colonel Autumn arrives at the purifier and demands that James surrender the installation. The Enclave's intervention turns a research site into an occupied objective. James dies during the confrontation, and the Lone Wanderer helps Doctor Li and the surviving scientists escape through the tunnels. Their destination is the Citadel. Lyons's headquarters becomes a refuge for people whose expertise is essential to the project and whose own workplace has been taken by a better organized military force.

Doctor Li's arrival brings the cost of the takeover directly to the Brotherhood's gate. Once the scientists are inside, the chapter has to consider more than sheltering them. The Enclave holds the purifier, and the project still needs the Garden of Eden Creation Kit that James had identified as a solution to its technical difficulties. The Lone Wanderer's search leads to Vault 87. Recovering that device can advance the project, but it also gives the Enclave another opportunity to capture the person carrying it.

At Raven Rock, President Eden presents his own plan for the water supply. He gives the Lone Wanderer a modified FEV sample and asks for it to be introduced into the purifier. His claims about cleansing the wasteland describe the Enclave president's intended use of the project, with lethal consequences for those the agent would target. Colonel Autumn's approach differs: he wants possession of the water facility as a means of securing the Enclave's position. Their disagreement leaves the purifier caught between competing plans within the same organization.

After the Lone Wanderer returns to the Citadel, Lyons's forces prepare to retake the memorial. Liberty Prime makes that attack possible on a scale ordinary Brotherhood squads couldn't manage alone. The enormous robot has stood as a difficult technical project under the scribes' care. With its operational problems overcome, it can advance against the Enclave's defenses. A machine recovered from the old military world now leads soldiers through obstacles separating the scientists from the water system they need to activate.

Sarah Lyons and the Brotherhood advance behind Liberty Prime toward the memorial. The robot breaks through fortified resistance and energy barriers, opening the route for the force following it. Its success connects the chapter's technological work to a public objective in a very direct way. The scribes' labor has produced a usable military asset, and that asset allows the Brotherhood to reach Project Purity. Recovering and repairing advanced equipment has become part of the effort to deliver clean water to people outside the order.

Inside the memorial, the confrontation with Autumn can end through combat or persuasion, depending on the Lone Wanderer's actions. Reaching the control room still leaves an urgent technical problem. Someone must enter the irradiated chamber and activate the purifier using the code James left his child. The base game frames that decision around the Lone Wanderer and Sarah, with lethal exposure attached to entering the chamber. The player can also make destructive choices, including using Eden's sample, rather than completing the benevolent result James intended.

The original ending stops the playable story at Project Purity. Broken Steel changes both the available resolution and what follows. With the expansion installed, an appropriate radiation-resistant companion can be asked to activate the purifier, and a Lone Wanderer who enters the chamber can survive to awaken later at the Citadel. Sarah's involvement also depends on the player's decision. The story therefore doesn't require every version of the Lone Wanderer to die for the project. Its continuation makes room for the person who helped recover the facility to see the consequences.

Under the successful clean-water outcome, the Brotherhood takes on distribution through the operation known as Aqua Pura. Water leaves the purifier in containers and moves toward communities that need it. Scribe Bigsley oversees work at the memorial, where the burden includes administration and supply problems alongside technical operation. A functioning purifier hasn't removed the dangers of transport or the possibility of misuse. Brotherhood personnel now have to deal with caravans, demands for water, and the complications of making a valuable resource available across an unsettled region.

Broken Steel also continues the war with the Enclave. Liberty Prime accompanies a later assault, but an orbital strike destroys the robot during the operation. The loss removes the machine that had made the march to the memorial so effective. The Brotherhood must continue through other means, and the Lone Wanderer's assignments eventually reach the Enclave's mobile base at Adams Air Force Base. The expansion allows another major branch there, including the possibility of turning destruction against the Citadel rather than completing the Brotherhood's campaign as intended.

For Lyons's chapter, Project Purity creates work that persists after the great assault. Soldiers can recover the memorial, but water still has to be processed, guarded, allocated, and carried to its destinations. Scientists who escaped to the Citadel have helped make the installation usable again, and the Brotherhood has committed its strength to keeping that result available. In the successful continuation, the concrete result is visible beyond the battlefield: containers of Aqua Pura leave the Jefferson Memorial, and people elsewhere in the Capital Wasteland receive clean water.


Arthur Maxson first appears in Fallout 3 as a child living inside the Citadel, surrounded by people who already know his surname. He's descended from Roger Maxson, whose break with the old United States Army began the Brotherhood. That history gives Arthur a place in the order before he's old enough to earn one through service. Within the Citadel, though, his daily position is still that of a young squire. Elder Owyn Lyons commands the chapter, and Sarah Lyons leads soldiers whose experience Arthur can only begin to imagine.

Speaking with Arthur brings that difference into focus. He knows that his family matters, but he hasn't yet acquired the certainty associated with the name. His conversation includes his admiration for Sarah, and his writing gives another glimpse of a boy trying out an identity in a community full of accomplished fighters. The Citadel contains training grounds, workshops and veterans. Arthur belongs within its walls, yet almost everyone around him has done things he hasn't. His inheritance makes him conspicuous during years when another child might have remained unremarkable.

Lyons's protection places him inside a particular version of the Brotherhood. Lyons's chapter spends its strength fighting super mutants and helping defend the Capital Wasteland. Arthur grows up near the arguments and sacrifices produced by that commitment. He also lives with the consequences of the split that created the Outcasts. The institution bearing his family's name has members who disagree strongly enough to leave their elder behind. For a child expected to embody continuity, the Brotherhood around him is already visibly divided.

Sarah provides a closer example of military service than the distant founder does. She's present, active and respected, leading Lyons' Pride in the chapter's hardest fighting. Arthur's admiration has a personal quality, but her position also matters to his upbringing. Here is someone whose standing comes from commanding soldiers in dangerous conditions. Arthur's surname can't supply that experience. When Fallout 4 later presents him as an elder, the distance between the Citadel's young squire and the commander aboard the Prydwen carries much of the change.

The intervening years reach us largely through Brotherhood accounts. The historical material aboard the Prydwen records Owyn Lyons's death and Sarah's succession as elder. It then reports that Sarah died in combat. Those losses removed both the leader who had sheltered Arthur and the soldier he admired. Fallout 4 doesn't provide a playable account of their final days. What its chapter history does describe is a period of unsuccessful leadership after them, followed by Arthur's elevation. His rise therefore belongs to a succession crisis as well as a family story.

The same records build a heroic account of his youth. They credit him with exceptional accomplishments against dangerous opponents and with dealing with a super mutant leader called Shepherd. These are the chapter's official claims about its elder. Their place in the record matters even without treating every feat as independently witnessed history. Soldiers reading those accounts encounter an Arthur who proves himself early, survives extraordinary danger and restores direction after weaker leaders. The account supplies the experience that the child in the Citadel once lacked.

His physical appearance in Fallout 4 contributes to that presentation. He's still young, but he carries a scar and wears a distinctive coat rather than appearing as another anonymous soldier in power armor. His speech is formal and assured. The change isn't confined to how other people describe him in terminals. The Sole Survivor meets someone who gives orders, defines enemies and expects obedience. Arthur has learned to occupy a position that once existed around him as an expectation attached to his ancestry.

Reuniting with the Outcasts gives his leadership a concrete institutional accomplishment. Their separation had weakened the eastern Brotherhood and left competing claims about its proper work. The Prydwen's history credits Maxson with bringing them back. That reunion gives him soldiers and technical resources, while also allowing the eastern chapter to present itself as a single Brotherhood again. He succeeds at something that Lyons's personal authority hadn't resolved. Members who had rejected the old elder's priorities can accept service under the founder's descendant.

The reunited chapter doesn't reproduce Lyons's policy unchanged. Maxson places the recovery and restriction of dangerous technology firmly within his campaign, and the Institute becomes the clearest target for that purpose. At the same time, the eastern Brotherhood continues to recruit outsiders. The Sole Survivor can enter through service with Danse rather than through birth inside the order. Arthur's chapter therefore contains practices inherited from different periods. Its recruitment can enlarge the organization, while its leadership insists on a demanding definition of what that organization exists to do.

Proctor Quinlan's material also describes the growing reverence surrounding Maxson and reports that he rejects worship directed toward him. That detail places another burden beside military command. A living descendant of the founder can become the subject of claims that reach beyond ordinary respect for an elder. Even an account praising his refusal contributes to his reputation. The official portrait presents a commander worthy of extraordinary devotion who remains disciplined enough to refuse it. Arthur's public identity is being preserved by scribes while he's still alive to act within it.

For the soldiers under him, ancestry and current achievement reinforce each other. Roger Maxson's name reaches back to the Brotherhood's beginning. Arthur's reunion with the Outcasts addresses a fracture those soldiers have experienced themselves. The Prydwen gives that renewed chapter a visible project and a means of leaving the Capital Wasteland. His authority consequently has several supports: family continuity, recognition by the order and an army capable of carrying out his decisions. The child once protected by Lyons now determines where that army will go.

That leaves Arthur in a position quite different from merely preserving his guardian's work. He inherits people who served Lyons, people who abandoned Lyons and recruits who never knew either side of the split. He has to make them function together under his command. Fallout 4 meets him after that consolidation, when his decisions already carry the weight of the reunited chapter. Aboard the Prydwen, the Maxson name belongs to the elder issuing the orders, and the soldiers around him are preparing to carry those orders into the Commonwealth.


The Prydwen enters the Commonwealth as something its scattered Brotherhood patrols could never have concealed. The airship crosses above the ruins, accompanied by vertibirds, and broadcasts the presence of an armed expedition. For people on the ground, the Brotherhood is suddenly a force with an airborne headquarters and the means to move soldiers across the region. For the reconnaissance team at Cambridge Police Station, its arrival means that the larger organization has finally reached the place where they've been struggling to hold their position.

Danse's team makes the preparation behind that arrival visible. Before the airship appears, the Sole Survivor can find the soldiers defending the police station against feral ghouls. Their expedition has suffered losses, and its remaining members need help. Haylen tends to the team's practical needs while Rhys remains suspicious of an outsider. Danse is trying to restore reliable communication with the Brotherhood. The force that later fills the sky has depended on people working from a damaged station, with limited support and immediate threats around them.

At ArcJet Systems, Danse seeks a deep range transmitter. The building's old industrial equipment and its synth occupants bring the Brotherhood's technical work into contact with the Institute before Maxson arrives. Recovering the transmitter gives the reconnaissance team a way to improve its communications. The mission also allows Danse to judge the Sole Survivor through shared work. Brotherhood expansion begins here with a specific piece of equipment and a small team trying to make itself heard. The airship's entrance follows that groundwork rather than replacing its importance.

After the arrival, Boston Airport becomes the expedition's ground base. The Prydwen remains above it, while soldiers, equipment and construction activity occupy the airport below. Vertibirds connect the force's airborne presence with its operations on the ground. This arrangement gives the Brotherhood a place to assemble machinery that can't be handled entirely aboard the ship. It also creates a fixed location that needs protection. The airship can travel, but the Commonwealth campaign soon gathers people and resources around an airport everyone else can locate.

Inside the Prydwen, the expedition resembles a working military community. Its spaces include bunks, medical facilities, supplies, workshops and command areas. Soldiers live close to the specialists who keep their equipment usable. Power armor may be the most recognizable part of a Brotherhood patrol, but a suit needs servicing between deployments. Weapons need attention, injuries need treatment, and recovered material needs somewhere to go. The ship carries those activities with it, allowing Maxson's force to operate far from the Citadel without expecting each patrol to provide everything for itself.

The proctors divide that labor into recognizable responsibilities. Ingram directs engineering work, including demanding projects on the ground. Teagan manages the supply side and provides equipment. Cade examines personnel and handles medical duties. Quinlan oversees research and the use of information brought back by field teams. None of these jobs disappears because the chapter has superior weapons. A damaged suit or an inaccurate report can interfere with a mission before a soldier ever reaches the intended target. The expedition's reach depends on these people doing their work repeatedly.

Ingram makes the physical demands particularly clear. She uses a power armor frame after losing her legs, and she continues to direct technical work in person. Around her, engineering involves parts, repairs and the problem of making enormous prewar machinery operate again. The Brotherhood can recover devices that would defeat an ordinary scavenger's resources, but recovery is only an early stage. Someone has to identify what's missing and determine whether replacement components can be found. Ingram's department turns recovered hardware into something the chapter can actually deploy.

The Prydwen itself has requirements that even its imposing size can't remove. Its propulsion and cooling systems need attention, and Ingram can ask the Sole Survivor to obtain coolant for the ship. That task brings the airship down to the level of materials that must be gathered and processed. Keeping it aloft is ongoing work. Aboard a vessel carrying soldiers and stores, maintenance isn't a background luxury. The same headquarters that allows the chapter to project strength also commits its engineers to supporting a complicated machine throughout the campaign.

Food creates another connection to the Commonwealth below. Teagan can offer assignments involving local farms, asking the Sole Survivor to secure crops for the Brotherhood. Depending on how the player handles the task, arrangements can involve payment, persuasion or coercion. Teagan's approach shouldn't be mistaken for a complete public account of Maxson's policy, but it shows a supply problem inside the expedition. The people aboard the Prydwen must eat. Their arrival places demands on a region whose farms already support residents living with insecurity and scarcity.

Maxson gives these departments a defined campaign against the Institute. He treats its synth production and secretive technological activity as dangers the Brotherhood must eliminate. Yet the Institute isn't a fortress visible from the airport. Its main facilities are underground, and its ability to send synths into the Commonwealth gives it reach without exposing an ordinary entrance. The Brotherhood's vertibirds can't solve that problem by flying farther. Reports, recovered data and knowledge of the Institute's transportation system become necessary to turn Maxson's declared objective into an accessible target.

The expedition consequently continues to send people into ruins and hostile territory. Haylen's technical recovery assignments, Rhys's requests to clear threats and Quinlan's research work all use personnel outside the ship. Field scribes may require escorts while they collect information. These missions show how a large headquarters relies on smaller groups leaving its protection. The chapter can concentrate equipment and specialists at the airport, but those specialists still need material from places they don't occupy. Its map of the Commonwealth grows through patrols whose members remain vulnerable once they're away from the main force.

The Prydwen changes the Brotherhood's position because it brings those separate activities together. Danse's reconnaissance work now has a command structure nearby. Recovered components can reach engineers, and information can reach scribes who compare it with other reports. Vertibirds carry that organized force beyond the airport, while the airport supports work too large for the ship. Maxson has arrived with an institution capable of sustaining a campaign. Its daily existence remains visible in the people repairing armor, treating soldiers and preparing the next patrol to leave.


Danse gives the Sole Survivor a place in the Brotherhood before either of them knows the fact that will threaten his own membership. At Cambridge Police Station, he needs help keeping his reconnaissance team alive. At ArcJet Systems, he works beside an outsider to recover communications equipment. The invitation to join grows out of those encounters. Danse has seen the Sole Survivor act under pressure, and he considers that service worth bringing into the order. His judgment opens a route into a community that places enormous weight on discipline and loyalty.

His own conduct follows those expectations. Danse reports to his superiors, takes responsibility for his team and speaks with confidence about the Brotherhood's mission. He doesn't present himself as someone looking for a temporary arrangement. The order has given him a purpose, and he measures his actions against it. When the Prydwen arrives, he's connected to something larger than the struggling group at the police station again. The Sole Survivor's introduction to that larger force happens partly through his recommendation and through the standing he already holds.

Traveling with Danse allows that public military identity to acquire personal detail. He can describe his life before joining, including his time as a trader in Rivet City. He also speaks about Cutler, the friend with whom he entered the Brotherhood. Cutler's eventual transformation into a super mutant forced Danse into an experience he remembers with pain. His account gives his hostility toward threats to humanity a history within his own service. The beliefs he repeats are connected to loss, and he has lived by them while asking others to do the same.

His reactions to the Sole Survivor can reinforce that impression. He respects conduct that fits his understanding of duty and disapproves of actions he considers irresponsible or disloyal. Through the companion relationship, the player may come to know him as a demanding soldier whose attachment to the Brotherhood is sincere. Those interactions matter when his identity is disclosed. By then, the player has had opportunities to observe his habits and decisions over time. The man condemned by the institution can be someone whose service the player has shared and whose approval they've earned.

The disclosure follows the recovery of Institute information during the Brotherhood quest line. Analysis of that material identifies Danse as a synth, designated M7-97. The discovery reaches Maxson through the chapter's own handling of the data. Danse disappears, and the elder orders the Sole Survivor to find and execute him. A mission against the Institute has produced information that turns inward upon one of the chapter's trusted paladins. Danse's previous record doesn't create an exemption from Maxson's judgment. Under the elder's policy, the newly discovered identity is sufficient to make him a target.

There's no indication that Danse knowingly joined as an Institute infiltrator. His confusion and distress in Blind Betrayal are part of the encounter, and he accepts the discovery as a devastating explanation of something he hadn't understood about himself. The game doesn't supply a complete account of every step that brought him from Institute creation to Brotherhood service. What the Sole Survivor confronts is immediate enough: Danse believes in the order that has condemned him, and he has trouble imagining a legitimate place for himself outside its ruling.

Haylen refuses to let the assignment remain a straightforward execution order. She cares about Danse and asks the Sole Survivor to hear him out. Her intervention draws on service alongside him, including the period when their reconnaissance team was isolated and under attack. For Haylen, the newly recovered information exists beside the person who protected and commanded her. She directs the Sole Survivor toward Listening Post Bravo, where Danse has taken shelter. Her action creates room for a conversation Maxson's initial command doesn't require.

Inside the bunker, Danse doesn't offer a triumphant argument that the Brotherhood has misunderstood everything. He's burdened by the possibility that he's the sort of being he's been trained to reject. He can accept his own execution because the command comes from an authority he still recognizes. That response makes his situation especially painful. The institution doesn't have to persuade him that loyalty matters. His loyalty helps make him willing to submit to a sentence based on his origin, even though his conscious service has been directed against the Institute.

The Sole Survivor can carry out the order, and Danse can die during the quest. The player can also persuade him to live. If that attempt succeeds and they leave the bunker, Maxson confronts them outside. The dispute then becomes direct. The elder has to face the officer whose record he knows, while the Sole Survivor argues for an exception. The available outcome depends on the player's decisions and success in persuasion. Blind Betrayal allows Danse's death or survival; it doesn't establish one result for every version of the Commonwealth.

If Maxson is persuaded to spare him, the terms are restrictive. Danse must be treated as dead and removed from the Brotherhood. His survival doesn't change the chapter's general position on synths, and it doesn't restore him to his old post. The Sole Survivor can remain within the order, but Danse's connection to it has been severed. This is a personal concession secured within an unchanged policy. Maxson permits a former paladin to live beyond the organization's protection, carrying the knowledge that the soldiers he served with must no longer accept him as one of their own.

A surviving Danse can continue as a companion, with practical consequences. Brotherhood forces become hostile while he's accompanying the Sole Survivor. The separation therefore persists beyond the conversation at the bunker. He can't resume ordinary service by traveling beside the same recruit he once sponsored. His armor and training remain, as do his memories of belonging, but Brotherhood patrols now respond to his presence as a threat. The player has to account for that exclusion whenever they bring him near the institution that shaped his adult life.

Danse's case gives Maxson's campaign a particular human cost without requiring a change of ending. Whether he dies or survives, the paladin loses the standing his actions had earned. Haylen remembers a commander worth defending, while Maxson applies a category he considers essential to humanity's safety. The Sole Survivor has to act between those judgments. At Listening Post Bravo, the question reaches a soldier who has already devoted himself to the Brotherhood, and the answer determines whether he leaves that shelter alive.


Finding a way into the Institute doesn't give the Brotherhood an uncontested victory. By that stage of Fallout 4, the Sole Survivor may have worked with several groups whose plans can't all be completed together. Maxson intends to destroy the Institute and treats the Railroad's protection of synths as another obstacle. The Railroad wants synths to escape, while the Institute expects its personnel and creations to remain subject to its authority. Each organization can use the Sole Survivor's access, but eventually that access leads to decisions that close other routes.

Mass Fusion makes the division concrete through a piece of machinery. The Institute seeks a beryllium agitator for its reactor project. The Brotherhood also wants the device for its work on Liberty Prime. The Sole Survivor can commit to the Institute's operation or warn the Brotherhood and pursue its alternative mission. The reactor component can't satisfy both campaigns in the way each faction intends. Taking the Institute route brings open hostility with the Brotherhood, while committing to the Brotherhood's version makes the break with the Institute explicit. Technical recovery has become an irreversible military decision.

On the Brotherhood path, the conflict with the Railroad comes into the open through Tactical Thinking. Captain Kells assigns the destruction of the Railroad's leadership in its headquarters beneath the Old North Church. These are people the Sole Survivor may already know through rescue work and covert operations. Their underground base protects an organization whose understanding of synths conflicts with Maxson's campaign. Carrying out the Brotherhood assignment removes that organization as an active rival. It also makes clear that the eastern chapter's objectives extend beyond destroying the Institute's laboratories.

The Railroad has its own conditional attack against the Brotherhood. If the Sole Survivor continues along its endgame, Rockets' Red Glare takes the conflict aboard the Prydwen. Deacon and Tinker Tom participate in an operation that uses a vertibird to reach the ship. Explosives planted aboard can destroy the airship and remove the Brotherhood's Commonwealth headquarters. The vessel that made the expedition so visible also concentrates its leadership and support facilities in a single target. In this branch, the force at the airport loses the airborne center around which its campaign was organized.

The Institute can bring about a different assault on the same expedition. During Airship Down, its forces attack Boston Airport, and the Sole Survivor helps compromise the Brotherhood's defenses. The Institute's plan uses Liberty Prime against the airship after its systems are turned to that purpose. The destruction therefore comes through machinery the Brotherhood has restored for its own war. This outcome belongs to an Institute-aligned route. It can't be combined into a single history with the Brotherhood successfully completing its own attack on the Institute.

If the Sole Survivor stays with Maxson's campaign, Liberty Prime becomes the means of reaching the underground enemy. Ingram's engineering work and the recovery missions supporting it lead to the robot's deployment during Ad Victoriam. Brotherhood soldiers move with a machine capable of crossing terrain and resisting opposition on a scale an ordinary patrol can't match. The destination is the ruined Commonwealth Institute of Technology. There, Prime opens a route down into the Institute, giving the Brotherhood access to facilities that its aircraft and ground patrols couldn't previously enter.

Inside, the campaign ends with an assault on the reactor. The Sole Survivor works with the Brotherhood to set up the Institute's destruction and escape before the explosion. The game permits an evacuation warning, so the treatment of people inside isn't identical in every playthrough even along this route. The destruction leaves a crater where the Institute's hidden complex had operated. For Maxson's chapter, the campaign has achieved its declared objective. The Institute no longer provides a functioning center for synth production and the activities the elder has condemned.

A Brotherhood victory also changes the Sole Survivor's place within the order. Maxson can promote the player to sentinel, granting a rank above the ordinary sequence of field assignments through which they entered. The Prydwen remains available in this outcome, and the chapter continues its presence in the Commonwealth. Its soldiers haven't packed away their equipment because the central target is gone. Patrols and military facilities remain part of the region. The conclusion of the main campaign leaves an armed institution operating among settlements rather than depicting its departure for home.

The Minutemen allow another arrangement, provided the Sole Survivor maintains the necessary relationships. With their help, the player can destroy the Institute without having the Brotherhood perform the assault. The Railroad and Brotherhood can both survive that main ending if the player hasn't made them enemies through other commitments. This route leaves groups with incompatible beliefs still present in the Commonwealth. Their continued existence doesn't amount to a negotiated settlement of every disagreement. It means the player's path to the Institute's destruction hasn't required eliminating them along the way.

A Minutemen conflict with the Brotherhood is also possible. If relations have become hostile, With Our Powers Combined allows the Minutemen to use settlement artillery against the Prydwen. The Brotherhood then attacks the Castle, and the Minutemen must defend it. This branch gives the local militia a means of fighting the expedition without adopting either the Railroad's infiltration operation or the Institute's assault plan. The settlements' artillery network matters because the airship is within reach. The organization built around defending Commonwealth communities can become the force that destroys Maxson's headquarters.

For the Brotherhood, the Commonwealth can therefore contain either a surviving expedition or the wreckage of its headquarters. Its victory over the Institute is one possible resolution, alongside routes that defeat it or leave it standing while another force finishes the main war. No single outcome is fixed here. The concrete ending depends on whom the Sole Survivor supports and which orders they carry out. In one branch, soldiers return to the Prydwen after the Institute's destruction. In another, the ship itself is destroyed above the airport where the expedition established its base.


At Fort Atlas, Scribe Valdez has work to do beneath the Brotherhood's new headquarters. During Mother of Invention in Fallout 76, she takes the player into the facility's lower levels to examine its machinery. The installation contains prewar systems that have survived without becoming fully understood by their new occupants. Valdez approaches them through inspection and technical tasks, asking the initiate to participate. The Brotherhood has claimed the building, but making use of what's inside requires someone prepared to investigate equipment whose condition and purpose can't be assumed from the insignia now displayed above it.

Her work gives preservation a practical shape. Machinery has to be examined before it can be evaluated, and observations have to be carried back into the chapter's understanding of the site. Valdez isn't merely accumulating objects in a storeroom. She's trying to learn enough about them to make informed decisions. At Atlas, that task belongs to someone who can explain procedures to a newcomer and assess how carefully they're followed. Technical knowledge survives through that transfer between people as well as through the continued existence of a machine.

Valdez also works inside the chapter divided by Rahmani and Shin. She's responsible for research and engineering while the senior officers disagree over what the Appalachian Brotherhood should become. Their disagreement doesn't eliminate the need for her work. Weapons still need competent handling, facilities still contain hazards, and recruits still need instruction. Her position suggests why a chapter can continue functioning during an argument about its direction. Some of its members are occupied with tasks that neither side can abandon without making daily operations harder for everyone at Fort Atlas.

Veronica approaches the same problem from a different place in Fallout: New Vegas. She leaves Hidden Valley to obtain supplies and has more contact with the Mojave outside than members confined to the bunker. At the 188 Trading Post, she can join the Courier and travel through communities the chapter has withdrawn from. Her attachment to the Brotherhood remains strong, but the journey puts her beside people finding ways to live without it. She sees working farms, trading routes and groups whose growing strength doesn't depend on permission from an elder.

If the Courier helps Veronica recover Vault 22's agricultural data, she can bring the elder research concerned with feeding people rather than equipping soldiers. Her proposal asks the chapter to judge preserved knowledge partly by what it could do for the communities outside.

Aboard Fallout 4's Prydwen, maintenance gives the same broad mission another daily form. Ingram's engineering work depends on tools, replacement components and people who understand the machinery around them. Scribes and technical personnel support soldiers whose armor can return damaged from the field. The ship's workshops place that labor close to the force that uses it. An order may describe its mission in declarations about humanity, but a technician still has to determine why a piece of equipment isn't working and what can be done with the parts available.

Field scribes extend that work beyond the headquarters. In Learning Curve, the Sole Survivor can escort a scribe gathering data from a dangerous location. The assignment makes scholarship physically vulnerable. Information sits in places occupied by enemies, and the person trained to retrieve it may need protection. Elsewhere, Quinlan accepts recovered technical documents, giving scavenged records a destination within the chapter. These tasks preserve material that could otherwise remain scattered through abandoned buildings. Their outcome depends on a scribe returning, a document being carried back and someone having the knowledge to use it.

Valdez's examination at Atlas, Veronica's attempt to make research useful and the Prydwen's maintenance work each involve people deciding what deserves attention. They don't reach the same conclusions about who should benefit. They do share the labor of keeping difficult knowledge usable, through inspection, explanation and repair. That work can continue while officers dispute policy and while a scribe questions the chapter that trained her. In the Prydwen's workshop, a damaged piece of equipment still needs attention before it can be issued again. Someone has to examine it, find the fault and make the repair.
