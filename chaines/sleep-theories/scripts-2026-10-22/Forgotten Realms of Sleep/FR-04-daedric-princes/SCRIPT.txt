At Azura's shrine in the Jerall Mountains, the Hero of Kvatch brings glow dust as an offering and approaches at dawn or dusk. Azura answers with a task concerning worshippers who haven't returned from a mine. An offering has led to an obligation, and the people waiting at the end of it belong to her. We'll meet the Daedric Princes through encounters like this, through the bargains mortals accept, the realms they enter, and the changes those encounters leave in ordinary places.

When Azura speaks, she describes five followers who went to destroy vampires in the Gutted Mine. They succeeded in killing the vampire Dratik, but contracted vampirism themselves. After recognizing what had happened, they sealed themselves inside. The journey that began as service to Azura ended with her worshippers cut off from the world outside. Their devotion hadn't protected them from infection. It did shape their response to it, because they chose confinement rather than return to the lives they'd left.

Azura asks the Hero to enter the mine and end their suffering. That's the obligation attached to this particular audience. The Hero isn't sent to recover a stolen treasure or settle an argument among the people at the shrine. The task concerns followers whose earlier service has left them in a condition they couldn't accept. Azura presents their deaths as a release, and the Hero must decide whether to carry out what she asks. Answering a Prince can mean taking responsibility for the unfinished consequences of someone else's devotion.

The mine gives that request a location small enough to understand. Cyrodiil has roads, settlements, farms and mines scattered beyond the walls of its cities. A place where people once worked can become shelter for vampires, and an expedition sent to clear it can become trapped there in turn. The Gutted Mine's danger has a history by the time the Hero arrives. Dratik is already dead. Killing him didn't remove the infection from those who confronted him, so the problem Azura wants resolved has outlasted the enemy they went there to destroy.

If the Hero accepts and completes the task, the worshippers inside must be killed. The game doesn't provide a successful version of this commission in which the Hero escorts them back to the shrine. That gives the encounter an uncomfortable weight even without dwelling on the fighting. The people in the mine were able to recognize their condition and take action to protect others. By the time the Hero reaches them, their decision to remain secluded has become part of the situation the visitor must enter.

Returning to Azura completes a journey between two very different places of worship. At the shrine, Mels and the other followers can gather openly beneath their patron's statue. In the mine, the afflicted worshippers have continued their service through withdrawal from the outside world. Azura thanks the Hero and says their spirits are free. That assurance comes from Azura herself. For the surviving followers at the shrine, their Prince has answered a loss within their own community through the work of a passing stranger.

The reward is Azura's Star, a reusable soul gem. Ordinary soul gems are consumed when their stored souls are used for enchantment or recharging enchanted equipment. The Star can be filled and used again. Its usefulness makes the reward easy to appreciate within the Hero's traveling life, where enchanted equipment needs replenishment and carrying supplies matters. After a task involving the release of Azura's followers, the Hero receives an object that can hold souls. The connection is striking, although Azura doesn't offer an explanation of that pairing.

This is also where a Daedric encounter begins to extend beyond its original setting. The Hero can leave the mountains with something that remains useful far from the shrine. Mels doesn't have to accompany them, and the Hero doesn't have to live among Azura's followers to carry her gift. A traveler who arrived with glow dust can depart with an artifact associated with a Prince. The exchange has brought the Hero into the affairs of a religious community, then placed a lasting reminder of that service among their possessions.


An Altmer reader opening Aedra and Daedra encounters a distinction built into the names themselves. The book translates Aedra as "our ancestors" and Daedra as those who aren't their ancestors. Those terms belong to an Elven account of where the world and its peoples came from. Before anyone reaches a shrine or asks a Prince for a favor, the language has already placed that encounter within a family history. Someone raised in another tradition may recognize the beings while understanding their place in creation quite differently.

The same book connects the Aedra with the making of the mortal world. They contributed to its creation, and that involvement changed what they could be. The Daedra, in this account, didn't take part in making it. That's the broad distinction behind the familiar names, though the word ancestor carries a stronger meaning for Elves than it does for the Empire's human worshippers. An Imperial praying to Akatosh needn't believe that the god occupies exactly the same ancestral position described by an Altmer religious writer.

The Monomyth gathers creation traditions that give this distinction a setting. Its accounts begin with a condition before the ordinary world, when the spirits who would become gods hadn't yet acquired all the separations familiar to mortals. The text discusses Anu and Padomay, and the emergence of spirits within the tension between them. These are religious explanations of how existence began. Their language moves through stillness, change, and the discovery of identity because there isn't yet a Tamriel with roads, kingdoms, or people to describe.

In the Altmeri account included in The Monomyth, the spirits learn to persist as themselves. Some grow stronger and more distinct, and recognizable divine figures emerge. Lorkhan eventually proposes the creation of a mortal world. That proposal gives the story its difficult turn. A world with its own existence requires something from the spirits who undertake it. Creation consumes their substance, and the participants discover that their undertaking has consequences they can't all escape. The ancestors of the Elves become bound up with the world their descendants inhabit.

Magnus has a particular place in this telling. He's associated with the design of the world, but withdraws when the cost of completing it becomes apparent. Other spirits depart as well. In this tradition, the sun is associated with Magnus's departure, while the stars are connected with the spirits who followed him. An Altmer looking upward can therefore understand the sky through an account of beings leaving creation. The lights above Tamriel belong to the same story that explains why mortal existence is limited.

Others remain. The Altmeri account describes Earthbones and the establishment of the conditions under which the world can exist. Y'ffre is associated with this giving of form and stability, a connection especially important in Bosmeri religion. The tale then follows a descent toward mortal ancestry, with later generations diminished from those before them. This helps explain the sorrow running through the Elven version of creation. Life in the world carries an inherited distance from an earlier divine condition, and Lorkhan bears much of the blame for bringing that condition to an end.

The human traditions collected in The Monomyth give the undertaking a different meaning. In its Cyrodilic account, Shezarr speaks in favor of making the world, and the spirits who agree put themselves into the work. They become connected with what they've created and care for it. Here, the expenditure involved in creation can be understood as generosity. The same broad event that an Altmeri account treats as a disastrous diminishment becomes the basis for a relationship between gods and the mortal beings who live within their creation.

That Cyrodilic story also describes spirits who refuse the undertaking. They keep their power and make realms of their own. It associates this refusal with the Daedric Princes, whose dealings with mortals develop after the mortal world exists. This gives a worshipper a way to explain why a Prince can possess an extraordinary realm without being counted among the makers of Mundus. In that account, the Prince's realm belongs to a separate act of creation, undertaken without the same sacrifice attributed to the Aedra.

Lorkhan's fate brings the argument into a place a traveler can actually reach. In the Altmeri telling, the other gods punish him, and his Heart is cast away. In Morrowind, the Heart of Lorkhan lies beneath Red Mountain. The Dwemer sought to use it, and the Tribunal drew divine power from it. Whatever a reader makes of the creation accounts surrounding Lorkhan, the Heart has material consequences for Vvardenfell. Religious history reaches into the mountain, where living people have tried to turn a divine remnant toward their own purposes.

The Heart doesn't settle every disagreement about why the world was made. Its presence does help explain why these stories remain so immediate to the people telling them. A Dunmer living under the Tribunal encounters rulers whose divine status depends upon something connected with creation itself. An Imperial pilgrim may approach the gods through a tradition of sacrifice and continued care. An Altmer can understand mortal limits through an account of ancestral loss. Each begins with a world inhabited by the same peoples, yet arrives at a different account of what life there means.

Aedra and Daedra adds another useful complication. It describes Aedra as bound to the Earth Bones and associates Daedra with a capacity for change. It also distinguishes the mortality of the Aedra from the possibility of banishing Daedra. Those claims help explain the religious categories without turning them into a division between kindly beings and malicious ones. Ancestry and participation in creation are the concerns of the terminology. A mortal still has to consider what a particular being asks of them, and what accepting that request will do.

For someone standing in Tamriel, creation is therefore present in ordinary acts of worship. The name spoken at an altar carries an account of who helped make the world, who withdrew, and who remained outside the undertaking. The books preserve disagreements because the people of Tamriel inherited different explanations of that past. A Prince can enter a mortal's life without belonging to the ancestral company described by Elven religion. That mortal already lives within Mundus, with all its limits, before the Prince makes any offer at all.


At Malacath's shrine in Cyrodiil, the trouble concerns ogres working in Lord Drad's mine. Drad treats them as labor he can possess. Malacath wants them freed. A traveler who accepts the task enters a dispute in which a wealthy landowner's business depends on captivity, while a Daedric Prince demands the captives' release. That can be an awkward introduction for anyone expecting the respectable estate and the Daedric shrine to occupy predictable sides of a moral divide. The ogres have a more immediate concern. They need someone to open their cages.

Malacath's instruction includes a condition that matters to the creatures inside the mine: the traveler mustn't kill the ogres they're supposed to rescue. Completing the task leaves the ogres free, and the balance at Drad's estate changes. Drad and his wife can afterward be found working under the ogres' supervision. The reversal carries Malacath's taste for retribution as well as his concern for those who were mistreated. His intervention helps particular victims, and it also gives their former owners a share of the humiliation they imposed.

The religious book Varieties of Faith places Malacath among the gods acknowledged by the Orcs, and associates him with the ostracized and the sworn oath. Those associations help explain why this mine attracts his attention. The ogres belong within his concern, and Drad has made them vulnerable to someone else's demands. Calling Malacath a protector makes sense here, provided we keep the people he's protecting in view. An Orc who honors him and a noble who has enslaved his creatures have very different reasons to care about his arrival.

Morrowind gives Malacath another public identity. In Tribunal religious teaching, he's one of the Four Corners of the House of Troubles, alongside Mehrunes Dagon, Molag Bal and Sheogorath. These Princes occupy a hostile place in the Dunmer religious world. Their dangers are understood through a tradition in which the Dunmer face tests and enemies. An Orc's reverence for Malacath therefore doesn't translate neatly into approval from a Tribunal priest. The same named Prince appears within communities that have inherited different stories about him and different expectations of his followers.

Even the layout of Morrowind makes that disagreement tangible. Daedric ruins stand across Vvardenfell, and worshippers may occupy their interiors. A traveler approaching one of these places can encounter hostility rather than the welcome available at a town's Tribunal temple. Yet danger at a ruin doesn't explain every person's reasons for worshipping there. The island also contains people whose devotion to a Daedric Prince belongs to established Dunmer religious life. Azura, Boethiah and Mephala have recognized places in traditions that Tribunal teaching itself has had to accommodate.

Molag Bal's shrine in Cyrodiil draws a traveler into a very different intervention. He directs attention toward Melus Petilius, a former fighter who has renounced violence after his wife's death. Melus has withdrawn from his old life, and Molag Bal wants that renunciation broken. The Prince's task requires provoking Melus at his wife's grave until he kills the petitioner with the supplied cursed mace. Molag Bal then restores the petitioner, rewarding a service that has broken Melus's renunciation of violence. Someone accepting this bargain helps a Daedric patron force an unwilling man back into conduct he's deliberately abandoned.

The reward belongs to the person who cooperates with Molag Bal. Melus bears the intrusion. That arrangement is worth keeping distinct from a shrine where worshippers ask for relief from an existing danger. Here, the Prince creates a task out of another person's grief and restraint. His interest in domination takes a recognizable form: he wants Melus to violate his own commitment. The traveler isn't asked to discover whether Melus secretly needs a fight. His refusal to fight is precisely what Molag Bal wants overcome.

These encounters explain why a sphere, the domain associated with a Prince, can't tell a traveler everything about the next request. Malacath's concern for the outcast produces a rescue at Drad's mine. Azura's concern for her followers produces an act of release that those carrying it out may find sorrowful. Molag Bal's interest in domination produces an assault on Melus's chosen way of living. Each domain gives the intervention a recognizable direction. The actual people involved tell us what accepting the task will cost.

Varieties of Faith also describes Azurah within Khajiiti religion, where she has a place in accounts of the Khajiit's origins and their proper form. That setting differs from a Dunmer account of Azura, even though the connection is recognizable. A Khajiit honoring Azurah isn't necessarily approaching her through the Tribunal's categories, and an Orc honoring Malacath isn't borrowing the Dunmer House of Troubles. Worship reaches a mortal through family, community and inherited practice. Before anyone hears a voice at a shrine, those traditions may already have taught them whether approaching it is an act of reverence or a dangerous departure.

A traveler can therefore recognize compassion in the freeing of Malacath's ogres while still examining the next demand on its own terms. Melus's grave gives an equally concrete reason to refuse Molag Bal. Neither decision requires settling every religious dispute in Tamriel. At the shrine, the immediate work is to understand who's asking, who needs help, and who will suffer if the bargain is accepted. The Prince's name matters. So does the person waiting in the mine, or standing beside his wife's grave.


In the Cavern of the Incarnate, the Nerevarine finds people who came before them and failed. Their spirits remain beside the path to Azura's ring, Moon-and-Star. Each had some part of what the prophecy seemed to require, yet none completed the task. Some lacked the strength or judgment to survive it. Others reached the wrong conclusion about their own importance. Azura's promise has already occupied mortal lives by the time the player arrives, and those lives haven't all ended in deliverance.

The ring gives this encounter a particular weight. Moon-and-Star belonged to Nerevar, and the traditions surrounding it say that anyone else who wears it will die. When the player takes it and survives, Azura names them the Nerevarine. The failed incarnates then offer their possessions and speak about their failures. They help someone who can continue where they couldn't. Their presence makes the prophecy feel like something that has passed through individual people, each with limitations, rather than a prediction waiting untouched for its appointed moment.

Azura has already reached the player through dreams. Early in Morrowind, her voice offers reassurance while the prisoner is still traveling toward an unfamiliar country. Later dreams bring Dagoth Ur's invitations and threats into that same private space. The journey involves learning which speaker is approaching and what each wants. Azura points the player toward a task that will take them through Ashlander camps, Tribunal institutions, and the blight beneath Red Mountain. Her guidance doesn't spare them the work of meeting people who have reasons to doubt them.

Nibani Maesa, the wise woman of the Urshilaku, is especially important here. She doesn't accept the newcomer as the finished answer to her people's prophecies. She studies what they bring and explains the trials still ahead. The Nerevarine must earn recognition among communities with their own disputes and expectations. Azura's intervention in the cavern gives the player a divine acknowledgment, but that acknowledgment doesn't make the Ashlander wise women or the leaders of the Great Houses unnecessary. The promise has to become credible within the country it's meant to change.

Behind that promise lies Nerevar's death and the Tribunal's use of the Heart of Lorkhan. In the Ashlander account preserved in Nerevar at Red Mountain, Azura witnesses an oath concerning Kagrenac's tools and the Heart. The Tribunal later use those tools to make themselves divine. That tradition ties their betrayal to the transformation of the Chimer into the Dunmer and to Azura's announcement that Nerevar will return. The change in their skin and eyes becomes part of a remembered religious dispute, carried by descendants who weren't present at Red Mountain.

The accounts of Nerevar's death disagree, including over the Tribunal's responsibility. What the player encounters in the present is more immediate. Vivec acknowledges the Tribunal's dependence on the Heart, and Dagoth Ur's return has prevented them from renewing their power as they once did. Their strength is diminishing while the blight spreads. Azura's old accusation now belongs to a situation in which the living gods need the person their Temple has treated as a threat. Vivec eventually gives the Nerevarine information and tools needed to reach the Heart.

That meeting allows the task to proceed without requiring the player to settle every disputed detail of the ancient battle. Vivec explains how Wraithguard protects its wearer from Kagrenac's tools and describes the blows needed to break the enchantments around the Heart. The Nerevarine must enter Dagoth Ur's stronghold and carry out that work. Azura has guided them toward this confrontation, while the means of completing it pass through a member of the Tribunal. The people caught in the broken oath still have practical knowledge the returning hero needs.

When the Heart is freed from those enchantments and Dagoth Ur falls, Azura appears to the Nerevarine. She acknowledges the fulfillment of the prophecy and gives them a ring. The blight has ended, and the Tribunal can no longer draw power from the Heart. Her promise has reached a recognizable conclusion, though Vvardenfell still contains the people and institutions shaped by everything that preceded it. The Nerevarine has accomplished the task through travel, persuasion, endurance, and help from mortals whose contributions remain their own.

In Cyrodiil's Gutted Mine, Ghola gro-Muzgol's note gives Azura's afflicted followers their own account of the infection and their decision to remain shut away. If the Hero fulfills her request, Azura thanks them for releasing those followers and grants her Star. Beside the Nerevarine's work against the blight, this smaller commission shows how her stated care can still require a mortal to carry out a sorrowful act.


At Boethia's shrine in Cyrodiil, a willing challenger can be sent away from Tamriel to compete in a tournament. The other contestants have come to prove themselves as well. There are rules, opponents and a promised reward, and the traveler enters knowing that the contest is lethal. In Skyrim, someone approaching Boethiah's shrine encounters a different requirement. Before the Prince's favor can be pursued, a companion must be brought to a place of sacrifice. Both encounters concern proving oneself, though the people who bear the cost arrive under very different circumstances.

In Oblivion, the shrine's worshippers explain that an offering of a Daedra heart is needed to approach Boethia. That requirement gives the meeting a deliberate beginning. A traveler has to obtain the offering and bring it to a community already gathered around the Prince. Once addressed, Boethia offers the opportunity to enter the Tournament of Ten Bloods. Accepting means leaving through a portal to an arena in Oblivion, where the challenger will face the other competitors.

The tournament draws its contestants from the different peoples of Tamriel. Within the arena, the traveler proceeds through successive opponents, each seeking the same victory. Their participation gives this trial a recognizable shape. They aren't residents whose village has been chosen for punishment, or companions brought along under another explanation. They are rival champions. Boethia has arranged a contest in which martial ability can be compared directly, and the challenger who survives returns having met the terms announced at the shrine.

Victory earns Goldbrand, a sword associated with Boethia. The reward suits the work that secured it: someone who has prevailed through fighting receives a weapon for further fighting. There's no release from the Prince's favored activity contained in the prize. Goldbrand accompanies its bearer back into Tamriel, where the successful contestant can continue to use what the tournament tested. For this encounter, Boethia's interest is easy to follow. The shrine offers a dangerous opportunity, the traveler accepts, and the contest produces a winner.

Boethiah's Calling in Skyrim begins around a cult whose members also expect strength to be demonstrated. At the Sacellum of Boethiah, east of Windhelm, the priestess describes a test requiring someone who trusts the traveler. That person must be persuaded to accompany them to the shrine and use its Pillar of Sacrifice. If the traveler follows this demand, the companion becomes trapped at the pillar. The instruction depends on the ordinary cooperation that makes traveling together possible.

A follower can carry out a request because they recognize the Dragonborn as someone they're willing to accompany. At the pillar, that familiar arrangement exposes them to betrayal. They haven't entered a tournament after hearing its terms. The Dragonborn has brought them into a ritual whose purpose wasn't shared with them. This makes the Skyrim demand more personal than the Cyrodiil contest. The participant's ability to secure another person's cooperation becomes part of the offering, and the companion pays for having cooperated.

If the sacrifice is completed, Boethiah speaks through the dead follower. The gathered cultists then receive a further test: they must contend with one another until a single survivor remains. Belonging to the shrine's community gives them no exemption. The priestess who explained the ritual and the worshippers who accepted its demands are themselves subject to the Prince's judgment. Their gathering cannot provide lasting shelter from the competition they honor. In this encounter, devotion brings them close enough to be tested, with consequences that reach the whole group.

The survivor is then sent after Boethiah's existing champion, who has displeased the Prince. This champion is at Knifepoint Ridge among bandits, and the task leads the Dragonborn away from the shrine to displace someone who already holds the position being offered. The Ebony Mail belongs to that champion. Once the task is fulfilled and the armor claimed, Boethiah recognizes a replacement. The former favorite's fate gives the new champion a concrete example of how service can end, even after someone has already earned the Prince's favor.

The sequence also changes what victory means across the two games. In Cyrodiil, winning the tournament completes the announced competition. At Knifepoint Ridge, success puts the Dragonborn into an office another mortal has just lost. The armor is useful, but wearing it also identifies the successful claimant with Boethiah's service. The Prince has assessed the cultists, rejected a champion and selected another. Someone accepting the reward has participated in that process from the sacrifice onward.

Dunmer religious writing places Boethiah within a much older account of how a people should live. In The Anticipations, the Tribunal Temple names Boethiah as the anticipation of Almalexia. The book credits Boethiah with teaching the Chimer and associates the Prince with their departure from the ancestral traditions of the elves. Within that religious account, conflict with an inherited way of life belongs to the people's beginnings. Boethiah is remembered as a teacher whose lessons helped make a distinct culture possible.

Varieties of Faith likewise gives Boethiah a broad place in Chimer tradition, including associations with philosophy, warfare and the establishment of their society. These accounts help explain why a Dunmer worshipper might approach the Prince with reverence. The worshipper has inherited stories about instruction and collective achievement alongside stories of conspiracy and betrayal. A shrine visitor in Skyrim meets one cult's immediate demand. A Dunmer reading these religious histories encounters a patron woven into explanations of where their people's customs came from.

Those traditions give Boethiah's trials a religious setting without making the sacrificed companion a willing contestant. The distinction remains there in the actions themselves. Cyrodiil's challenger enters an announced tournament. Skyrim's follower obeys someone they have agreed to accompany, while the cultists discover that their own lives are also available for testing. At Knifepoint Ridge, the previous champion has already lost the patron's approval. If the Dragonborn takes the Ebony Mail, they leave wearing the armor of a servant Boethiah ordered them to replace.


Beneath the Arena in Vivec, the Morag Tong has a headquarters that a visitor can easily miss. Reaching it takes the player away from the city's public walkways and into its lower passages. Inside, Eno Hlaalu receives those who want to join an organization devoted to sanctioned assassination. Mephala belongs to the religious life of this guild. Here, her association with murder has an established place in Dunmer society, complete with ranks, assignments, and documents that members can present to the guards.

A Morag Tong writ names someone the guild has authorized its members to kill. If a guard confronts the assassin over an authorized killing, presenting the appropriate writ can settle the matter. That arrangement gives the guild an unusual position within Morrowind. The same authorities who pursue an ordinary murderer can recognize a Tong execution as lawful. For the person named on the document, the distinction offers little comfort. For the assassin, it determines whether the journey back through a city ends in arrest.

Joining the Tong begins with a killing as well. Eno Hlaalu sends the applicant after Feruren Oran, who can be found at the Elven Nations Cornerclub in Vivec. The assignment brings the guild's work into a place where people gather and conduct their ordinary affairs. Once accepted, the player receives further writs against named individuals. The work requires finding particular people within the settlements and households of Vvardenfell. An assassin has to learn where someone belongs before carrying out the commission against them.

The book The Black Glove describes the Morag Tong's worship of Mephala and presents the guild's killings as religious acts. It's the guild's own account of its purpose, and that devotion matters to how its members understand their work. A writ can carry civic permission while the execution also carries sacred meaning for the person performing it. Mephala's worship has therefore become part of an institution whose business passes through both government and religion. The guild keeps its headquarters concealed, even though its authority can be acknowledged in public.

The Tong's conflict with the Dark Brotherhood adds another connection to this religious setting. In Morrowind, the player can undertake work against the rival organization, and Eno Hlaalu has concerns beyond the names on routine writs. The guild protects its own position and pursues enemies within the same profession. That gives service to Mephala a practical daily shape. Members receive instructions from a superior, advance through a hierarchy, and act on behalf of an organization with rivals. Their devotion comes with obligations to other mortals who can judge their performance.

In Skyrim, Mephala reaches into a household through a much smaller opening. At Dragonsreach, Balgruuf's son Nelkir has been listening to a voice behind a locked door. The trouble comes to the player's attention through reports about the Jarl's children, and Balgruuf can direct the player toward his son. Nelkir describes the Whispering Lady as someone who tells him things. Among them is a claim about his parentage. The game gives that information through the boy's account of what the voice has said, so it remains part of the confidence she's cultivated with him.

Nelkir's interest is understandable within the situation. He's a child in the Jarl's residence, surrounded by adults whose conversations and decisions aren't all open to him. The voice offers access to household secrets and encourages his resentment toward his father. Mephala has found a listener who lives close to the person governing Whiterun. She doesn't need Nelkir to hold an official position. His place in the family gives her a way into Dragonsreach, and his curiosity brings another person to her door.

When the player speaks at the door, Mephala directs them toward the Ebony Blade locked beyond it. Nelkir knows that Balgruuf and the court wizard, Farengar Secret-Fire, have keys. Following that information means taking a key from someone within the residence and opening a space the household has kept shut. The quest draws the player through a short chain of confidences. Balgruuf asks for help with his son, the son shares what he's learned, and the hidden speaker uses that access to arrange the weapon's release.

The chamber contains a written warning about the blade. It describes efforts to destroy the weapon at the Skyforge and urges that it remain concealed. The sword has already caused enough concern for someone to shut it away and leave an explanation beside it. By the time the player reaches it, the Whispering Lady has supplied a different reason to enter that room. She wants the blade taken up again. The warning and Mephala's invitation meet in the same enclosed place, with the player able to read one while considering the other.

The Ebony Blade absorbs health from those it strikes, and Mephala explains how its enchantment can regain strength. It feeds on the deaths of people who regard its wielder as a friend. That condition ties the weapon's improvement to relationships the player has formed elsewhere. Someone helped through a quest, or won over through a favor, may qualify because of that goodwill. The invitation gives an ugly use to work that previously made a person's life easier. Mephala asks the wielder to turn an existing bond into nourishment for her artifact.

Taking the sword doesn't require the player to carry out that further demand. They can leave its enchantment at its initial strength and decline to feed it through betrayal. If they do pursue its improvement, Mephala responds as the blade grows stronger. The bargain is specific enough that its consequence is difficult to mistake. The weapon rewards the destruction of friendly ties, and its patron encourages the wielder along that course. Nothing about finding the chamber makes those later deaths an unavoidable part of the story.

Eno Hlaalu hands an assassin a document naming an authorized target. The voice in Dragonsreach sends a visitor toward a sword that rewards turning on friends. These are different encounters with Mephala, shaped by the mortals and customs around her. In Vivec, her worshippers have built a guild that gives killings a recognized procedure. In Whiterun, her influence passes through a child's confidences and a stolen key. Once the door is open, the player can carry the Ebony Blade back into a city full of people they've already helped.


In Raven Rock, Elder Othreloth tends a temple where Azura, Boethiah and Mephala receive public worship. A visitor arriving from the mainland might associate Daedric shrines with remote places and small gatherings outside ordinary civic life. Here, the temple belongs to the settlement's daily religious arrangements. Its priests care for the dead as well as the living. The Princes honored inside have a recognized place in Dunmer tradition, and Othreloth explains that place through a change that has already overtaken the religion of Morrowind.

He calls them the Reclamations. The name refers to Azura, Boethiah and Mephala recovering the standing they held before the ascendancy of the Tribunal. For a long period, the public religion of Morrowind centered on Almalexia, Sotha Sil and Vivec, three beings worshipped as living gods. By the time the Dragonborn reaches Raven Rock, the temple teaches that these former rulers were false gods. Othreloth speaks as a priest of the restored faith, with a clear judgment about the religious order that came before his own.

That judgment would have sounded very different inside the Temple encountered in Morrowind. In that game, Vivec still inhabits his palace, pilgrims travel between sacred sites, and priests teach a faith organized around the Tribunal's continuing presence. The pilgrimage described in The Pilgrim's Path takes worshippers to places associated with divine deeds and Dunmer history. A pilgrim makes offerings and receives blessings. The Tribunal's standing is built into journeys across Vvardenfell, into the work of its priests, and into the stories people are expected to know.

Azura, Boethiah and Mephala haven't vanished from that older Temple's teaching. The Anticipations gives each a place in relation to a member of the Tribunal. Azura anticipates Sotha Sil, Boethiah anticipates Almalexia, and Mephala anticipates Vivec. Within this account, their earlier teaching prepares the way for the gods the Temple now worships. The book allows the Temple to acknowledge the Princes' importance to the ancestors of the Dunmer while placing the Tribunal above them in the religious order. Someone reading it encounters an explanation for why older patrons still deserve recognition.

This arrangement also distinguishes the three Princes from the figures the Temple identifies as the House of Troubles. Religious texts place Mehrunes Dagon, Molag Bal, Malacath and Sheogorath in that adversarial group. Their opposition and the hardships associated with them have a place in Temple teaching, too. Dunmer religion therefore doesn't gather every Daedric Prince into one shared position. A priest can revere Mephala's ancient instruction while describing another Prince through stories of danger and testing. Even under the Tribunal, the particular identity of the Prince matters.

Outside the Temple's approved account, the Ashlanders preserve another understanding of the past. Their wise women carry traditions concerning Nerevar, the Tribunal and the return promised through the Nerevarine prophecies. In Morrowind, the Nerevarine must deal with these communities directly, including Sul-Matuul and Nibani Maesa among the Urshilaku. These encounters reveal religious disagreements within Dunmer society while the Tribunal Temple still holds public authority. An Ashlander camp and a Temple establishment can share names from the sacred past and tell sharply different stories about what those names mean.

The Dissident Priests bring another dispute inside the Temple's own world. At Holamayan, Gilvas Barelo preserves and explains writings that challenge the approved history. During the main quest, those records help the Nerevarine investigate claims that established religious authorities have rejected. This matters to the later Reclamations because the Tribunal's account was being contested before Raven Rock's priests pronounced it false. Doubt had specific keepers and specific places of refuge. It survived in manuscripts, in conversations with priests, and in traditions passed down beyond the Temple's usual reach.

The events beneath Red Mountain then change the material circumstances of Tribunal worship. The Nerevarine breaks the enchantments binding the Heart of Lorkhan, ending the source from which the Tribunal renewed their divine power. Vivec explains his dependence on that source during the main quest. After Dagoth Ur's defeat, the Tribunal can no longer maintain their old position in the same way. Azura appears to the Nerevarine at the conclusion of that struggle. The patron whose prophecies the Temple resisted has an immediate presence in the outcome.

Raven Rock's temple belongs to the religious world that follows this decline. Othreloth presents the return to the three Princes as a recovery of proper worship. From his position, the Reclamations have resumed their rightful place after the Tribunal's deception. The name itself carries that claim of restoration. Yet the earlier arrangements remain visible in the texts and encounters of Morrowind. A reader can follow how Azura moves from the Temple's account of an anticipation to the central place she holds in the later temple, without treating those two settings as interchangeable.

The Tribunal also remains within the later religion, though its position has changed. Almalexia, Sotha Sil and Vivec are remembered as saints rather than worshipped as the ruling gods of the Dunmer. That leaves room for their historical and religious significance to continue after the rejection of their divine claims. For a community inheriting generations of Tribunal devotion, this is a substantial adjustment. The names attached to its older sacred stories survive, while the priests teach a different account of who deserves worship and how the former gods should be regarded.

Othreloth's responsibilities make the restored religion tangible. In Raven Rock's temple quest, ash spawn have entered the ancestral tomb, and he needs someone to clear them out. If the Dragonborn helps, the service addresses a problem in the community's burial place. The priest's concern reaches beyond explaining which divine beings have precedence. The dead require care, and the living need access to a place where their ancestors are honored. Under the Reclamations, that familiar Dunmer obligation continues. Raven Rock's restored faith has a tomb to tend, and Othreloth is responsible for tending it.


At Kvatch, an Oblivion Gate stands outside a city that its surviving residents can no longer enter safely. The refugees have gathered below, while Savlian Matius and the remaining guards hold their position near the approach. Beyond them, Daedra occupy the ruined streets. Mehrunes Dagon's arrival in ordinary life looks like this: people separated from their homes, soldiers unable to recover their city, and a road that now ends at an opening into another world. Before anyone reaches the Prince himself, his invasion has already changed where people can live.

Dagon's sphere includes destruction, revolution, change, and ambition. Those associations help explain why his worship can attract people who want an existing order swept away. In Oblivion, however, the consequences reach people who haven't asked for any transformation. Kvatch's residents lose their city because a movement devoted to Dagon has worked to admit his forces into Tamriel. The distance between a cult's promised new world and the experience of the people already living there is visible in the refugee camp.

Passing through the gate takes the player into the Deadlands, Dagon's realm of Oblivion. Lava divides stretches of scorched ground, and towers rise above an environment hostile to a mortal visitor. The creatures encountered there include beings deployed against Tamriel, while the structures provide a way for the invasion to continue. A gate isn't a brief vision or a message delivered through a shrine. Someone can cross it, travel through the place on the other side, and return. So can the forces assembled there.

Within the towers, Sigil Stones sustain the connections between the worlds. Removing the stone closes the gate and returns the player to Tamriel. That gives the defender at Kvatch something practical to accomplish, even when the power behind the attack seems far beyond a city's guards. Savlian can't march his remaining people into a secure city while the gate continues to admit enemies. Once the opening is shut, the effort to reclaim Kvatch can proceed. The Prince's invasion has a structure that mortals can disrupt, one opening at a time.

The larger assault depends on preparations made within the Empire. The Mythic Dawn, led by Mankar Camoran, worships Dagon and works toward the collapse of the barriers keeping him out. Its agents assassinate Emperor Uriel Septim VII and his known heirs. The theft of the Amulet of Kings then obstructs the restoration of the Dragonfires, whose protection is bound to the imperial covenant with Akatosh. These acts connect a hidden religious movement to the vulnerability of the whole Empire. Palace murder and a burning provincial city belong to the same campaign.

The cult's writings give that campaign a religious explanation. The Mysterium Xarxes is attributed to Mehrunes Dagon, and Mankar Camoran's Commentaries present its teachings through his own interpretation. For an outsider investigating the Mythic Dawn, the Commentaries also contain clues that lead toward the cult. Books circulate through recognizable mortal channels, including a bookseller in the Imperial City, even though the movement they describe seeks an upheaval far beyond the city. Dagon's followers recruit and conceal themselves among the people whose world they intend to remake.

At the shrine in Lake Arrius Caverns, that commitment has become an organized community. Mankar addresses his followers before departing through a portal with the Amulet of Kings. Harrow receives the player as a prospective initiate, and the prisoner Jeelius is presented for sacrifice. If the player refuses to kill him and manages to free him, Jeelius can survive and return to the Temple of the One. His presence gives the cult's worship an immediate mortal cost. Someone has been captured and brought underground so that another person's admission into the group can be marked by his death.

Mankar's promised Paradise develops the same bargain on a much larger scale. He uses the Mysterium Xarxes to create the realm of Gaiar Alata, where the player eventually pursues him. It contains beautiful surroundings, but it also contains followers who suffer, die, and return to suffer again. Eldamil, a former member of the Mythic Dawn, helps the player there after becoming disillusioned. His account and his circumstances expose what service has brought him. The reward offered to the faithful has placed them somewhere their leader can continue to subject them to torment.

Mankar also offers an account of the world's origin that makes Mundus the realm of Lorkhan, whom he describes as a betrayed Daedric ruler. He casts the gods worshipped by the Empire as usurpers. That's Mankar's religious claim, spoken by the leader conducting the invasion. It helps explain how he can present the destruction of cities as a restoration. Within his teaching, the people resisting Dagon defend a stolen arrangement. For Savlian and the survivors of Kvatch, resisting means recovering streets and buildings where they lived before the attack.

Martin Septim ultimately stops Dagon's entry into the Imperial City by breaking the Amulet of Kings and becoming Akatosh's avatar. Dagon is driven back, but Martin is lost, leaving a stone dragon at the Temple of the One and an Empire without a Septim emperor.

That ending gives the invasion a lasting place in ordinary geography. Kvatch has been devastated, the imperial succession has been broken, and the Temple of the One contains the visible remnant of Martin's final act. Dagon's followers sought a remade world through the removal of its rulers and protections. They failed to deliver Tamriel to their Prince, though the damage they caused remains part of the world that survives. For the refugees below Kvatch, the closing of the gate is the beginning of a difficult return to a city whose homes and defenders have already been lost.


In Markarth, Vigilant Tyranus stands outside an abandoned house, asking whether anyone has seen people entering or leaving. He suspects Daedra worship inside. If the Dragonborn agrees to help him search, an ordinary doorway in the city leads into Molag Bal's reach. There's no journey across Oblivion at the beginning of this encounter. Tyranus enters as a Vigilant of Stendarr investigating a possible threat, with someone beside him who has agreed to assist. Inside, the investigation becomes a situation neither visitor can leave on the terms they expected.

Objects begin to move, and a voice directs the intruders toward violence. Tyranus recognizes that something more dangerous than a hidden worshipper is present. The exit won't provide the escape he wants, and Molag Bal tells the Dragonborn that only one of them will leave alive. Tyranus eventually attacks. If the player continues the quest, the Dragonborn kills the man who brought them inside. Before Molag Bal asks for any formal service, he's already made cooperation between the visitors collapse. Tyranus's religious office and his awareness of Daedric danger don't protect him once he's inside the house.

Below the living rooms, a shrine waits with a rusty mace. When the Dragonborn approaches the weapon, a cage closes around them. Molag Bal now addresses a captive who has followed his directions deeper into the building. The mace belongs to him, and he wants it restored through the punishment of a particular mortal. He releases the Dragonborn to find that man. This release gives the player a task outside the house, while leaving the shrine and its waiting weapon as the place where the arrangement must be completed.

The man Molag Bal wants is Logrolf the Willful, a priest of Boethiah. According to Molag Bal, Logrolf has desecrated his altar and performed rites there in service to the rival Prince. His anger has a specific religious setting. Someone devoted to Boethiah has entered a shrine of Molag Bal and treated it as a place where another patron can be honored at his expense. Molag Bal intends to answer that insult by forcing the priest himself to submit. Repairing the altar alone wouldn't satisfy the demand he gives the Dragonborn.

Logrolf is being held by Forsworn when the Dragonborn finds him. If freed, he assumes Boethiah has had a hand in his rescue. He can be persuaded to return to Markarth, where he expects to confront the presence beneath the house. That expectation makes his return especially grim. The person who releases him from one captivity is bringing him toward another, and his confidence in his patron doesn't prepare him for what awaits. At the shrine, Molag Bal confines him and orders the Dragonborn to use the rusty mace against him.

The punishment continues beyond what Logrolf expects a mortal body to endure. Molag Bal revives him and orders the ordeal repeated until the priest abandons his resistance and pledges his soul. Only after that submission does Molag Bal demand his death and restore the mace as the Dragonborn's reward. The quest gives the artifact a history the player has participated in. Its restoration involves making a named priest renounce the allegiance that brought him into conflict with Molag Bal. The weapon leaves the house after its patron has obtained that surrender.

Molag Bal's association with domination becomes quite concrete here. He traps Tyranus and the Dragonborn together, turns their fear into a fight, and then uses the survivor to fetch Logrolf. Each action makes the following demand possible. Even the rescue among the Forsworn becomes part of his purpose once Logrolf returns to the altar. Coldharbour, the realm associated with Molag Bal, gives his worship a destination beyond Tamriel. In the abandoned house, however, he achieves what he wants through a locked exit, a shrine, and mortals made to act against one another.

Dawnguard approaches Molag Bal through a family whose connection to him reaches much further into their past. Serana explains that she and her parents worshipped him before becoming vampires. Their condition began in a religious life they had already entered, and she describes the family's transformation as an ordeal. She doesn't welcome questioning about every part of it. Her account leaves enough to understand that being granted vampirism by Molag Bal can carry lasting suffering, even within a family that sought his favor and regarded him as its patron.

Harkon speaks about that past differently. In his own account, his devotion and sacrifices won Molag Bal's gift of pure vampirism for himself, Valerica and Serana. He presents the result as an extraordinary elevation. At Castle Volkihar, that belief has a visible setting: Harkon rules a vampire court and can offer the Dragonborn the Vampire Lord transformation. If the player accepts, entry into that form comes through him. Harkon's pride helps explain why he treats the family's origin as a source of standing. Serana's reluctance makes the cost of that same history harder to overlook.

The family doesn't remain united by its shared patron or its shared transformation. Valerica hides Serana away with an Elder Scroll because she fears what Harkon's pursuit of the prophecy will mean for their daughter. Harkon seeks a way to remove the threat sunlight poses to vampires, and Serana becomes endangered by the plan he builds around that desire. Valerica withdraws to the Soul Cairn, while Serana eventually emerges from Dimhollow Crypt into a world changed during her absence. Molag Bal's gift hasn't given these people a secure household or a common purpose.

Serana can accompany the Dragonborn in opposition to Harkon, and the Dawnguard story brings his plans to an end. Her continued presence allows her family's religious past to be heard from someone who has to live with its consequences. In Markarth, Molag Bal rewards a service that forces Logrolf to surrender his allegiance. At Castle Volkihar, Harkon takes pride in a gift that his daughter remembers with pain. Serana survives him, carrying her own account of how the family became what it is.


Meridia's Beacon can turn an ordinary search through a chest into a summons. In Skyrim, taking the object brings the Prince's voice directly to the Dragonborn. She wants her Beacon returned to her shrine at Mount Kilkreath, where a necromancer has occupied her temple. The object has a particular use there, and Meridia expects the person who finds it to carry out the work. Her light will help clear the temple, though accepting that help means entering a place where the dead have already been put to someone else's purpose.

The shrine stands above the road in Haafingar, with a statue of Meridia overlooking the approach. Beneath it lies the temple she wants reclaimed. When the Dragonborn returns the Beacon to its pedestal, Meridia raises them high above the ground and explains the intrusion. Malkoran has established himself in her sanctuary. According to Meridia, he uses the dead left by Skyrim's war to sustain his work, trapping souls within the desecrated temple. Her complaint concerns necromancy, the fate of those souls, and the violation of a place dedicated to her.

Meridia's description gives the task a clear direction. The Dragonborn must enter the temple, guide her light through it, and destroy Malkoran. She can speak, lift her prospective agent into the air, and send light into the building. She still requires someone to move through its passages and deal with the necromancer inside. That arrangement makes the quest feel different from a priest asking for help on a god's behalf. Here the patron gives the instructions herself, while the difficult journey through the sanctuary remains the mortal's responsibility.

Inside Kilkreath, the war has a physical presence. Desecrated corpses lie throughout the ruins, and hostile corrupted shades occupy the passages. These aren't distant casualties mentioned in a report from a battlefield. Their remains have been brought into a religious building and caught up in Malkoran's necromancy. The gold that can be taken from the bodies adds an uncomfortable, practical detail to the journey. Even while pursuing the person exploiting the dead, an adventurer can search those same remains for money. The temple doesn't separate its sacred business from the habits of dungeon exploration.

The route depends on a series of pedestals that carry Meridia's light farther into the complex. Activating them opens the way through the ruins, so restoring the temple is something the Dragonborn does room by room. The light also creates hazards along its path. Its presence doesn't make every part of the sanctuary safe for the person helping her. Kilkreath remains a damaged, occupied place that has to be negotiated with care. Meridia supplies the means of reaching Malkoran, and the Dragonborn has to make that means work inside the building.

Malkoran waits in the inner sanctuary among corrupted shades. His defeat requires more than overcoming his living body, because a shade of Malkoran emerges after he falls. The necromancy that has filled the temple extends to its occupant's own final resistance. Once that shade is defeated, the Dragonborn can approach Dawnbreaker, the weapon Meridia has promised. The sequence keeps the reward tied to the condition of the sanctuary. Malkoran and the hostile presence around him must be dealt with before the sword can be claimed and the assignment completed.

Dawnbreaker carries Meridia's opposition to the undead beyond Mount Kilkreath. Its enchantment burns those it strikes, and when it kills undead, it can produce an explosion that harms or drives away others nearby. A sword taken from the reclaimed temple can therefore continue the work that brought the Dragonborn there. Against a group of draugr or other undead, its special effect has an obvious practical value. Meridia has given her agent an artifact suited to repeating a particular kind of service wherever the road leads.

After the cleansing, Meridia speaks of spreading her influence and carrying her light into the world. The Dragonborn's responses can be willing or reluctant, but she treats the completed deed as useful to her regardless. Someone who entered Kilkreath because necromancy troubled them has also restored a Daedric sanctuary and recovered a Prince's weapon. Someone interested chiefly in Dawnbreaker has accomplished the same restoration. Meridia doesn't require those motives to be identical. Her immediate concern is that Malkoran has been removed and that an armed mortal can now take her influence elsewhere.

Her shrine quest in Oblivion gives that concern another local setting. At the shrine in Cyrodiil, an offering drawn from undead remains allows the petitioner to approach her. Meridia then directs the Hero of Kvatch toward necromancers in Howling Cave. They're disturbing the dead, and she wants them destroyed. There's no occupied Mount Kilkreath in this encounter, no Beacon to recover, and no chain of light to guide through a temple. The offense still falls within the same recognizable concern: necromancers have made use of the dead, and Meridia recruits a mortal to intervene.

Howling Cave brings the petitioner into the company of the necromancers and the undead around them. Completing Meridia's demand earns the Ring of Khajiiti. Its enchantments increase speed and provide a chameleon effect, helping its wearer move quickly and become harder to see. That's a different reward from Dawnbreaker, with no need for every gift to reproduce the same power. In Skyrim she equips someone to fight undead with a radiant sword. In Cyrodiil, she rewards completed service with an artifact useful for movement and concealment.

Taken together, these encounters explain why a mortal might welcome Meridia's intervention. Malkoran's victims gain nothing from his occupation of Kilkreath, and the necromancers in Howling Cave have made the dead part of their practice. Serving Meridia can end those abuses. Her demands remain specific, though. In Kilkreath, she directs the restoration of her sanctuary, determines the enemy to be removed, and sends Dawnbreaker out with its new bearer. The Dragonborn leaves with a useful weapon after doing something that benefits the dead. Meridia has also regained her temple, and her light now travels with the person who answered her summons.


Sinding is locked inside the jail at Falkreath when the Dragonborn meets him. Outside, a family is mourning the child he killed. He admits responsibility, and his account leads to a ring he stole from Hircine, the Daedric Prince of the hunt. Sinding had hoped the ring would help him manage his transformations into a werewolf. According to him, Hircine cursed it in response to the theft, making those transformations unpredictable. By the time he reaches Falkreath, the thing he sought as protection has become another danger to everyone around him.

He passes the ring to the Dragonborn and asks for help seeking Hircine's forgiveness. Accepting it brings the player into the consequences of his bargain, since the cursed ring can cause involuntary transformations in a wearer who has lycanthropy. Sinding then transforms and escapes confinement. The conversation began with a prisoner who could explain his predicament in human speech. It ends with a werewolf loose beyond the jail, while the Dragonborn carries the object that connected him to the Prince.

Sinding directs the Dragonborn toward a great beast whose death will draw Hircine's attention. This is a white stag, encountered outside Falkreath. When it dies, Hircine appears through an aspect of the animal. The Prince answers the appeal concerning the ring by assigning a hunt of his own. Sinding has fled to Bloated Man's Grotto, and Hircine wants him killed and skinned. The request for relief has brought the Dragonborn into a position where removing a curse means pursuing the person who first asked for help.

Bloated Man's Grotto already contains other hunters when the Dragonborn arrives. Sinding has become their quarry, and the surviving encounter makes clear that hunting a werewolf carries risks for the pursuers. Hircine's followers have entered the grotto expecting to take a trophy, but Sinding can kill them. His strength hasn't released him from the hunt. It has made him a dangerous target, the kind of quarry whose defeat would give the successful hunter something worth presenting to a Prince.

The Dragonborn can follow Hircine's demand and kill Sinding. In that branch, taking his skin brings another appearance by the Prince, and the reward is the Savior's Hide. The armor has a direct connection to the creature pursued through the grotto. Sinding's attempt to escape the curse ends with his death, and the person who heard his plea becomes the hunter who completes Hircine's task. The bereaved family in Falkreath remains part of this story too. Understanding Sinding's affliction doesn't undo the child's death.

There's another resolution if the Dragonborn agrees to help Sinding survive. Together, they can turn against the hunters inside the grotto. Once those pursuers are dead, Sinding intends to remain there, away from people he might harm. Hircine then appears outside and accepts this result as well. He removes the curse from the ring and allows the Dragonborn to keep it. His satisfaction suggests that reversing the hunt still meets his interests when the supposed quarry and an ally overcome the hunters sent against it.

This makes Hircine's response unusually revealing. The Dragonborn can spare the target he named and still receive his favor through a different outcome. Sinding survives in that branch because the people hunting him have been defeated. Hircine appreciates the successful hunt even after its direction changes. The uncursed ring can grant additional werewolf transformations to a wearer with lycanthropy, so the reward remains tied to the condition Sinding struggled with. It doesn't turn him back into an ordinary man or erase what brought him to Falkreath.

Bloodmoon gives the hunt a larger ritual setting on Solstheim. During its main story, werewolves attack the Skaal village, and the Nerevarine contracts lycanthropy. The player can seek a cure or accept life as a werewolf, with different tasks following that decision. Hircine's presence therefore reaches the Nerevarine through an affliction that changes how the character can live among other people. A human community has suffered an attack before the Prince's ritual draws its participants together.

The Skaal understand the approaching events through their tradition of the Bloodmoon Prophecy. Korst Wind-Eye interprets signs that foretell Hircine's hunt, including the red moon associated with its arrival. Their knowledge belongs to people living on the island where the hunt will happen. For the inhabitants of Solstheim, this is a threat to their community, with missing people and werewolf attacks giving the prophecy immediate consequences. Hircine's chosen ground lies beneath Mortrag Glacier, where the Nerevarine is eventually taken into a maze.

Other captives inside include Captain Falx Carius, the Skaal leader Tharsten Heart-Fang, and the frost giant Karstaag. They come from different parts of Solstheim, and their presence brings the island's military outpost, Skaal village, and remote castle into the same deadly contest. Within the maze, cooperation can offer temporary help, while the conditions of the hunt keep placing participants in danger. Tharsten's own connection to lycanthropy emerges there. The Nerevarine has to pass through a gathering in which another captive may also become an opponent.

At the end, Hircine confronts the surviving Nerevarine and offers a contest against one of his aspects. The player's answer about a hunter's greatest quality determines whether that aspect embodies strength, speed, or guile, with a different reward attached to each. Hircine makes those qualities part of the ritual itself. The mortal who has endured his werewolves and maze must now face a form of the Prince suited to the quality selected. Surviving that encounter completes the hunt and allows the Nerevarine to leave the glacier.

Beside this elaborate ritual, Sinding's grotto is a smaller place, with a much more personal request at its beginning. Yet both stories repeatedly change who occupies the dangerous position. The Nerevarine may carry lycanthropy into a hunt arranged for powerful captives. Sinding can flee his pursuers, defeat them with help, or die at the Dragonborn's hands. Hircine rewards successful participants in either pursuit. If Sinding survives, he remains in the grotto, with the hunters gone and his own transformations still something he must live with.


Lod, the blacksmith in Falkreath, is looking for a dog. He asks the Dragonborn to find the animal outside town, and the errand seems ordinary enough until the dog begins speaking. Barbas has a problem with his master, Clavicus Vile, and wants help settling their disagreement. He can speak for himself, explain what he wants, and lead the way to a shrine. Before the Dragonborn has met the Prince, his estranged companion has already made the first request.

Barbas describes Vile as a Daedric Prince associated with wishes and bargains. His own situation gives that introduction a practical complication. He and Vile have quarreled, and Vile has sent him away. Barbas wants to return, but he needs someone else to help bring them together. The Dragonborn becomes involved as an intermediary in a dispute that began before their arrival. The talking dog knows his master well enough to expect difficulties, even while pursuing a reunion with him.

Their journey leads to Haemar's Shame, where vampires occupy the caves around Vile's shrine. When the Dragonborn reaches him, Vile explains that these worshippers had asked him for a cure. He treats their deaths at the Dragonborn's hands as fulfillment of that request. The account belongs to Vile, and his satisfaction is part of what makes it unsettling. For the vampires, a wish to escape their condition has ended in destruction. Vile can describe the outcome as a service because he decides how broadly to interpret what they asked.

The Prince initially welcomes the prospect of granting a reward, but his tone changes when Barbas's return becomes the subject. He doesn't want his companion back. Barbas, meanwhile, speaks as someone entitled to argue with him. Their exchange gives the shrine an unusual character. The Dragonborn hasn't arrived before a solitary figure whose followers merely repeat his instructions. Vile's own companion disputes his judgment, and their separation has left the Prince with diminished power. Barbas is part of the power Vile needs, despite their ability to speak and act apart.

Vile proposes a task involving the Rueful Axe, which is held by the mage Sebastian Lort at Rimerock Burrow. Bringing it back will settle the matter of Barbas's return. Barbas also supplies an account of the weapon's origin. According to him, Lort's daughter became a werewolf, and her father appealed to Vile for relief from his distress. The axe was the Prince's answer. That story leaves the father with a weapon when he had sought help for his daughter, and it gives the errand a troubling context before the Dragonborn reaches Lort.

The journey to Rimerock Burrow puts distance between the request at the shrine and its eventual terms. The Dragonborn must retrieve the axe from Lort before Vile presents his final proposal. Once the weapon is returned, the Prince offers a different arrangement: use it to kill Barbas, and keep it as a reward. What began as assistance with a reunion has become an invitation to remove one of the participants. Vile now asks the Dragonborn to turn on the companion who brought them to him.

Barbas objects and urges the Dragonborn to reject the offer. He argues that Vile will take him back if the killing is refused, and he identifies the Masque of Clavicus Vile as the reward to seek. His advice is useful, though he has an immediate interest in the decision. He wants to survive and return to his master. The Dragonborn therefore hears two incompatible requests from beings whose power belongs together. They share a connection close enough that the dispute concerns Vile's own strength, yet neither speaks on behalf of both.

If the Dragonborn refuses to kill Barbas, Vile eventually accepts the reunion and grants the Masque. Barbas returns to him, and the axe is surrendered. If the player instead accepts Vile's proposal and kills the dog, the Rueful Axe becomes the reward. Vile says Barbas will eventually return anyway. That claim makes the proposed killing seem especially mean-spirited: Vile describes an outcome that won't permanently solve his complaint, while asking a mortal to perform it. The two branches leave the Dragonborn with different artifacts and different participation in the quarrel.

An earlier encounter in Oblivion gives Barbas a similar role beside a different bargain. At Vile's shrine in Cyrodiil, the Prince asks the Hero of Kvatch to recover Umbra, a sword associated with capturing souls. During the quest, Barbas speaks through a hound statuette placed in the Hero's possession. He warns against bringing the weapon back. Once again, the mortal who accepts Vile's task receives advice from within the Prince's own circle that runs against the task's completion.

The search leads through Pell's Gate, where Irroke the Wide remembers Lenwin, his former apprentice. His account connects the sword to a particular person's altered life. Lenwin has left that earlier identity behind, and the Hero finds her in the ruin of Vindasel calling herself Umbra. She won't freely surrender the sword. Recovering it requires killing its bearer, so Vile's request draws the Hero into a confrontation with someone whose attachment to the weapon has displaced the name and life Irroke remembers.

If the Hero obtains Umbra, they can give the sword to Vile in exchange for his Masque or refuse the exchange and keep it. Barbas's warning makes the return to the shrine more complicated than delivering a requested object. In Skyrim, he wants reunion with Vile and argues against the axe's use. In Oblivion, he tries to prevent a delivery Vile has requested. Across these encounters, Barbas repeatedly intervenes in the bargain itself, with knowledge and preferences the mortal wouldn't receive from Vile alone.

Clavicus Vile doesn't need every wish to follow an identical trick for these encounters to make sense together. The vampires at Haemar's Shame, Sebastian Lort in Barbas's account, and the adventurers sent after his artifacts each approach a particular request. Vile answers through the circumstances available to him, and the terms can change as the errand proceeds. At his Skyrim shrine, the question finally becomes whether the Dragonborn will use the returned axe against Barbas. Refusing leaves the dog reunited with his master and the Masque in mortal hands.


Sam Guevenne offers the Dragonborn a drinking contest in a tavern, with a staff as the prize. He looks like a man prepared to share an evening's entertainment, and the invitation doesn't require a pilgrimage or an offering at a shrine. If the Dragonborn accepts and continues drinking, the contest ends in a blackout. The next clear moment comes in the Temple of Dibella in Markarth, where Senna is waiting for an explanation. Sam is gone, the staff hasn't appeared, and the Dragonborn has apparently been busy enough to leave obligations scattered across Skyrim.

Senna's concern is the temple. She describes the Dragonborn's disruptive behavior and expects the mess to be cleaned up. To someone who remembers sitting with Sam, this is a difficult place to begin: a priestess recognizes them, has a complaint against them, and knows something about their movements that they don't know themselves. Agreeing to help gives the search a practical starting point. The Dragonborn can collect the scattered refuse and apologize, though persuasion or payment can also settle the encounter. Senna then directs them toward Rorikstead.

In Rorikstead, Ennis accuses the Dragonborn of stealing his goat, Gleda, and selling her to a giant. His account gives the search an unexpectedly rural turn. The Dragonborn began with a promised staff and now faces the possibility of retrieving livestock from a buyer who has no obvious reason to surrender it. If they agree to recover Gleda, they must find her near the giant Grok and bring her back to Ennis. What sounded like a passing act of drunken mischief has deprived someone of an animal he wants returned.

Gleda also makes the scale of the disturbance easier to picture. Rorikstead is a farming settlement, and Ennis's complaint belongs to the daily life of that place. He isn't seeking a Daedric artifact or an explanation of Sam's identity. He needs his goat. The Dragonborn can resolve his grievance through the recovery, or use other available means to obtain information, but Ennis places Whiterun on the trail. The journey continues because the people left behind remember enough to point toward another person who dealt with the Dragonborn.

That person is Ysolda. In Whiterun, she knows about a wedding the Dragonborn was arranging and a ring she supplied on credit. From her perspective, there's an unpaid agreement to settle. From the Dragonborn's perspective, an entire marriage plan has appeared without any remembered courtship. Ysolda connects the ring with Witchmist Grove, where the intended bride can be found. The errand is still presented through an ordinary transaction: someone borrowed something valuable for a wedding, and the person who provided it expects either payment or its return.

If the Dragonborn follows that lead, the prospective bride turns out to be Moira, a hagraven. She recognizes the Dragonborn as the person she expected to marry. Asking for the ring back brings the engagement to a hostile end, and recovering it allows the Dragonborn to return it to Ysolda. The encounter gives a concrete answer to the wedding story without supplying a remembered version of the proposal. Ysolda knows about the arrangement, Moira regards it as real, and the ring has passed between them through the Dragonborn's actions.

Ysolda can also be persuaded or paid, so visiting Moira isn't an unavoidable part of every version of the quest. Whichever route the player takes through that conversation, the search can proceed to Morvunskar, the fort associated with the wedding gathering. By now, the promised drinking prize has required dealings with a priestess, a farmer, and a merchant. Sam hasn't been available to answer any of them. The Dragonborn has had to negotiate with people whose concerns are immediate, even while trying to establish how the evening carried them so far.

Morvunskar adds a different obstacle. Hostile mages occupy the fort, and reaching the destination means passing through danger before finding the portal within. Beyond that portal is Misty Grove, where a feast is underway and Sam can finally be found. The change of setting gives the search a fitting destination. The tavern contest has led through recognizable settlements and personal complaints to a place outside the ordinary route between Skyrim's towns. Here, the host is ready to explain himself, after the Dragonborn has already spent the effort finding him.

Sam reveals that he's Sanguine, the Daedric Prince associated with indulgence and debauchery. He treats the night's excesses as entertainment and gives the Dragonborn the Sanguine Rose. His pleasure in the episode differs sharply from the concerns encountered along the way. Senna had a disturbed temple to attend to, Ennis wanted Gleda back, and Ysolda expected a debt to be settled. Sanguine can enjoy the whole affair from the feast at its end. The Dragonborn has met the people who were left to deal with its separate consequences.

The Sanguine Rose is a staff shaped like a flowering rose, with the power to summon a Dremora to fight for its wielder. Receiving it completes the promise that made the contest attractive in the first place. Sanguine doesn't impose a continuing term of service in this encounter. Instead, the obligations arise during the entertainment itself, through property taken, credit extended, and promises other people understood the Dragonborn to have made. When the visit to Misty Grove ends, the Dragonborn returns to the tavern where the contest began, carrying the staff Sam offered before anyone mentioned a missing goat or a wedding ring.


In Dawnstar, people are afraid to fall asleep. The nightmares have become a shared trouble in the town, and the conversations at the Windpeak Inn bring that trouble into an ordinary public room. Erandur, a priest of Mara, believes he knows where the dreams originate. Above Dawnstar stands Nightcaller Temple, once occupied by worshippers of Vaermina. He asks the Dragonborn to accompany him there and help end the disturbance. His explanation gives the townspeople's suffering a location, though reaching it will also bring him back to a past he hasn't yet disclosed.

Vaermina's sphere includes dreams and nightmares, and her influence in Dawnstar reaches people who haven't sought her out. They don't need to visit a shrine or ask for a gift. Inside Nightcaller Temple is the Skull of Corruption, an artifact Erandur identifies as the source of their distress. He explains that it draws upon the memories of sleeping people. For Dawnstar's residents, the immediate problem is exhaustion and fear. Whatever purpose the Skull has within Vaermina's worship, its presence above the town has made sleep something the people below can no longer rely on.

Erandur opens a concealed entrance inside the temple, and the journey continues into rooms that have been cut off for years. Orc invaders once attacked the sanctuary. To stop them, the priests released a substance called the Miasma, which placed both attackers and defenders into a prolonged sleep. As Erandur and the Dragonborn pass through, sleepers begin to wake, and the old conflict becomes dangerous again. The temple contains people whose last waking concerns belong to that attack. Dawnstar's present suffering has its source in a sanctuary where an earlier struggle never reached an ordinary end.

Erandur's familiarity with the place soon requires an explanation. He admits that he was once a priest of Vaermina, known as Brother Casimir. When the Orcs attacked, he fled and left the other priests behind. His service to Mara followed that abandonment, and returning now means facing the people he escaped. This admission changes the journey for the Dragonborn. Their guide knows the temple because he belonged to it, and his wish to destroy the Skull is bound up with something he did there. He offers that history himself, even though it gives his companion a reason to doubt him.

A magical barrier prevents them from reaching the Skull directly. Erandur proposes using Vaermina's Torpor, a potion that allows the drinker to enter a state called the Dreamstride. The temple's library provides information about it, and the potion can be found within the sanctuary. This gives the search a particular rhythm: Erandur supplies knowledge from his former religious life, while the Dragonborn must take the risk of putting that knowledge into practice. His experience is useful throughout the temple. It's also a continuing reminder that the priest of Mara once helped sustain the worship he's returned to undo.

Through the Dreamstride, the Dragonborn experiences the attack as Brother Casimir, Erandur's former identity. The other priests address Casimir and send him to release the Miasma. The Dragonborn follows that remembered course through the temple and carries out the action that put its occupants to sleep. Erandur's confession now has a setting and a sequence of events around it. The experience places the traveler inside his earlier role, close enough to participate in the sanctuary's desperate response to the invaders. When the Dreamstride ends, the Dragonborn has reached the other side of the barrier and can remove the obstruction.

Near the Skull, Erandur encounters Veren Duleri and Thorek, former fellow priests who survived in the temple's unnatural sleep. They recognize him as Casimir and accuse him of leaving them. Erandur acknowledges what he did, but the meeting doesn't produce forgiveness or a peaceful departure. The confrontation ends in fighting, and only afterward can he proceed with the ritual to destroy the artifact. His return has brought him face to face with the people he abandoned. Their anger concerns an actual shared past, one that his later devotion to Mara hasn't erased.

As Erandur begins the ritual, Vaermina addresses the Dragonborn. She claims that he intends to betray his companion and take the Skull for himself, and she urges the Dragonborn to kill him. Her warning arrives while he's occupied with the work he promised to perform. It's an accusation from the Prince whose artifact he is trying to destroy, and his supposed present intention isn't independently confirmed. The player must decide whether to act on it. Erandur's admitted flight gives the accusation something painful to attach itself to, even though that past failure doesn't establish a new betrayal.

If the Dragonborn lets Erandur finish, he destroys the Skull, and Dawnstar's nightmare affliction ends. He survives and can become a follower. If the Dragonborn instead kills him during the ritual, the Skull can be taken as Vaermina's reward. As a usable staff, it can gather dreams from sleeping people to strengthen its destructive effect. That property carries the temple's intrusion into sleep beyond Dawnstar. Keeping the artifact offers a practical benefit to its bearer, while destroying it completes the task Erandur brought the Dragonborn there to accomplish.

In Oblivion, Vaermina's shrine presents a different encounter with troubled sleep. Her worshippers require a black soul gem before the visitor can approach her, and Vaermina then sends the traveler to recover an orb stolen by the wizard Arkved. Inside Arkved's Tower, the search passes through spaces shaped by nightmare, with unsettling changes of scale and rooms far removed from an ordinary wizard's residence. Arkved himself lies asleep, and the orb can be recovered nearby. Returning it earns the Skull of Corruption. In that encounter, the visitor works on Vaermina's behalf and leaves with the artifact she provides.

The two quests place a mortal on different sides of Vaermina's business. Arkved's tower contains something she wants returned; Nightcaller Temple contains something Erandur wants removed from the world. In Dawnstar, the decision eventually rests beside a priest performing a ritual, with the Prince speaking against him. Allowing him to continue leaves the Dragonborn without the staff, but it also leaves Erandur alive to walk out of the sanctuary. Below the temple, the townspeople can sleep again without the Skull reaching into their dreams.


Brother Verulus won't let visitors into Markarth's Hall of the Dead while the remains entrusted to him are being disturbed. The trouble comes to the Dragonborn through a priest trying to protect a burial place. Before Namira's name enters the conversation, there's a problem within the city's ordinary religious life. Families have brought their dead to the hall, and Verulus can no longer assure them that the bodies are safe. He needs someone to investigate what has happened beyond the entrance.

If the Dragonborn gains access, Eola approaches inside the hall. She speaks as though she already knows something shameful about the visitor, describing an appetite that the Dragonborn has supposedly concealed. Her confidence is part of the encounter. The player hasn't supplied the past she describes, and her account shouldn't be mistaken for an established history of the character. Eola offers recognition on her own terms, inviting the Dragonborn to accept both her description and the companionship that comes with it.

That approach makes Namira's worship unusually personal. Eola doesn't begin by asking the visitor to study a doctrine or admire a distant realm. She suggests that something others would condemn can be understood and welcomed among her people. The invitation may sound accepting, but it carries a demand involving another person's life. Following her means moving beyond the disturbance in the burial hall and helping prepare a gathering where the cult's appetite will be given a victim.

Namira's shrine quest in Oblivion gives a different setting for this association with things people recoil from. Her followers live apart from the more comfortable society of Cyrodiil. A visitor whose Personality is too high is rejected at the shrine. The condition turns a familiar advantage in conversation into an obstacle: someone who usually makes a favorable impression doesn't belong there yet. Approaching Namira requires entering a community that regards that social attractiveness with contempt, rather than treating it as a reason to listen.

Namira then sends the visitor to Anga, an Ayleid ruin occupied by her Forgotten Ones. Priests of Arkay have entered the ruin with light, intending to reach the people living there. From the priests' perspective, their presence is a religious mission. Namira describes the intrusion as a threat to her followers and wants the priests removed. The dispute takes place in rooms where darkness is already part of the inhabitants' lives. Bringing illumination into Anga changes the conditions under which those inhabitants can remain secluded.

The spell Namira provides, Namira's Shroud, extinguishes the priests' light. Once a priest is left in darkness, the Forgotten Ones attack him. The visitor's assigned role is to make that possible, allowing the inhabitants to carry out the violence themselves. Completing the task earns Namira's ring. There's a harshness to the arrangement that remains even if the priests seem intrusive. Protecting the Forgotten Ones' chosen way of living, as Namira demands it here, means helping them kill the people who have come among them.

Arkay's priests connect Anga with Markarth, though their situations differ. In Cyrodiil, priests venture into a place inhabited by Namira's followers. In Skyrim, Brother Verulus is already responsible for a hall that Eola has entered and violated. He has duties to the dead and to the living families who leave them in his care. Eola treats his religious responsibility as an inconvenience to be exploited. The two quests bring Namira's followers into conflict with Arkay's servants through specific places, each with an existing purpose.

If the Dragonborn accepts Eola's invitation, she directs them to Reachcliff Cave. Draugr have made the site unsafe for the feast she wants to hold, and she asks for help clearing them out. This gives the invitation a practical stage before its full cost arrives. Eola can accompany the Dragonborn through the cave, becoming an ally during the danger there. That cooperation can make her seem familiar by the time she explains the next task. The intended gathering still depends on bringing someone else into a situation he doesn't understand.

Eola wants Brother Verulus brought to Reachcliff Cave under the pretense that the Dragonborn needs his help. The priest who first allowed the investigation can become its intended victim. His willingness to travel rests on the request presented to him, while Eola and the Dragonborn know what awaits. Once he arrives, Eola takes charge of him and directs him toward the place prepared for the meal. Keeping that deception in view matters, because the cult presents the gathering as a welcome for the Dragonborn.

The guests give the feast a connection to daily life in Markarth. Banning, who works at the stables, is there. So are Lisbet of Arnleif and Sons Trading Company and Hogni Red-Arm, the city's meat merchant. These are people the Dragonborn can encounter through work and trade, outside any shrine. Their presence makes Reachcliff Cave feel like a concealed part of the same local community. Eola's circle includes residents who can conduct ordinary business in the city and then gather elsewhere for practices their neighbors haven't been invited to see.

The Dragonborn can decline Eola's invitation when they first meet, rather than help arrange the feast. If the Dragonborn instead follows the invitation as far as Reachcliff Cave, it's also possible to turn against the assembled cult and save Verulus. Those branches keep the priest's fate from being inevitable. Accepting Eola's earlier help doesn't require completing her demand. If the Dragonborn does carry out the final act and take part in the meal, Namira addresses them and grants her ring, whose benefits include a reward for feeding on the dead.

Across these encounters, Namira receives worship among people who embrace what others find repellent, but their circumstances can't all be treated as the same. The Forgotten Ones inhabit a dark ruin apart from society. Eola's guests include people with established places in Markarth. The invitation at Reachcliff Cave therefore asks something quite specific of the Dragonborn: participation in a community whose welcome depends on harming the priest brought there. If Verulus is rescued, he can leave the cave alive, and Namira's promised ring remains unearned.


At Peryite's shrine in the Reach, Kesh the Clean asks for ingredients before the Dragonborn can speak with his Prince. A silver ingot, a flawless ruby, deathbell, and vampire dust must be brought together for an incense that makes the audience possible. The shrine is an outdoor place, with a dragon-shaped image of Peryite and a worshipper who knows how to approach him. There's no royal court to pass through. Yet the task that follows will take the Dragonborn into a Dwemer ruin occupied by people whose disease is part of their devotion.

Peryite is associated with pestilence and with natural order, an unusual combination if order brings to mind clean streets and healthy households. His concerns include processes that mortals would rather interrupt or escape. Disease belongs within that description, though the suffering it causes remains quite immediate for the people who encounter it. Kesh's title, the Clean, sits beside his service to a Prince whose followers can bear visible illness. At this shrine, cleanliness doesn't amount to a rejection of Peryite or his gifts.

Once Kesh prepares the incense, inhaling its fumes allows the Dragonborn to hear Peryite. The Prince describes a congregation of afflicted Bretons and the overseer who has failed him. Orchendor was meant to guide these followers, but Peryite says that he has turned away from his patron and must be killed. The request gives the audience a practical purpose. Kesh has arranged contact, and Peryite now has someone who can enter the refuge and reach the servant he wants removed. The promised reward depends on carrying out that assignment.

The congregation's destination is Bthardamz, a Dwemer ruin in the Reach. Peryite explains that he spared these followers from a plague and sent them there under Orchendor's care. That account makes their illness part of an obligation to him. They have survived, gathered, and settled where their patron directed them. Orchendor's position comes with responsibility for those people, as well as duties toward the Prince. By the time the Dragonborn arrives, the refuge has become a place Peryite wants an outsider to penetrate.

Bthardamz gives the congregation substantial shelter. Its passages and chambers extend through a large Dwemer complex, where the Afflicted occupy an environment built by people who are long gone. Dwemer machinery remains within it, so reaching Orchendor involves passing through both the inhabitants' defenses and the dangers of the ruin itself. The choice of refuge has a plain consequence for Peryite's errand. His disobedient overseer is deep inside a place that can keep an approaching enemy occupied well before that enemy reaches him.

The Afflicted also carry their patron's association with disease into their encounters with the Dragonborn. They can expel a poisonous substance against an attacker. Illness has therefore become something they can use to defend their community, even as it marks them as members of it. Their condition isn't background decoration around a shrine. It changes what happens when the Dragonborn enters their home. Peryite's commission brings his prospective agent into conflict with the very followers whom, by his own account, he once protected.

Within Bthardamz, the Afflicted have concerns that reach beyond the command given at the shrine. The Dragonborn can overhear a brother and sister discussing whether to remain, with the sister wanting to leave and her brother resisting. Their disagreement gives the refuge a different scale. Peryite has spoken about a congregation and its overseer; these two people have to decide whether they can continue living there. Sharing an illness and a patron hasn't made their wishes identical, or settled what either of them should do about their circumstances.

Orchendor waits farther within the complex. He's a mage who can teleport during the confrontation, and his immunity to magic distinguishes him from the followers encountered on the way. Peryite's complaint concerns his failure as an overseer, but Orchendor is also capable of resisting the person sent to punish him. If the Dragonborn completes the commission, that resistance ends with his death. There's no replacement appointed during the visit, and the quest doesn't provide a scene of the surviving congregation receiving new guidance.

Returning to the shrine brings another audience through Kesh's incense. Peryite accepts the completed service and awards Spellbreaker, a shield whose protection includes a ward against spells when raised. It's a fittingly practical payment after the journey to Orchendor. The Dragonborn has performed a dangerous task and receives an object that can protect its bearer against further danger. Peryite remains at a distance throughout the enforcement. Kesh facilitates the meetings, the Dragonborn travels to Bthardamz, and Orchendor bears the punishment.

An encounter with Peryite in Oblivion places a visitor in a different kind of service. At his shrine in Cyrodiil, his followers stand unresponsive because their souls have become separated from their bodies. Peryite explains that they attempted a summoning ritual that went wrong. Their effort to reach him has left them unable to recover themselves. Here the trouble begins with worshippers trying to cross a boundary they don't know how to manage. The Prince asks the visitor to retrieve them, and opens the way into Oblivion.

The visitor must find the stranded souls while leaving the followers' bodies behind at the shrine. Recovering them allows those worshippers to return, and Peryite gives Spellbreaker as the reward for this service too. The comparison with Bthardamz is useful because the people being recovered are Peryite's own followers. Their mistake receives a rescue. Orchendor's disobedience receives a death sentence. Both errands require a mortal to go where Peryite directs and put a disturbed arrangement back into the condition he wants.

These encounters give Peryite's order a specific character. In Cyrodiil, worshippers regain their bodies after a failed ritual. In Skyrim, a protected congregation loses the overseer accused of abandoning his duties. The dragon image at the shrine may occupy little space, but the obligations attached to it extend into inhabited ruins and across the boundary of Oblivion. For the Dragonborn who accepts the bargain, the matter ends with Spellbreaker in hand. For the Afflicted at Bthardamz, life must continue in the refuge after Orchendor is gone.


A giant is attacking Largashbur when the Dragonborn first encounters the stronghold's crisis. The Orcs living behind its walls have been struggling with these attacks, and their chief, Yamarz, hasn't secured their safety. Atub, the stronghold's wise woman, is willing to ask an outsider for help. That request already tells us something about the situation inside. Largashbur has a chief and warriors, along with a religious tradition meant to guide them, yet Atub has to reach beyond the settlement to seek relief from Malacath's displeasure.

Yamarz objects to the outsider's presence. Atub nevertheless explains that the tribe is cursed and needs to contact Malacath. She asks the Dragonborn to bring troll fat and a Daedra heart for a ritual. These are physical ingredients that someone must obtain before she can proceed. Malacath may be the stronghold's patron, but the Orcs can't resolve their trouble merely by acknowledging him. Atub undertakes the work of approaching him, while Yamarz's position as chief leaves him answerable for what has happened under his leadership.

For these Orcs, Malacath belongs within the daily organization of the stronghold. Skyrim's Orc strongholds recognize chiefs, wise women, and rules governing conduct within their communities. The Code of Malacath includes prohibitions against theft and unjustified violence, alongside obligations to defend oneself and answer injuries. This gives his worship a setting beyond a remote shrine. A dispute between residents, the standing of a chief, and the defense of the walls can all have religious weight. At Largashbur, those concerns have gathered around Yamarz's failure to protect his people.

The book The True Nature of Orcs gives a much older explanation for the bond between Malacath and the Orcs. It recounts the tradition that Trinimac, once a revered Elven deity, opposed the teachings associated with Veloth's movement, and that Boethiah defeated and transformed him. In this account, Trinimac became Malacath, while his followers became the Orcs. The book presents that change through a hostile description of degradation. It's an in-world account of a divine transformation, carrying its author's attitudes toward the people whose origins it describes.

That tradition helps explain why Malacath's worship is so closely associated with Orcish identity and exclusion. Followers who once belonged to an older Elven religious order are described as becoming a separate people alongside their changed god. The story gives the Orcs a patron who shares their separation from those who reject them. At Largashbur, however, that bond doesn't spare the tribe from harsh judgment. When Atub performs her ritual, Malacath answers by condemning Yamarz. The chief's title hasn't persuaded the Prince that he's fulfilled the duties attached to it.

Malacath directs Yamarz to deal with the giants at his shrine and recover the warhammer they have taken. The order gives the chief a specific task through which to answer the accusation against him. He's expected to enter the place the giants occupy and confront the danger himself. Atub has obtained an answer, though it requires the very chief who has failed the tribe to act. The Dragonborn's part also changes here. Having supplied the ritual ingredients, the outsider is now drawn into Yamarz's attempt to carry out his patron's demand.

Yamarz wants the Dragonborn to accompany him to Fallowstone Cave. He offers payment for assistance and insists that he must be the one to face the giant leader. On the journey through the cave, though, he relies on the Dragonborn to deal with the dangers along the route. His behavior puts the stronghold's predicament into a smaller, more immediate setting. At Largashbur, others have been living with the consequences of his leadership. In the cave, another person is again expected to make his passage possible while he retains the position of the warrior responsible for the task.

Beyond the cave lies Giant's Grove, where the giant leader waits near Malacath's shrine. Here Yamarz attempts another arrangement. He asks the Dragonborn to kill the giant for him, with payment in exchange for concealing what happened. The request goes further than accepting help on a dangerous journey. Malacath has demanded that Yamarz answer for his failure, and Yamarz now wants to return with the appearance of having done so. The tribe would hear about a victory achieved by the chief while the person who actually accomplished it remained silent.

The encounter can proceed differently depending on the player's response. If the Dragonborn refuses to do Yamarz's work, he attempts to fight the giant and is killed. If the Dragonborn agrees and defeats the giant, Yamarz turns against the outsider to keep his arrangement secret. That route also ends with his death. These alternatives give his final failure different forms. In one, he can't survive the challenge he was ordered to undertake. In the other, his effort to preserve his reputation leads him to attack the person who made his apparent success possible.

After the giant leader is defeated, the Dragonborn can recover Shagrol's Warhammer. Malacath addresses the survivor and directs them to take it back to Largashbur. His response leaves no need to maintain Yamarz's proposed account of the expedition. The Prince knows the chief has failed, and the return to the stronghold carries that judgment home. Atub, who began by seeking help for her people, receives news of Yamarz's death along with the recovered weapon. The danger at the shrine has been addressed, but the settlement must now continue without its chief.

Malacath names Gularzob as the new chief and instructs the Dragonborn to place the warhammer upon the shrine. There it becomes Volendrung, the Daedric warhammer given as the reward for completing the task. The stronghold's curse is lifted. For Largashbur, the result includes a replacement for Yamarz and the restoration of its relationship with its patron. Atub's appeal has brought a severe answer, followed by a way for the community to continue. Gularzob remains among the Orcs as their chief, while the outsider can leave with Volendrung.


Mercer Frey has taken something that belongs beneath a temple, and the damage reaches the thieves working in Riften. The Skeleton Key is Nocturnal's artifact, an unbreakable lockpick with powers beyond opening an ordinary lock. Its proper place is the Ebonmere, a conduit between her realm of Evergloam and the Twilight Sepulcher. When Mercer removes it, he breaks the arrangement he was sworn to protect. Karliah connects that theft to the Thieves Guild's failing fortunes, giving its troubles an explanation that reaches beyond bad jobs and empty coffers.

Before the Dragonborn learns about the Key, Mercer appears to be the person keeping the Guild together. He leads its members from the Ragged Flagon's hidden headquarters beneath Riften. Karliah, meanwhile, is remembered as the traitor who murdered the former Guild Master, Gallus. The investigation at Snow Veil Sanctum overturns that account. Mercer turns on the Dragonborn, and Karliah saves the person who accompanied him. Her intervention creates an opportunity to examine Gallus's death without relying on the man who benefited from it.

Gallus left a journal written in Falmer, so Karliah needs help making its contents readable. Enthir and Calcelmo's research become part of that work. Once translated, the journal exposes Mercer's theft from the Guild and his violation of the Nightingales' obligations. This matters to Karliah in a personal way. She's been blamed for killing Gallus, who was also her lover. Returning to Riften with his account allows her to challenge Mercer before the people who have accepted his version for years.

The Guild's vault supplies a more immediate confirmation. Its wealth is gone. Brynjolf and the other thieves have to confront the fact that their leader has been stealing from them while they struggled to recover their standing. Mercer has also planned a theft at Irkngthand, where the Eyes of the Falmer remain within a Dwemer ruin. Recovering those treasures and pursuing Mercer gives the Guild a practical objective. Karliah believes the pursuit also requires repairing a religious obligation that most of the Guild has never shared.

She takes Brynjolf and the Dragonborn to Nightingale Hall, where the Nightingales' connection to Nocturnal becomes explicit. They are a small, secretive circle associated with the Guild, pledged to defend her shrine and the Ebonmere. Gallus, Karliah and Mercer had belonged to that circle. Membership in the Thieves Guild alone doesn't carry the same oath. The merchants, burglars and other members working out of Riften haven't all entered the agreement that Karliah now asks her companions to accept.

If the Dragonborn continues through this part of the Guild's story, Karliah leads an induction before Nocturnal. The oath carries service beyond mortal life: a Nightingale is bound to guard the Twilight Sepulcher after death. Brynjolf has to consider that prospect alongside the immediate need to stop Mercer. Nocturnal accepts the new arrangement, though her response gives little comfort to Karliah. The Prince expects the stolen Key returned. Becoming a Nightingale places the Dragonborn inside an existing obligation, with the earlier betrayal still unresolved.

Karliah describes Nocturnal's favor through the luck a thief needs when skill can't settle everything. A successful burglary depends on circumstances outside the burglar's hands, even after careful preparation. In Karliah's account, the closed Ebonmere has deprived the Guild of that advantage. Her explanation fits the Guild's experience of repeated failure, but it remains the religious understanding she brings to those events. What the journey makes tangible is the damaged sanctuary itself, where the consequences of Mercer's theft have fallen on its guardians.

At Irkngthand, Mercer demonstrates why the Key is more dangerous in his possession than an unusually good lockpick would suggest. Karliah explains that it can unlock potential as well as physical barriers, and she connects Mercer's extraordinary abilities to his misuse of it. The pursuit leads to the great statue holding the Eyes of the Falmer. If the Dragonborn completes the confrontation, Mercer dies, the chamber floods, and the survivors escape with the recovered Key. The Guild's betrayer is gone, while Nocturnal's property still needs to reach its destination.

The Key can remain in the Dragonborn's possession if the player delays returning it. During that delay, it remains useful as an unbreakable lockpick, and the quest to restore the Ebonmere remains unfinished. This gives the artifact a particular temptation within ordinary play. The Dragonborn has seen what Mercer did with it and has sworn to repair the breach, yet carrying it offers an immediate convenience. Finishing Karliah's task means giving up an object that makes the everyday work of opening locked containers easier.

Inside the Twilight Sepulcher, Gallus remains as a spectral guardian. His presence gives the promise of service after death a named face. He's still at the place his oath bound him to protect, and he directs the Dragonborn through the Pilgrim's Path toward the Ebonmere. The route includes a chamber where light is dangerous and darkness provides passage. Nocturnal's association with shadow becomes a condition of moving through her sanctuary. Reaching the inner chamber requires the traveler to accommodate the place they have entered.

Returning the Skeleton Key opens the Ebonmere again. Nocturnal addresses the Dragonborn, and Karliah can finally meet Gallus without Mercer's accusation standing between them. The restoration also allows the Dragonborn to receive a Nightingale agent's power. The reward follows the return of the artifact and the fulfillment of the oath's immediate demand. Gallus is finally free to depart for Evergloam, while the Dragonborn's own Nightingale oath remains in force.

In Oblivion, Nocturnal's shrine near Leyawiin presents a simpler recovery. She sends the player after the stolen Eye of Nocturnal. Following the thieves' conversation leads to Tidewater Cave, and returning the Eye earns the Skeleton Key. That encounter doesn't include the Nightingale oath made in Skyrim. The two recoveries therefore leave the mortal in different circumstances. In Cyrodiil, Nocturnal grants the Key as payment for recovering her property. At the Ebonmere, the Dragonborn surrenders it under an agreement whose service continues after death.


Septimus Signus lives beside a sealed Dwemer lockbox in an outpost north of the College of Winterhold. The object occupies his attention so completely that conversation keeps returning to what might be inside it. He believes it contains the Heart of Lorkhan. That belief gives his work an extraordinary purpose, though the contents will turn out to be something else. By the time the Dragonborn finds him, Septimus has already accepted help from Hermaeus Mora, and his search has become part of the Prince's business.

The Dragonborn has a practical reason to consult him. Septimus knows how to reach an Elder Scroll beneath Skyrim, and he supplies a blank lexicon and an attunement sphere for the journey. His directions lead through Dwemer ruins into Blackreach, then to the machinery in the Tower of Mzark. There, the Scroll can be released while the lexicon receives an inscription. For someone following the main quest, the Scroll is the immediate concern. Septimus wants the information brought back in the smaller device.

That arrangement gives the expedition two beneficiaries. The Dragonborn gains access to something needed for a separate undertaking, while Septimus obtains a record he couldn't retrieve himself. He remains in his outpost as someone else crosses the ruins and operates the machinery. Hermaeus Mora's involvement works through this existing need. The Prince has a servant who desperately wants the box opened, and that servant can offer useful directions to a traveler capable of going where he cannot.

When Septimus examines the inscribed lexicon, his problem becomes more specific. The lock requires Dwemer blood, which he cannot obtain. He proposes creating a substitute by combining blood from the surviving elven peoples. If the Dragonborn continues his work, the next collection includes Altmer, Bosmer, Dunmer, Orsimer, and Falmer blood. His scholarship now asks the traveler to bring back material taken from bodies. The quest's change of errand is plain enough: recovering an inscription has led to gathering the ingredients for a mechanism that imitates an absent people.

Hermaeus Mora addresses the Dragonborn through a dark, hovering manifestation at the outpost. He identifies himself as the power behind Septimus's pursuit and presents the scholar as someone whose usefulness is nearly exhausted. Septimus is still speaking about his discovery as the goal of his life's work. His patron is already discussing a successor. The Dragonborn can answer Mora with resistance or receptiveness, but the encounter makes clear that Mora's interest reaches beyond the contents of the lockbox. He's assessing the person who has made the expedition possible.

Once the blood mixture opens the lock, Septimus enters and finds the Oghma Infinium. His expected discovery has vanished in the presence of an actual one, and he has only a brief opportunity to recognize the discrepancy before he's reduced to ash. Mora then offers the book to the Dragonborn. The scholar who spent himself pursuing the sealed chamber doesn't leave with its treasure. The traveler who completed his work receives an artifact that can increase their abilities through reading, with its benefits directed toward different kinds of skill.

The Oghma Infinium makes Mora's payment attractive in a way that's easy to understand. A person who survives by fighting, spellcasting, or stealth can gain useful knowledge from it. Septimus's fate doesn't make the reward imaginary. It makes accepting the reward uncomfortable, because the Dragonborn has just seen how the previous servant's usefulness ended. Mora can deliver something valuable while treating the mortal who sought it as replaceable. Curiosity brings Septimus into the arrangement, and the Prince uses that curiosity to keep a difficult project moving.

On Solstheim, the Black Books give the same patron a wider reach. These volumes can carry a reader into Apocrypha, Mora's realm, where books fill immense structures and Seekers and Lurkers threaten visitors. Reading becomes a physical crossing into a place with passages to navigate and dangers to survive. A volume found in a ruin can therefore alter what happens far outside its resting place. Someone opens a book on Solstheim, and Mora gains an opportunity to meet that reader within his own domain.

Neloth approaches these books with a scholar's appetite and considerable confidence. In Nchardak, he accompanies the Dragonborn through a Dwemer complex to reach a Black Book protected by its machinery. Their work requires restoring access through the ruin before the volume can be read. Neloth can help investigate the object and explain what he knows about it, but the Dragonborn makes the passage into Apocrypha. His involvement gives another view of mortal curiosity: research can be organized and technically capable while still bringing someone within reach of a Daedric patron.

Apocrypha also contains rewards that encourage further visits. Some Black Books allow the Dragonborn to select abilities after completing their passages. The Black Book called Untold Legends can grant access to a Dremora merchant or a Dremora who carries belongings, depending on the selected reward. Those are practical conveniences for a traveler, alongside more directly martial benefits available elsewhere. Mora's library can supply services as well as instruction. Its appeal extends to the ordinary difficulties of carrying possessions, finding a buyer, and surviving another journey.

Mora's demand for the Skaal's secrets shows what he still wants from outside his library. He offers the knowledge the Dragonborn needs in exchange for traditions kept by Storn Crag-Strider's people. Storn agrees because of the danger facing Solstheim, despite his distrust of the Prince. When he reads the Black Book, Mora takes the knowledge from him, and Storn dies. Frea witnesses the loss of her father. The bargain reaches a village whose guarded teachings matter enough to Mora that he makes their surrender a condition of help.

Mora's collection therefore includes knowledge maintained through living people, with consequences for those left behind. Septimus supplies labor toward opening a box. Storn yields teachings preserved among the Skaal. The Dragonborn receives usable rewards and further demands. These encounters suggest a reason for Mora's continued bargaining: even a realm crowded with books has things its ruler wants mortals to bring him. At Skaal Village, that acquisition leaves Frea without her father, while the knowledge he protected passes into Apocrypha.


Miraak sends the Dragonborn out of Apocrypha the first time they meet. The visitor has reached him through a Black Book beneath his temple on Solstheim, yet arrival doesn't make them his equal. Miraak recognizes another bearer of dragon blood, measures their strength, and has his servants dismiss them. He remains in Hermaeus Mora's realm, with dragons at his command and plans already affecting people across the island. His confinement allows considerable reach. What he lacks is a way to leave on his own terms.

On Solstheim, that reach enters people's working lives. Under Miraak's influence, residents abandon their ordinary activities to labor at the standing stones. Frea finds members of her own Skaal community caught in this work. At his temple, she searches for a way to release them and accompanies the Dragonborn into the ruins. Miraak's return is already costing people hours they can't account for and effort they never agreed to give. The temple connects an absent master in Apocrypha to exhausted workers in settlements far beyond its walls.

Miraak's history helps explain why he expects obedience. He was a dragon priest and a Dragonborn during the age when dragons ruled. The book The Guardian and the Traitor recounts his rebellion against the dragons, his defeat by Vahlok, and his escape through a Daedric intervention. Miraak's survival in Apocrypha places his later ambition within a long dependence on Hermaeus Mora.  By the time the player encounters him, Miraak has spent a long age in a realm whose master preserved him and now stands between him and freedom.

His temple contains a more physical reminder of that earlier life. Dragon remains lie there, connecting the worship surrounding his name with the creatures he once opposed. His status as Dragonborn matters because he can absorb dragon souls, just as the player character can. He understands the power the newcomer is gathering. After their first meeting, he can appear when the Dragonborn kills a dragon and take its soul. A victory in Skyrim or on Solstheim can therefore supply Miraak while leaving the person who fought without the soul they expected to receive.

These appearances make his situation unusually clear. Miraak can interrupt another Dragonborn's progress outside Apocrypha, but he still needs additional strength to escape. He intends to use the rival's soul to complete that effort. The player's arrival gives him an opportunity he couldn't obtain through his followers alone. His ambition also explains why releasing the standing stones matters beyond helping individual workers. As the Dragonborn breaks his influence over them, the arrangement supporting his return begins to come apart.

The means of doing that comes through Bend Will. At Saering's Watch, the Dragonborn learns the first word of the shout and uses it to cleanse the Wind Stone. The Skaal working there are freed, although a Lurker emerges and must be defeated before the danger passes. For Frea and Storn, this is an answer to an immediate community crisis. People can return to the village. For the Dragonborn, it demonstrates a power connected to Miraak that can undo part of his work and eventually make reaching him possible.

Learning the rest brings the Dragonborn into direct dealings with Hermaeus Mora. Through the Black Book Epistolary Acumen, reached during the expedition with Neloth into Nchardak, the Prince supplies another word. He withholds the final one until the Skaal surrender knowledge they've kept from him. Mora presents this knowledge as the price of defeating Miraak. The demand reaches into a small community whose shaman has maintained its traditions, and makes that community's guarded learning part of a dispute between the Prince and his rebellious servant.

Storn understands the danger when the Dragonborn returns. He has resisted Mora's interest in Skaal secrets, and he doesn't treat the offered exchange as harmless. Even so, Miraak's threat leaves his people exposed, and Storn agrees to read the book. Frea objects, fearing what will happen to her father. Storn proceeds, and Mora takes the knowledge through an encounter that kills him. The Dragonborn receives the final word of Bend Will beside the loss that purchased it. Frea is left without her father, and the Skaal have lost their shaman.

There is a painful comparison here with Miraak's own dependence. Storn accepts a known danger because his village needs relief from Miraak. Miraak has used other people's labor and dragon souls to prepare his own departure. Both dealings pass through Mora, though the people paying are in very different positions. By requiring the Skaal's knowledge before granting the last word, the Prince gains something he wanted while equipping someone capable of removing his troublesome servant. The Dragonborn's progress serves his interests before the final confrontation even begins.

With Bend Will complete, the Dragonborn returns through Waking Dreams and travels deeper into Apocrypha. The shout allows them to bend the dragon Sahrotaar and ride him to Miraak's summit. Miraak has been waiting for this encounter as a means of escape. During the fight, he restores himself by killing dragons and absorbing their souls, consuming the allies around him to continue. The same Dragonborn gift that made his rebellion possible has become the means by which he prolongs his last attempt to get away.

When Miraak is finally overcome, Hermaeus Mora intervenes and kills him. The Prince treats his servant's rebellion as a failure and addresses the victorious Dragonborn as a replacement. Mora's claim doesn't settle the player's entire future. The Dragonborn can return to Solstheim, where Frea and the Skaal remain, carrying the consequences of what happened. Miraak never makes that journey home. After surviving his ancient defeat and extending his influence across the island, his attempt to leave Apocrypha ends within the realm where Mora kept him.


Dervenin waits in Solitude with a problem that sounds, at first, like a servant's complaint. His master has gone away, and he wants someone to bring him back. Following that request takes the Dragonborn toward the Blue Palace, where the abandoned Pelagius Wing sits within a working royal household. Elisif's court carries on elsewhere in the building. The disused rooms belong to another history, associated with Emperor Pelagius Septim III. Sheogorath enters this ordinary city through a servant, a locked part of a palace, and an errand whose meaning changes once someone agrees to it.

Getting inside involves the palace's mortal staff. Una or Erdi can provide access if persuaded, while Falk Firebeard can also become a route to the key under the appropriate circumstances. That small administrative obstacle matters to the setting. The Dragonborn doesn't begin by marching through an obvious gate into Oblivion. The approach passes through people who work in the palace and know which doors are usually kept shut. Dervenin also gives the traveler Pelagius's hip bone, an unsettling token connected to the person whose abandoned rooms they're entering.

Inside the wing, the journey breaks away from the building. The Dragonborn arrives at a strange outdoor table where Sheogorath is speaking with Pelagius. The quest places this encounter within Pelagius's mind. Ordinary equipment is unavailable, and Sheogorath supplies the Wabbajack for the work he demands. Asking him to return with Dervenin becomes a negotiation over his holiday. He'll leave after the Dragonborn deals with the difficulties he presents. The visitor who came to retrieve someone now has to take part in the Prince's arrangement.

Pelagius's troubles appear as separate situations that can be approached and changed. In the arena associated with his suspicions, the obvious contest draws attention toward fighting creatures. The answer instead concerns the figures watching the contest. Sheogorath has built a problem in which following the most conspicuous action doesn't settle anything. The Dragonborn must recognize that the surrounding audience belongs to the situation too. It's an encounter designed around redirected attention, with the Wabbajack giving the visitor a way to alter what initially looks fixed.

Another part of the mind gives Pelagius's confidence and anger visible bodies. His confidence is small, while the anger facing it is much larger. The Wabbajack changes their proportions, allowing the Dragonborn to strengthen one and diminish the other. This is the quest's own fantasy language for Pelagius's distress.  Sheogorath has turned an internal struggle into a confrontation whose participants can be resized by a Daedric staff.

The sleeping Pelagius presents a different task. Disturbing figures arrive around him, and the staff can transform them into harmless replacements. Each change allows the sequence to continue until his sleep is no longer surrounded by those threats. The Dragonborn's actions bring the local task to an end, and Sheogorath agrees to depart. Dervenin gets the return he wanted. The traveler returns to the palace with the Wabbajack, having passed through an experience that the ordinary rooms of the Pelagius Wing couldn't have suggested on their own.

In Oblivion, Sheogorath's request begins at a shrine and moves toward an inhabited settlement. Border Watch is a Khajiit village whose residents have a prophecy about its destruction. Ri'Bassa explains the signs associated with it. Sheogorath wants the visitor to make the first signs happen, after which he'll provide the last. Here the Prince doesn't supply a troubled mind as a separate place to enter. He uses a community's existing beliefs, its food, and its animals to produce an event the residents will interpret through a story they already know.

The local inn holds an unusual cheese collection. One particularly pungent cheese can be taken and placed in the village's outdoor cooking pot. Rats gather in response to the smell, supplying the apparent vermin outbreak that the prophecy describes. Ri'Bassa acts on the danger by putting out rat poison. His response gives the visitor the means to arrange the next sign. The village's attempt to protect itself therefore becomes part of Sheogorath's task, even though Ri'Bassa is trying to prevent the disaster he believes is approaching.

Putting that poison into the sheep's feeding trough kills the flock and produces the next expected calamity. These are material actions with consequences for the village. The cheese belongs to someone, the rats have to be dealt with, and the sheep are lost. The visitor knows that deliberate interference caused the signs. The villagers experience a sequence that matches their prophecy. That difference lets Sheogorath turn familiar routines into a frightening confirmation without needing to announce himself in the village at the beginning.

For the final sign, Sheogorath takes over. Burning dogs fall onto Border Watch, supplying the extraordinary conclusion that ordinary interference couldn't produce. He then rewards the traveler with the Wabbajack. The request has used a mixture of deception and direct Daedric intervention. The first stages exploit what the residents already expect, and the last brings something unmistakably beyond their daily experience. Border Watch's people have been drawn into a performance arranged for Sheogorath, with no comparable opportunity to negotiate the terms themselves.

The two quests leave very different kinds of change behind. Pelagius's difficulties become situations the Dragonborn can resolve before leaving his mind. Border Watch loses animals and endures a manufactured fulfillment of its fears. Sheogorath directs both encounters, but the people caught inside them occupy different positions. Dervenin petitions for his master's return, Pelagius receives an unexpected visitor, and Ri'Bassa tries to respond to events whose cause has been concealed from him. The Prince's involvement can be personal, theatrical, helpful in a limited task, or cruel toward an unsuspecting settlement.

Neither city needs to become a permanent Daedric province for Sheogorath to leave a mark. In Solitude, a palace door leads into his diversion. At Border Watch, a cooking pot and a feeding trough become the means of fulfilling his request. His influence reaches people through places they already use, and the Wabbajack remains with the traveler afterward. Beyond those temporary intrusions, he also has a realm where servants, guards, rulers, and residents have to live with his presence as part of their ordinary government.


Haskill receives the traveler in a small room beyond the strange door in Niben Bay. He has a desk, a manner suited to official business, and questions about whether the visitor intends to proceed. The entrance to the Shivering Isles therefore includes something like an interview. When the room gives way around the traveler, the wider realm appears beyond it. That transformation is extraordinary, but Haskill's role remains recognizable. Someone has to receive arrivals, explain the invitation, and direct them toward the work their host expects them to perform.

The first inhabited stopping place is Passwall, a settlement within the Fringe. Arrivals haven't yet reached unrestricted passage through the realm. The Gatekeeper blocks the Gates of Madness, and the people nearby have their own responses to its presence. Some travelers attempt to fight it and fail. Jayred Ice-Veins studies a way to overcome it, while Relmyna Verenim has a much more personal connection to the creature she made. Before the visitor reaches Sheogorath's court, passage already depends on local knowledge and on a being assigned to guard a boundary.

The Gatekeeper's defeat opens access to the routes into Mania and Dementia. These names describe the major divisions of the Isles, with different appearances and different political households. Mania has brightly colored growth and extravagant vegetation. Dementia has darker wetlands and a more enclosed atmosphere. People live, travel, trade, and serve within both regions. The distinction affects the surroundings a traveler crosses, but it also reaches into government. Sheogorath's realm has offices attached to these divisions, and those offices come with rivals, servants, and expectations about succession.

New Sheoth gathers that government into a city. Bliss is associated with Mania, while Crucible belongs to Dementia. Their streets contain homes and businesses, so reaching the capital also means entering places where residents have practical concerns. Cutter works as a smith in Crucible, and the Missing Pauldron supplies armor in Bliss. Merchants need materials and customers. Residents have complaints about their neighbors. The city gives the visitor a setting in which Daedric strangeness continues alongside employment, property, and the need to obtain something useful from another person.

Above these districts, the palace contains Sheogorath's court and the households of the Duke of Mania and Duchess of Dementia. Thadon holds the first office, and Syl holds the second. They serve beneath Sheogorath, but each maintains a separate position within the realm. Meeting them introduces obligations that can't be reduced to the Prince's passing remarks. Their servants know their habits, their households have internal dangers, and their titles can be transferred through recognized rituals. A newcomer is stepping into an arrangement that existed before the invitation arrived.

Haskill helps make this arrangement workable. He explains duties, conveys information, and continues serving even when Sheogorath's instructions seem difficult to interpret. The player can eventually summon him for advice, extending his administrative role beyond the throne room. His restrained responses give the court a continuing point of reference. Sheogorath can change the immediate task, yet Haskill still knows where the visitor is expected to go and which part of the realm's business needs attention. That makes him essential to navigating the Isles as an inhabited place.

Sheogorath's early request involving Xedilian shows the realm maintaining one of its own installations. The site once drew adventurers into a sequence of tests and needs to be restored. The traveler repairs its machinery and then helps decide how the people drawn inside will be dealt with. Kiliban Nyrandil explains the facility and supervises its operation. Xedilian has a purpose within Sheogorath's domain, someone assigned to oversee it, and equipment that can fall out of working order. Restoring it is court service with consequences for the outsiders who enter afterward.

The two great households introduce different experiences of service. Thadon sends the visitor to recover the Chalice of Reversal from Dunroot Burrow, where felldew is needed to gain entry. That journey makes the environment itself part of the difficulty, as dependence on the substance develops within the quest. Syl's concerns lead into an investigation inside her own household, where she suspects a conspiracy. Her torturer, Herdir, accompanies the inquiry. Both rulers can send someone into danger, but the demands arise through their particular households and concerns. Their offices have recognizable reach even when their methods are disturbing.

Religious ceremony also has a public place in New Sheoth. The Great Torch requires the Flame of Agnon, and the struggle at Cylarne involves the Golden Saints and Dark Seducers. These Daedric groups have duties and commanders rather than appearing only as wandering opponents. Once the flame is obtained, the choice of which temple to favor in New Sheoth gives the act a local affiliation. A task performed outside the city returns as a ceremony within it. Worship, armed service, and court favor meet in the same assignment.

The offices of Mania and Dementia become especially concrete when Sheogorath allows the traveler to replace one of their rulers. The routes differ. Taking Thadon's place involves his ritual and the lethal use of greenmote. Taking Syl's place involves pursuing her and obtaining her heart. These are conditional alternatives, each with its own participants and consequences. Whichever route is taken, the title comes through a process the court recognizes. Sheogorath's approval matters, and so does the prescribed act that makes the succession acceptable within his realm.

The surviving ruler doesn't necessarily accept the result peacefully. The political cost follows the visitor into the court's wider troubles. Holding an office creates an allegiance, while removing its previous holder leaves other people with reasons to react. This gives the title weight beyond a ceremonial reward. The traveler has entered the same system that previously sent them on errands. They now possess a place within it, along with a household affiliation and a more direct involvement in the realm's survival.

By then, the Isles have supplied a capital, divided territories, guarded entrances, religious ceremonies, and procedures for replacing rulers. Their inhabitants can be endangered by Sheogorath's decisions, but they also depend on arrangements that continue between those decisions. Haskill remains at his work because invitations and decrees still need to be carried out. The traveler who first sat before his desk has gradually become responsible for part of that world. When the realm faces invasion, the threatened places are already familiar as streets, settlements, courts, and homes.


Order begins appearing in the Shivering Isles as something its residents fear. Crystals emerge, Knights of Order advance, and familiar places become sites of attack. At Passwall, the settlement where the traveler first waited beyond the strange door, the danger reaches people who had seemed part of the realm's entrance. Sheogorath calls this recurring destruction the Greymarch. He knows its approach and has sought a champion to interfere with it. The preparations that brought the visitor into his service belong to an attempt to prevent a catastrophe he has experienced before.

The attackers have a recognizable organization of their own. Knights of Order come through active obelisks, and priests help maintain those incursions. The objects spreading through the Isles are therefore connected to a functioning invasion. Defending a settlement involves interrupting the means by which attackers continue arriving. For the people living nearby, the Greymarch changes which roads and buildings can be used safely. The realm's unusual customs don't protect its residents from having to abandon a position or depend on armed defenders when an enemy reaches them.

Sheogorath directs the player toward measures that might preserve the Isles through this return. The ruined defense at the entrance needs attention, including the creation of a replacement Gatekeeper with Relmyna Verenim's help. The great Daedric servants of the realm also come under attack. Depending on the player's position in the court, the struggle involves restoring the Golden Saints at Brellach or the Dark Seducers at Pinnacle Rock. These tasks concern the realm's capacity to keep functioning. Its ruler needs defenders who can return to service and a boundary that can be guarded again.

Yet Sheogorath's own presence becomes increasingly uncertain as the crisis develops. His explanation places him inside the recurring event. When the decisive change comes, he disappears as Sheogorath and becomes Jyggalag, the Prince of Order. The threat isn't merely another ruler arriving at his border. The identity that has governed the Isles gives way to the identity leading their destruction. The player is left with Haskill and an unfinished effort to preserve a realm whose sovereign can no longer perform the role that the crisis requires.

Jyggalag later explains the history behind that transformation. According to his account, his growing power once frightened the other Princes, who cursed him to become Sheogorath. At the end of each era, he could return as Jyggalag and destroy the realm he had inhabited, only to become Sheogorath again afterward. That is the explanation he gives within the story. The events of the Greymarch make the relationship between the two identities immediate, while the ancient gathering and motives of the other Princes reach the player through Jyggalag's testimony.

Haskill's response to Sheogorath's disappearance is practical. The office remains, and the visitor must acquire the means to occupy it. The Staff of Sheogorath needs to be remade, so the search leads to Dyus in Knifepoint Hollow. Dyus served Jyggalag as keeper of his great library and retains knowledge associated with the earlier order. His survival provides a connection to a history that the current court can't supply by itself. Haskill can direct the player toward him, but Dyus must explain what materials the new staff requires.

Those materials draw the traveler into places tied to perception and succession. The branch comes from the Tree of Shades in the Grove of Reflection, where obtaining it includes confronting a reflection of the player. The other requirement is the eye of Ciirta, who lives among the Apostles at the Howling Halls. These journeys aren't an ordinary commission for a replacement weapon. The staff is bound to the position the player is being asked to assume, and its creation depends on objects obtained through particular encounters within the threatened realm.

Dyus makes the staff once the materials are brought to him, but it still needs the power associated with Sheogorath's office. The Font of Madness in the palace should provide that. Order has reached the source, however, and the roots beneath the palace must be cleared before the process can be completed. The court's conflict also returns here through the ruler who wasn't replaced by the player. That ruler's allegiance to Order carries the earlier succession dispute into the attempt to preserve the Isles. Local politics and the Greymarch now obstruct the same work.

Restoring the font allows the staff to be completed. Its role is connected to governing and defending the realm, and the final attack reaches the palace itself. The player stands where Sheogorath should have stood, carrying the instrument prepared for that responsibility. The confrontation with Jyggalag brings the effort to a direct conclusion. He can be defeated, and this time the familiar cycle doesn't simply resume with the destruction and rebuilding of his realm. Someone else has taken the place that previously returned to Sheogorath alone.

Afterward, Jyggalag acknowledges the break. He departs as himself, while the player remains to hold the position of Sheogorath. His account allows him a future beyond the repeated return to the same curse. For the inhabitants of the Isles, the immediate consequence is that their realm survives under a new holder of its ruling role. Haskill continues to serve. The duties attached to the court continue too. Becoming the ruler doesn't empty the streets of residents or remove the practical needs that made the threatened domain worth defending.

This outcome gives a particular meaning to taking on a Daedric role. The player has performed the realm's work, occupied one of its offices, reconstructed its ruler's staff, restored the source that empowers it, and defended the palace. The transformation develops through those connected acts and the unusual division between Jyggalag and Sheogorath. It doesn't supply a rule under which every mortal who serves any Prince will inherit that Prince's place. Here, the vacancy and the recurring transformation make the player's assumption of the role possible within this specific crisis.


Azura's Star can leave a shrine in a traveler's pack and remain useful far from the place where it was received. That portability is part of what makes a Daedric artifact different from a single audience with a Prince. A command may be completed and a voice may fall silent, while the object continues affecting later journeys. Azura's Star, Nocturnal's Skeleton Key, and Meridia's Dawnbreaker do this in different ways. Each gives a mortal a particular capability, and each carries a history that determines what possessing it actually means.

A conventional soul gem is consumed when its stored soul is used for enchanting or recharging. Azura's Star can be used again. That distinction makes it valuable during ordinary travel, when enchanted equipment needs replenishing and suitable gems may be scarce. The Star still requires a soul to be captured and a use to which that soul can be put. Carrying the vessel doesn't supply every other part of the process. Its enduring quality concerns the container, with the rest of the practice continuing to require actions by the person who owns it.

Skyrim places the same named artifact inside a dispute over what it should become. Aranea Ienith directs the Dragonborn toward a vision connected with the Star, and Nelacar supplies information about Malyn Varen's work. Malyn tried to use the artifact in pursuit of continued existence within it. His experiment leaves the Star damaged and creates a problem that can't be settled by picking it up. The Dragonborn must enter the artifact and confront what remains of Malyn before it can return to useful service.

The Dragonborn's choice between Aranea and Nelacar determines how the Star is restored. Returning it through Aranea restores Azura's Star and preserves the association with Azura's service. Working with Nelacar produces the Black Star, altered to hold black souls. These alternatives shouldn't be folded into one completed history. They show how an artifact can become the subject of competing mortal projects. Malyn has already interfered with it, and the Dragonborn's decision directs its restoration toward different ends, with consequences for the people who helped recover it.

Nocturnal's Skeleton Key has an immediately understandable attraction for a thief. In Oblivion, recovering the Eye of Nocturnal from thieves in Leyawiin earns the Key as a reward. It can be used as an unbreakable lockpick. The object therefore enters the repeated small obstacles of travel and theft: a locked door, a closed chest, an entrance someone intended to restrict. Its Daedric origin doesn't require every use to become a fresh conversation with Nocturnal. Once carried away, it continues helping its owner at locks encountered elsewhere.

In Skyrim, Mercer takes the Key from the Twilight Sepulcher and uses it for more than opening ordinary locks. Its usefulness to whoever carries it now conflicts with the Nightingales' duty to return it.

Recovering it from Mercer doesn't automatically complete the obligation. The Dragonborn can carry it for a time, but finishing the return requires taking it through the Pilgrim's Path and replacing it in the Ebonmere. That act restores something the Nightingales were supposed to guard. Possession and rightful custody have separated during the quest. The practical advantage of retaining an unbreakable lockpick sits alongside a named responsibility to Nocturnal. The artifact's story therefore includes the point at which the traveler is expected to stop carrying it.

After Malkoran is defeated, Meridia grants Dawnbreaker and expects it to be carried against the undead. The sword brings fire and a particular power against those enemies into places beyond Kilkreath. Its effects can be striking, especially when the destruction of an undead creature causes nearby undead to be caught in its burst. Yet the weapon still has to be carried into danger and used by someone. Receiving it doesn't remove every threat from a tomb, and Meridia's approval doesn't turn the bearer into an untouchable extension of her will.

These artifacts change different repeated actions. The Star preserves a vessel used in enchantment. The Key assists with opening and, in Skyrim's quest, belongs to a damaged divine connection that requires restoration. Dawnbreaker helps its bearer confront undead after a temple has been reclaimed. Their powers shouldn't be measured as if every description were a promise of unlimited strength. Their stories show particular uses, particular demands, and people who can misuse, lose, recover, or return them. Malyn, Mercer, and Malkoran each become involved in a different kind of interference.


The gate outside Kvatch leads into the Deadlands, while the strange door in Niben Bay leads toward the Shivering Isles. Both allow a mortal to cross into Oblivion, but they lead into very different circumstances. At Kvatch, the opening belongs to an invasion that has devastated a city. In Niben Bay, the entrance carries an invitation and leads to Haskill's reception. The shape of an opening alone can't tell the traveler what waits beyond it, why passage has become possible, or what must happen for the passage to close.

The Deadlands reached during Oblivion's main quest are hostile places of lava, exposed rock, towers, and Daedric defenders. The route beyond the Kvatch gate also contains members of the city's guard who entered before the player. Their presence ties the otherworldly terrain to the struggle outside. Savlian Matius can't recover the city while the gate keeps providing an enemy route. The journey into Mehrunes Dagon's realm therefore has a specific military purpose. Closing that opening gives the defenders a chance to advance on the streets and people they were unable to reach.

Within an invasion gate, a Sigil Stone sustains the connection. Reaching and removing it closes that particular passage and returns the traveler to Tamriel. The action has a local result that can be seen at the place where the gate stood. Other gates can remain open elsewhere, and the wider crisis continues. The stone isn't a master switch for every boundary between Mundus and Oblivion. Its role belongs to the gate being entered, which is why the campaign repeatedly distinguishes defending a town from ending the conditions that have allowed the invasion to spread.

The invasion depends on the failure of the protections associated with the Dragonfires and the Amulet of Kings. Its gates therefore belong to a particular breach, unlike the invitation through Sheogorath's door or the passages opened by Mora's books.

The Shivering Isles use their entrance differently. Haskill receives visitors before the realm opens around them, and the Fringe places another boundary between arrival and wider access. The Gatekeeper stands before the Gates of Madness. Passing through the first door doesn't settle every question of movement inside Sheogorath's domain. The traveler can reach Passwall and still be unable to proceed into Mania or Dementia. That layered entrance gives the realm its own way of receiving, delaying, and testing outsiders before they reach the places governed from New Sheoth.

Once inside, the visitor finds settlements, roads, political households, and inhabitants who have business unrelated to the traveler's arrival. Golden Saints and Dark Seducers serve the realm, while its mortal residents run shops and maintain homes. The Isles contain dangerous creatures and destructive demands, but travel there also means learning a local arrangement of authority. Haskill's directions matter because destinations have assigned purposes. The palace, the temples, and installations such as Xedilian belong to a world that continues working between the visitor's tasks, even when that work is harsh toward those caught in it.

Apocrypha is approached through another kind of object altogether. In Dragonborn, Black Books carry the traveler into Hermaeus Mora's realm. A book can be found within a ruin on Solstheim and become an entrance when read. The route doesn't resemble a road continuing through a standing gate. Its connection depends on the particular book and the conditions of its use. The Dragonborn's body remains associated with the place where the reading began, while the experience carries them into spaces where Mora's servants and accumulated writings surround the path forward.

Inside Apocrypha, passages wind among immense collections of books. Seekers and lurkers inhabit the realm, and the architecture can change as the traveler advances. Books and mechanisms open further sections, so reading becomes part of navigation as well as the reason for entering. Some journeys culminate in another book that grants a choice of powers. The realm's libraries are therefore active environments with obstacles and rewards arranged along a route. Possessing an entrance text doesn't mean the Dragonborn can immediately reach every collection or demand an answer from its ruler.

The Black Book called Waking Dreams has a particular role in reaching Miraak. Other Black Books lead through their own sections of Apocrypha and have different rewards at their ends. They aren't interchangeable tickets to any desired location in Oblivion. The Dragonborn must acquire the relevant means of progress, including the knowledge required to confront Miraak in his refuge. Mora's involvement shapes that progress. The journey through his realm depends on what he permits, what the books provide, and what the traveler has become capable of doing within the story.

The inhabitants also change the experience of crossing. A Dremora defending a tower in the Deadlands participates in Dagon's invasion machinery. A Golden Saint serving in the Isles belongs to Sheogorath's government and can give orders or defend a position. A seeker in Apocrypha occupies a realm organized around Mora's collections and secrets. Calling all of them Daedra identifies a broad connection, but it doesn't tell the visitor how to deal with the being directly ahead. Local allegiance and the purpose of the place remain important after the boundary has been crossed.

For the people living near these entrances, the practical question is always local. Kvatch needs its gate closed before its defenders can reclaim the city. Passwall's arrivals need a way past the Gatekeeper. A reader on Solstheim needs the right Black Book and a route through what it opens. Each crossing connects two places under circumstances that belong to that journey. Beyond the threshold, the traveler still has to learn whose servants are present, what sustains the passage, and which actions will allow them to leave again.


Access to a Prince changes from shrine to shrine. At Azura's, Mels Maryon explains both the glow-dust offering and the dawn-or-dusk approach. Malacath's followers first have to accept the visitor sufficiently to explain the troll fat required there. The intermediary determines whether someone even learns the local procedure.

Other offerings connect with a recognizable interest. Meridia accepts substances associated with the undead. Sanguine's Cyrodilic brandy suits a patron concerned with indulgence. Sheogorath's lettuce, yarn, and lesser soul gem form a much less predictable collection. A visitor cannot turn one successful approach into a list that will open every shrine.

Nocturnal provides a clearer limit to that expectation. Her commission in Oblivion requires no material offering. The visitor still has to approach her particular shrine, but there is no gift to obtain first. The request concerns something she wants recovered, so willingness to undertake the work matters more than bringing another object to the statue.

Hermaeus Mora's invitation depends on an existing history of activity. Casta Flavus brings the summons after the Hero has completed the other Daedric commissions and progressed through the main crisis. The visitor is being noticed for what they have already done, rather than simply arriving with a suitable substance. Those conditions belong to this encounter; another mortal's meeting with Mora need not follow the same route.

The people around the shrines therefore have practical authority. They can explain a time, refuse a conversation, identify an offering, or carry an invitation. Their service gives the divine encounter a local entrance, embedded among Cyrodiil's roads and settlements. Reaching the statue is sometimes only the first part of gaining a hearing.


Refusing a Prince changes different things in the encounters with Erandur, Barbas, and Sinding. Erandur can finish destroying the Skull of Corruption if the Dragonborn leaves him alive. Vaermina loses the intervention she demanded, the staff becomes unavailable, and Dawnstar is freed from its nightmares. The refusal permits another person's intended act to reach completion.

Barbas has leverage within his own relationship with Clavicus Vile. Rejecting the demand to kill him allows their reunion, after which the Dragonborn receives the Masque instead of keeping the Rueful Axe. The Prince still participates in the settlement. Barbas's connection to him makes an alternative bargain possible, so the result differs from destroying the object a Prince wants preserved.

Hircine can recognize Sinding's survival as another successful hunt, with the other hunters becoming the defeated quarry. Helping Sinding can earn the uncursed ring. The Dragonborn has changed sides within a pursuit whose patron accepts the changed result. Sparing the werewolf does not erase his earlier crime in Falkreath, or place the conclusion entirely beyond Hircine's sphere.

These alternatives depend on the people and objects present. Erandur has a rite he can complete; Barbas has a bond he can resume; Sinding has attackers he can survive with help. A mortal who refuses still needs a course of action that changes the encounter. Simply declining to continue an errand can leave it unresolved, without securing any of those outcomes.

The surviving companions make the consequences tangible. Erandur can leave the temple and later travel with the Dragonborn. Barbas returns to his master. Sinding remains alive in the grotto if the traveler helps him withstand the hunt. Those futures are substantial, while their reach remains particular. Choosing one does not automatically cancel the Dragonborn's unrelated oaths or debts elsewhere.


The Elder Scrolls Online gives the Princes an earlier-era setting, long before the journeys through Morrowind, Oblivion, and Skyrim. Its Gold Road story introduces Ithelia, the Forgotten Prince, associated with paths and possibilities. Her return belongs to that separate story, rather than an unseen encounter in the Dragonborn's travels.

Ithelia's absence has a cause within the fiction. Hermaeus Mora helped erase the memory of her existence, fearing the danger of her power. Her return confronts him with a history he tried to keep inaccessible. A missing name in a mortal account can therefore reflect an act of concealment, although Mora's assessment remains the judgment of an interested participant.

The disturbance has a recognizable place in Tamriel. West Weald includes Skingrad, its surrounding countryside, and the sudden appearance of the Dawnwood. People living there face changes in the landscape as well as the emergence of unfamiliar Daedra. The Forgotten Prince's return reaches beyond a revised entry in a scholar's book. It has brought uncertainty into roads and settlements whose inhabitants must now find their way through it.


In Morrowind's religious writing, Boethiah can appear as an ancestral teacher whose guidance helped shape the people who became the Dunmer. The Book of Daedra describes Boethiah through deceit, conspiracy, and the overthrow of authority. Those accounts concern the same Prince, yet they ask the reader to notice different things. A Dunmer religious history is concerned with the origins and obligations of a people. The anonymous catalogue approaches Boethiah through dangerous acts, while Dunmer religious teaching gives the Prince a constructive place in its history.

Azura's part in the end of Morrowind also gives the Nerevarine something that a temple book can't supply on its own. After Dagoth Ur's defeat and the destruction of the Heart's enchantments, she speaks to the person who has carried out the work. Her appearance and reward belong to the protagonist's experience of the event. A temple author can organize inherited teachings, while the Nerevarine has received a direct address. Even then, Azura speaks as a participant with her own long involvement in the Tribunal's history.

The difference between a worshiper's gratitude and a victim's account becomes especially clear in Dawnstar. Erandur understands the suffering there through his former membership in Vaermina's priesthood and his later service to Mara. The people losing sleep experience the result without sharing that history. Their immediate concern is that they can't escape the nightmares. Vaermina's intervention at the end of the temple journey concerns possession of the Skull of Corruption and the killing she wants the Dragonborn to carry out. Each speaker is situated differently around the same artifact.

Vaermina tells the Dragonborn that Erandur will betray them. If he is allowed to finish his rite, he instead destroys the staff as promised. That conditional outcome gives a direct reason to reject her warning as a reliable forecast of his conduct. The disagreement doesn't require a general rule that Princes always lie. Here, the Prince wants an action, describes the man who stands in its way, and can be answered by letting that man complete what he said he would do.

Malacath's request in Oblivion offers a different collision of descriptions. Lord Drad treats the ogres in his mine as labor he is entitled to use. Malacath calls for their freedom, and the visitor can find them confined at the estate. Drad's comfortable position depends on the arrangement continuing. The Prince's interest falls upon those held beneath it. Looking at the mine gives the listener a concrete way to understand why the account offered by its owner would differ from the demand coming from the shrine.

The Shivering Isles introduces another kind of difficulty because Sheogorath's own condition changes during the story. At first, the ruler gives the visitor work within a realm that has a palace, officials, and established divisions. The approaching Greymarch threatens those institutions. Later, Sheogorath's transformation into Jyggalag changes how the visitor understands the ruler who has been issuing instructions. A person describing the court before that transformation would be describing an earlier stage of an event whose full consequences had not yet arrived.

Jyggalag explains that the other Princes cursed him into becoming Sheogorath and that the Greymarch has repeated as part of that condition. The explanation is his account of the distant cause. The visitor also experiences the immediate transformation and can confront Jyggalag during the expansion's conclusion. Those are different kinds of access within one story. The change of ruler is encountered directly, while the ancient judgment of the other Princes reaches the visitor through the being who says he suffered it.

Haskill supplies another perspective from inside the Isles. He advises the new arrival, explains duties, and continues to attend the court through the upheaval. His knowledge is practical and administrative as well as religious. For residents whose lives depend on the realm's institutions, the question of who occupies the throne has consequences beyond how a scholar classifies a Prince. Someone has to carry out the ruler's duties. The expansion lets the visitor acquire responsibilities that make an earlier outsider's description inadequate to their later position.

These disagreements grow out of identifiable lives. A Tribunal writer arranges ancestral religion around living gods. A Raven Rock priest serves the Reclamations. Drad defends his estate, Erandur attempts a repair, and Jyggalag explains the curse from which he has emerged. Their words belong beside what happens in their temples, mines, and realms. Following those circumstances lets an account remain clear without forcing every speaker into agreement. Tamriel preserves conflicting descriptions because its inhabitants meet divine powers in circumstances that can give them very different things to say.


At the Twilight Sepulcher, the Dragonborn can finally complete the journey begun with the recovery of the Skeleton Key. Gallus waits as a Nightingale Sentinel, able to explain the Pilgrim's Path to the living bearer. Beyond him, the sanctuary's trials lead inward, through passages where light itself can be dangerous.

The key remains in the Dragonborn's possession during the approach. Its return is a choice the player can postpone, but the restoration remains unfinished while the traveler carries it elsewhere. In the completed quest, that delay ends at the Ebonmere. The Dragonborn places the stolen artifact where it belongs, restoring the connection with Nocturnal's realm.

Nocturnal appears after the placement. She recognizes the service and grants access to the Nightingale powers, while the oath already undertaken remains. The traveler can choose an ability and later return to change it. The sanctuary now offers a reason to revisit that no longer depends on recovering Mercer's stolen possession.

Karliah and Gallus are reunited at the restored Ebonmere. For Gallus, the work of guarding the damaged sanctuary is finally over; he can depart for Evergloam. Karliah remains with the knowledge of what has been repaired and of the separation that still follows their meeting.

The Thieves Guild has other work waiting outside this chamber. Its standing across Skyrim depends on members, contacts, and jobs beyond the Sepulcher. Here, one particular journey has reached its destination. As the Dragonborn turns toward the way out, the Skeleton Key stays behind. The path back through the sanctuary can be traveled with an empty place among the belongings carried in.
