Beneath Red Mountain, the Heart of Lorkhan remains within reach of people who can descend far enough into the mountain. The mountain gives that connection a particular weight. The religious background is preserved in books such as The Monomyth, which gathers different accounts of creation. These traditions describe divine beings participating in the formation of the mortal world, with Lorkhan closely involved in bringing that undertaking about. Their explanations differ over what he intended and what the other spirits understood. Some describe deception and a terrible loss. Others give the making of the world a more favorable meaning. Reading these accounts together, the Nerevarine encounters cultures arguing over the origin of the same world they inhabit.

In the Altmeri account included in The Monomyth, Lorkhan's involvement leads to judgment by the other gods. Trinimac removes his Heart, but destroying it proves beyond them. Auri-El sends it away, and its resting place becomes associated with the mountain. Within that telling, the Heart's survival belongs to the story of creation itself. The gods can punish Lorkhan, yet the part removed from him continues to exist within the world whose making he helped bring about. That account gives the buried object an origin, though Tamriel's peoples don't all share the Altmeri judgment of Lorkhan. Human traditions can regard the figure responsible for mortal existence with much greater sympathy. The name and character of that figure also change between traditions. The Monomyth lets those differences remain visible rather than arranging every religion into a single agreed account. Enthir's participation brings a practical complication into Arniel's research. An item can have been arranged for and still remain unavailable until someone settles the business surrounding its delivery. The Dragonborn becomes the person moving between the scholar and the supplier. Arniel may be thinking about the Dwemer and divine forces, but his experiment depends on exchanges conducted among living people, with their own conditions and frustrations.

One of the objects that reaches the project is a warped soul gem. Arniel intends to use it in place of the power source involved in the ancient Dwemer work. The difference is already substantial. Kagrenac worked with the Heart of Lorkhan beneath Red Mountain. Arniel has a portable gem that requires further preparation before he considers it suitable. The gem gives him something he can handle and alter within the limits of his own research. Its preparation takes the Dragonborn away from the College and back into Dwemer sites.

Arniel supplies a spell called Arniel's Convection and directs the Dragonborn to Dwemer convectors. These are surviving devices within the ruins, and the task requires a particular use of them. The warped soul gem goes inside a convector, the spell is applied to the apparatus, and the gem is retrieved afterward. Arniel's instructions turn a piece of ancient machinery into a working part of a modern experiment.

The treatment must be repeated at other convectors before the gem is ready to return to Arniel.

Keening is much harder to treat as an ordinary research supply. The blade belongs to Kagrenac's original set of tools, and Arniel needs it for the experiment's final stage. Its delivery becomes another problem for the Dragonborn to resolve through Enthir. The missing courier must be found, and Keening recovered so that it can reach the College. By the time Arniel receives it, the project has drawn on loose components, fixed Dwemer installations, a specially treated soul gem and an artifact carried out of a failed delivery.

In Morrowind, Keening belongs alongside Sunder and Wraithguard in the work performed upon the Heart. Arniel's equipment is different. He doesn't have the Heart, and he doesn't assemble the complete original set of tools. His ability to handle Keening in Skyrim also comes without an explanation that settles the dangers described in Morrowind. For his experiment, the blade provides a direct connection to Kagrenac's craft. The gem and the preparations around it belong to Arniel's attempt to recreate conditions with the materials available to him.

When the preparations are finished, Arniel asks the Dragonborn to attend the experiment at the College. This time the adventurer's role changes. There are no more components to retrieve or convectors to locate. Arniel performs the work himself, with the Dragonborn present to witness it. He applies Keening to the prepared gem. The attempt doesn't immediately give him the result he expects, and he continues. Then Arniel vanishes. The room that held the scholar and his assembled experiment no longer contains the man who had been directing it.

There's an immediate consequence for the Dragonborn. After Arniel disappears, the player gains the ability to summon Arniel's Shade. The summoned figure can assist in combat, so Arniel's connection with the world hasn't ended in an entirely inaccessible absence. A recognizable shade can be called into the Dragonborn's presence. That gives the quest a result beyond an empty space at the College. It doesn't provide a conversation in which Arniel explains where he went, what he experienced, or whether the outcome matched what he'd hoped to achieve.

The comparison with Kagrenac is close enough to matter. A scholar investigating the Dwemer uses Keening on a prepared source of power and disappears during the attempt. Unlike the disputed final moments at Red Mountain, this happens with the player present. Yet the differences remain part of the event: a warped soul gem, preparation through convectors, a single researcher and an incomplete set of instruments. Arniel's disappearance shows that his smaller arrangement can produce an extraordinary effect. Extending that result to every missing Dwemer would require answers his experiment never supplies.


Yagrum Bagarn gives the disappearance a human scale, even though he isn't human. In the corprusarium beneath Tel Fyr, a Dwemer can still speak about the people whose cities stand empty. He remembers colleagues and their disagreements. He returned from what he calls an Outer Realm and searched for his people. Any explanation of their fate has to leave room for him, living with illness under Divayth Fyr's care, unable to supply the answer that later scholars would most like to hear.

The most direct reading is a catastrophic failure in Kagrenac's work. He had the Heart beneath Red Mountain, instruments made to act upon its power, and a project to construct Anumidium. In Vivec's account, Kagrenac uses his tools as the battle turns against the Dwemer, and the disappearance follows. Read that way, an unfinished or misunderstood procedure overtakes the people connected with it. Yagrum's report that Kagrenac's colleagues disputed his conclusions gives this interpretation a particular reason to be considered. Doubt existed among people who understood the work more closely than later explorers could.

The difficulty comes when that account is placed beside Nerevar at Red Mountain. There, Dagoth kills Kagrenac, and an intervention guided by Azura removes the Dwemer's borrowed immortality. The action responsible for their end has changed, along with the person performing it. The Nordic songs arrange the participants differently again. These differences leave the catastrophic reading broad: the Heart project may have brought disaster, while the surviving accounts disagree about whose action caused it. Choosing a precise final sequence would mean choosing between stories that don't fit together.

The Rourken make the scale of that disaster harder to picture. Their migration carried them west, away from the alliance in Resdayn and toward the homeland remembered in Hammerfell's traditions. If their disappearance belongs to the same event, the destruction of a chamber under Red Mountain can't explain the geographical reach by itself. Something about the event would have had to extend beyond the immediate battlefield. The surviving histories provide no apparatus that can be followed between those distant settlements, so a proposed connection has to remain part of the interpretation.

A transformation associated with Numidium begins from another concrete feature of Kagrenac's work. Anumidium was an attempt to construct a god using the Heart. Divine Metaphysics concerns that project and its instruments, while The Egg of Time argues against a feared danger involved in linking to a divine source. Together, those works put a change in the Dwemer's condition within the concerns of their own scholarship. The idea of transformation therefore has a place in this discussion, although the books don't deliver a description of the people after the disappearance.

The familiar suggestion that the Dwemer became Numidium's skin gives that transformation an especially definite shape. It imagines a particular destination within the brass god for an entire people. No surviving account confirms that the Dwemer became Numidium's skin. Numidium's later history doesn't fill the gap. In Daggerfall, the struggle concerns the Totem and the Mantella, with competing outcomes reflected in the Warp in the West. Those events demonstrate extraordinary consequences around a later activation, under different conditions. They leave Kagrenac's original result unresolved.

A broader transformation reading can still be considered without deciding what substance, spirit or component the Dwemer became. Kagrenac's project concerned divine power, and the disappearance is repeatedly tied to his work. Yet Yagrum can't describe a completed transformation, and the Nerevarine finds no population waiting inside the machinery at Red Mountain. During Morrowind's climax, using the tools to break the Heart's enchantments ends Dagoth Ur's dependence upon it and cuts off the Tribunal's source. It doesn't produce a return of the missing Dwemer. That particular intervention leaves their absence unchanged.

Displacement beyond ordinary existence offers a third reading, prompted in part by Yagrum's survival. He says he was elsewhere when the disappearance happened. That makes his absence relevant, because he shares the ancestry of the missing people while having been outside their usual world at the crucial time. Perhaps location affected who was caught in the event. His account doesn't identify the Outer Realm precisely or describe a passage taken by anyone else. It gives this interpretation an exception to explain, rather than a route that another traveler can follow.

Arniel Gane's experiment makes an altered state easier to imagine. After his disappearance, the Dragonborn can summon his shade, so his loss from the College has a continuing consequence that can be encountered directly. But Arniel worked upon a warped soul gem with Keening, without the Heart or the full original instruments. His result belongs to that arrangement. It gives a comparison for disappearance accompanied by some continuing presence, while leaving the Dwemer without an equivalent summons, conversation or known place of arrival. Repeating his preparations wouldn't amount to a demonstrated means of recovering them.

The functioning ruins help distinguish the missing people from the places they left behind. Doors still respond to the appropriate devices. Nchardak's pumps change water levels when Neloth and the Dragonborn operate its systems. Automata continue to challenge visitors in passages whose builders are gone. A catastrophic failure could leave equipment intact, and either transformation or displacement could also leave these structures behind. The machinery preserves practical traces of Dwemer work without selecting one of those explanations. Its surviving operations have become part of other people's lives, research and dangers.

In Markarth, that inheritance is especially ordinary. People live within Dwemer construction while Calcelmo studies material beneath Understone Keep. Beyond the inhabited spaces, Nchuand-Zel still contains defenders capable of making an expedition fail. A visitor can learn enough to open a route or restart a system and still know little about the people who made it. The disappearance remains unresolved, but these smaller encounters have definite outcomes. A mechanism accepts the right device. A door opens into another surviving chamber, and someone who isn't Dwemer passes through.
