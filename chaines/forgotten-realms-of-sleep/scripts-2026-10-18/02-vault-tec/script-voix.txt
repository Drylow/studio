At the terminals, the Sole Survivor can change the settings. In the workshop, they can build a place where Clem and the other newcomers have a home.


Vault 76 opens with its residents alive and ready to leave. The shelter has carried them through the destruction outside, and Reclamation Day brings the task they were selected to perform. They're supposed to rebuild America. Inside, the celebration has already passed by the time the player wakes, and the remaining journey through the vault leads toward the surface. The rooms, instructions, and supplies belong to a shelter preparing to empty itself. Life underground has reached its intended end, with work waiting beyond the door.

Vault 76 is a control vault. Its population was chosen for the skills and abilities Vault-Tec expected would help establish a new society after the war. That gives its familiar equipment a practical purpose. Residents collect a Pip-Boy and receive supplies before leaving, while the C.A.M.P. provides a means of establishing a place to work and live outside. The expectation is that these people will carry their training into Appalachia. The vault has preserved a community that now has to learn how to function without its enclosed surroundings.

The overseer has gone ahead of them. Her recordings allow residents to follow her route, beginning with the camp she establishes outside the vault. There, the work of reclamation becomes ordinary and immediate. A person needs somewhere to prepare food, make equipment, and put useful materials together. Her camp gives the departing residents an example they can use. After years in a building where essential systems were already installed, they're entering a region where shelter will often require gathering the materials and constructing it themselves.

Alongside that public assignment, the overseer carries a separate order. Vault-Tec wants her to secure the nuclear silos named Alpha, Bravo, and Charlie. She is to claim them for the company, regardless of competing claims, including those of surviving government or military personnel. The residents' mission concerns rebuilding a country, while her private instructions reserve some of its most destructive surviving facilities for Vault-Tec. Her position requires her to carry both assignments into the same damaged region, without knowing who or what she'll find there.

Those orders give the overseer's journey a difficulty that successful vault maintenance couldn't prepare her to settle. Vault-Tec could keep residents supplied underground and arrange the terms of their departure. Beyond the entrance, the overseer encounters the consequences of decisions made by people who survived without that protection. Towns have their own histories of emergency and loss. Organizations have formed, worked, and collapsed. Securing a facility for the company's use offers no immediate answer to the sickness spreading through Appalachia or the absence of the people who once cared for its settlements.

In Flatwoods, the overseer's route meets the work left behind by the Responders. They were emergency workers and volunteers who tried to help survivors after the bombs. Their training systems remain available even though the people who organized them are gone. A new arrival can learn through those surviving instructions, taking on tasks connected with water, food, and medical aid. For someone emerging from Vault 76, this is an education in what reclamation already meant to people outside. They'd been attempting it long before the vault door opened.

The Responders' remains at Morgantown Airport make the failure of that effort harder to overlook. Their facilities and records preserve an organization trying to cope with the Scorched, and the player follows work aimed at understanding and resisting the disease. The overseer's recordings register the devastation she finds along her route. Her residents were selected to help rebuild, but selection alone hasn't equipped them with everything they need. Knowledge gathered by the Responders becomes part of their preparation. The vault's graduates have to become learners again, using work that people on the surface didn't live to finish.

The Scorched also change what settling the region requires. Establishing a camp won't remove the danger, and repairing a building won't protect its occupants from infection. The original journey draws the player toward the Responders' inoculation research and through the remains of other groups that confronted the same catastrophe. The Fire Breathers, the Free States, and the Brotherhood of Steel each leave records and equipment behind.

The overseer's response to a nuclear launch away from a fissure site makes her doubts about Vault-Tec explicit. In that recording, she condemns using the weapons against other people and questions what Vault-Tec has set in motion. The original story allows nuclear force a role in confronting the scorchbeast threat, but it also leaves the residents capable of directing that force elsewhere. Access to the silos therefore places a responsibility on people who were raised to regard themselves as builders. The overseer can't make their decisions for them once they're outside.

The silo orders tell us what Vault-Tec wanted this overseer to do after the war. They don't settle who began the Great War or supply a single explanation for every vault. Vault 76's own history remains important here: it protected its population and released them to rebuild. Its success as a shelter coexists with instructions that give the company a claim over nuclear weapons. A resident can benefit from the years of protection and still have reason to reject what Vault-Tec wants done with the world beyond the entrance.

In Appalachia, the residents' most useful inheritance grows as they work. They bring equipment and training out of Vault 76, then add the Responders' lessons, recovered research, and the results of their own efforts. The overseer's camp offers an early place to begin, with workbenches ready for people who have only just left home. Farther out, each resident can establish another camp and make something the next person might use. A water source, repaired equipment, a place to prepare food: these are things the surviving residents can build and maintain in the country they've entered.
