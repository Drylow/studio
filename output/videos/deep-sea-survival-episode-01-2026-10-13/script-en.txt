You are halfway through pulling in your fishing net when the water underneath your boat turns black. Something down there has stopped the net. You let go, but the rope keeps sliding across the deck, faster now, straight toward your ankle.

You jump onto the cabin step. The rope tightens against the railing and tears the metal loose. Your boat tilts. An empty bucket rolls overboard, and something beneath the surface closes around it. You see a pale eye, wider than the cabin window, before the water covers it again.

The net is gone. So is part of your railing. You are still standing, which puts you ahead of the boat beside you. Its stern rises out of the water, hangs there for a moment, and disappears. Nobody comes back up. You grab the radio and call the harbor. Someone answers, but all you catch is, "Stay away from the channel."

That channel is your only route home.

On day one, you try the engine anyway. It starts, coughs, and stops. A torn section of net has wound itself around the propeller. Normally, you could climb down and clear it. You lean over the broken railing, and that huge eye rolls upward to meet you. You step back inside. The engine can wait.

Your boat was stocked for a fishing trip. There is food in a cooler, drinking water under the bench, a repair kit, and an emergency raft packed behind the cabin. That container usually gets in your way. Today, you leave it right where it is.

You turn the cabin light off before sunset. Other boats are doing the same. For a while, their dark shapes sit around you, and that feels better than being alone. Then a purple tentacle folds around a boat farther out. The cabin crushes sideways. Its radio keeps transmitting after the lights have gone underwater.

You reach for your own radio. Before you press the button, something knocks against your hull.

On day two, you wait for daylight to check the damage. There are deep scrapes along the red paint. They end just below the cabin, where the radio battery is mounted on the other side of the wall. You switch the radio on. A knock follows. You switch it off, and the next knock takes longer.

That could be a coincidence. You would really like it to be a coincidence. You turn the radio back on for a quick call. This time, the floor shifts beneath your feet. You shut it off and crawl away from the wall. Until you know more, you keep anything that vibrates quiet.

The cabin has a folded chart in a drawer. You spread it over the bench. The shipping lane runs beyond a chain of navigation buoys, past a rocky island with an old service landing. If you could reach the island, you might get out of the water entirely. But the current is carrying you sideways, away from it.

You find a spare oar beneath the bench and start paddling. The island gets smaller. You check the chart again and realize the current is carrying you faster than you can paddle.

By day three, a shape follows the boat under the surface, moving slowly enough to stay beside you. Sometimes you can see its back. Sometimes it fades into the deeper blue. Every scrape makes you lift your feet.

You drop a loose piece of railing over the stern. It strikes the hull on the way down. The shadow turns toward the sound and follows the sinking metal. For the first time since the net vanished, the water directly beneath you is clear.

You push with the oar immediately. It buys you a little distance, until the shadow returns. You stop paddling and sit down. The broken railing could distract it. You have more loose metal aboard. You also have a boat that still needs most of its pieces.

On day four, something smaller reaches you first. A gray fin moves between the boat and the floating remains of your net. Then a second fin appears, circling the scraps. You watch from the cabin doorway until a jaw rises from the water and snaps the rope hanging over the stern.

You pull every trailing line onto the deck. One is caught beneath the boat. You give it a tug, and the shark hits the hull hard enough to throw you onto your back. You abandon the rope. It can keep the rope. You would rather keep your arm.

Rain arrives that night. You put the clean containers outside and hold the cabin door against the wind. The rain is useful. The wind carries you farther from the island. By morning, the last nearby boat is only a mast sticking out of the water.

On day six, you open the cooler and find seawater inside. The shark's impact knocked it against the broken rail, cracking the corner. You move the food that stayed sealed into the cabin. The rest goes overboard. Fins gather beside the stern almost immediately, and you shut the door before they start hitting anything else.

You clean the cabin floor and find water creeping under the bench. It comes back after you wipe it away. Outside, the same section of hull has a fresh dent.

You press a repair pad against the damaged spot from inside. It slows the leak. You keep the bucket beside it. That night, you wake up to the bucket sliding across the floor. The boat has tilted again. You empty it, brace it against the bench, and stay awake until morning.

Day eight brings an engine sound. You climb onto the cabin step and wave your yellow jacket above your head. A fishing boat is moving toward the buoys. You can see someone at the wheel. They see you too. The boat turns in your direction.

Then the dark shape beneath you rolls away and crosses toward them. You wave harder, trying to make them stop. Their engine gets louder. The water rises under their bow, and their boat disappears behind a wall of spray. When the spray falls, the boat is still there, but its bow points toward the sky.

You fasten your jacket again. Nobody reaches you. If rescue arrives the same way, calling it closer could get everyone killed.

On day ten, the purple tentacle comes for your cabin. You hear it dragging across the roof before you see the tip against the window. The suction cups flatten against the glass. You crawl toward the door, but the tentacle follows the wall and blocks it.

You squeeze out through the opposite window. Your cap catches on the frame. You pull it free and land on the narrow side deck. The tentacle breaks the first window behind you. Glass scatters across the bench where you were sleeping.

You grab a metal pan from the doorway and throw it over the broken railing. It hits the water beside the stern. The tentacle loosens and slides toward the splash. You run to the bow before it can feel along the deck again. When it finally sinks away, the roof sags above the bench.

You drag the raft container onto the foredeck, where you can reach it without going back inside. The pan bought you time. Your cabin no longer gives you somewhere safe to sleep.

By day twelve, the island has disappeared behind the horizon. The chart still gives you one possible destination: a large bell buoy marking the outer edge of the shipping lane. It has a maintenance platform above the water and a steel frame around the bell. You have seen it before. From your boat, you hated its constant clanging.

Now, a sound coming from somewhere else sounds useful. You watch the horizon through the cabin's small binoculars. For most of the day, you see waves. Near sunset, a red mark appears between them. It vanishes, comes back, and vanishes again. You keep the binoculars pointed there until your arms start shaking.

You are drifting toward the buoy. If the boat holds together, you may not need the engine at all.

On day fourteen, you start moving supplies into a waterproof bag from the cabin. Sealed food, water, the chart, a handheld radio from the emergency kit, and a small signal light. You keep the boat radio off. The handheld stays off too. You tuck the bag beside the raft and sleep on the deck, away from the broken cabin roof.

The leak is faster now. You can still empty the bucket, but every trip leaves you more tired. You mark the water level on the inside wall. After a short rest, it has reached the mark again. You move your supplies higher, onto the cabin step, and leave the bucket floating under the bench.

On day sixteen, the sea goes oddly smooth around the boat. You lean over the remaining rail. The dark creature is directly beneath the hull, so close that its back blocks the sunlight through the water. It lifts, and the boat rises with it.

The repair pad comes loose inside. You hear water rushing under the floor. The creature drops away, but the boat stays crooked. You climb over the sloping deck, pull the raft clear of the rail, and get it into the water. It opens beside the boat, bright orange against the dark sea.

The raft is ready. You stay aboard for one more night because the buoy is still too far away to see clearly without the binoculars. You want the bigger boat between you and the creature for as long as you can keep it.

On day eighteen, the cabin fills before you can finish moving the last supplies. A wave rolls through the broken window and pushes the bench loose. Your storage bag slides toward it. You catch the bag with the oar, drag it back, and throw it into the raft.

The next wave knocks you off your feet. Water sweeps across the deck and carries you through the missing section of railing. Your jacket pulls you upward. You break the surface beside the red hull, reach for the raft, and grab an empty handful of water. The raft has swung around the bow.

You kick toward it. Beneath your feet, something pale moves across the dark shape. Teeth. You grab the rope linking the raft to the boat and pull yourself along it until your hand reaches the orange side. You haul yourself inside and lie there, coughing seawater onto the floor.

The boat's stern is already underwater. As the bow rises, it pulls the raft toward the sinking cabin. You release the connecting line and push away with the oar. Your cap is still on your head. Your boat is standing almost upright in front of you.

The huge eye appears beside the cabin window. The creature rolls under the hull, and the last red section slides into the water. Your empty bucket floats up afterward. You watch it bob beside the place where you were sleeping.

For the next two days, you barely use the oar. Every time it bumps the raft, a wake appears behind you. You let the current do the work and check the red buoy whenever the waves lift you high enough. It is getting closer. Slowly, but close enough now that you can hear the bell.

On day twenty-one, the wake changes direction. You brace yourself for a hit, but the dark shape passes beneath the raft and continues toward the buoy. The bell strikes again. You watch a swell climb the buoy's side before it sinks away.

The creature is following the vibration there too. That gives you room to paddle. It also means your destination already has something waiting beneath it.

You reach the buoy on day twenty-three. Its maintenance ladder is slippery and covered in weed. You wait for the raft to lift alongside it, grab a rung, and climb onto the small platform with your bag and oar. You tie the raft beside the ladder. When you look down, the creature's eye is below the bell's steel frame.

You climb higher, above the bell. The steel shakes with each wave. For the first time in days, you can stretch your legs without moving a boat. You open the handheld radio and make a short call. A voice answers clearly enough to understand.

There are ships moving through the outer lane. Small craft are being picked up near the marker buoys. You give the buoy's number from its plate and tell them about the shape underneath it. The voice tells you to stay on the platform. You turn the radio off and put it back in the bag.

On day twenty-five, a storm makes the buoy swing so hard that you cannot stay on the platform. You climb inside the upper frame and hold on. The raft slams against the ladder below you. Its rope stretches, snaps, and disappears into the spray.

You lose your way back onto the water. The bag stays with you because you have looped its strap through the frame. You pull it onto your knees and wait for the wind to ease. Through the rain, you see the purple tentacle rise beside the bell, then fold back into the waves.

By morning, the buoy's lower platform is bent. The bell has jammed against its frame. It no longer rings. You look down and see the creature turn back toward you as soon as your shoe slips against the steel.

On day twenty-seven, a large cargo ship appears in the outer lane. It is far enough away to look safe. You switch on the signal light and point it toward the bridge. A reply flashes from the ship. Later, the handheld radio crackles with your buoy number.

They can see you. Their crew is preparing a pickup boat, but you warn them about the fishing boat that tried to help. You describe the engine noise, the wake, and the eye. The reply takes a while. When it comes, they tell you the pickup boat will stop short of the buoy. You will have to reach it.

You look at the water where your raft used to be. The creature is still beneath you. The bell is silent. You climb down far enough to reach the bent part of its frame, then climb back up before the steel moves again. If you can free the bell, you might move that eye to the other side.

On day twenty-nine, you spend the daylight trying to shift the bent bracket with the end of the oar. You saved the oar when you climbed off the raft. Until now, it has mostly been in the way. You brace it against the frame and push. The bracket moves a little, then holds.

You stop before the wood breaks. Wait for a wave. Push again as the buoy rolls. This time, the bracket bends far enough for the bell to swing. It rings once, and a dark swell moves under the platform. You pull your feet onto the ladder and climb away from it.

Day thirty begins with a pickup boat sitting beyond the buoy. Its engine is quiet. A crew member in a yellow jacket throws a floating line toward the platform. It lands short, and they pull it back. The second throw reaches the ladder. You catch it and hold on.

Below you, the creature turns. You wait for the buoy to roll. The bell strikes, and the swell moves toward the far side of the frame. You climb down the ladder, keeping the rescue line across your shoulder, and step into the water.

Your jacket brings you back up. The crew pulls. You keep your feet near the surface and watch the buoy slide away behind you. A wake turns toward the line. Then the bell rings again. The wake bends back around the red marker, close enough that you can see the pale eye beside the platform.

Hands grab your jacket and lift you over the pickup boat's side. You land on the floor beside an open supply bag. Someone puts a bottle of water into your hand. You can hear the cargo ship's horn beyond them, and you can see its ladder waiting above the water.

The crew hauls the line aboard and turns toward the ship. Behind you, the buoy rings again. You look back once. The dark swell circles beneath the empty platform where you spent the night. Then the cargo ship fills your view, and a crew member takes your hand on the first rung.
