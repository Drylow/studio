The hill on the postcard is not a steep one. A cobbled street rises gently between houses, then levels out beside a stone wall. At the top stands a bookshop with a dark blue door. A climbing rose follows the line of its window, and above the shop, a lamp shines behind a curtain patterned with small leaves.

Mara tells you that it is the last shop on the hill. Beyond it, the street becomes a lane between gardens, with a view over the valley when the weather is clear. People sometimes walk up without intending to buy a book. They like the window, the bench outside, and the mild sense of arrival at the end of the street.

The postcard shows the bench after rain. Its wooden seat is dark, and a little water has gathered in the hollows of the cobbles. The blue door stands open beneath an awning. You can imagine stepping inside, leaving the soft patter of the street behind and finding the warmer sound of footsteps on an old wooden floor.

Niall is at a table near the back, sorting a small collection of books. He has laid a square of felt beneath them to protect their covers. Some are clothbound, their colours mellowed by sunlight. Others have paper jackets with painted gardens, coastlines, or little houses among trees. He lifts each book gently before deciding where it belongs.

He does not arrange everything by subject. There are shelves where he keeps a more personal order: books for rainy afternoons, books to take to a garden seat, books that feel companionable when read a few pages at a time. Customers have grown familiar with these quiet distinctions. They often begin by telling him where they would like to read.

One woman reads at her kitchen table while bread is in the oven. A man who lives near the river carries a book to the bench beneath the pear tree. Someone else likes a short chapter before the evening train reaches home. Niall listens to these descriptions carefully. The place can be as useful as the kind of book they mention.

Today, he is making a little display beside the window. A low stand holds a book about gardens, open at a drawing of an espaliered pear tree. Beside it, he places an essay collection with a green cover and a small volume of poems. A smooth pebble keeps a handwritten card from curling in the warm air above the radiator.

The card simply says that these are books to linger with. Niall wrote the words slowly, leaving generous space around them. He likes a window that does not demand too much attention. Someone passing in the rain can glance at it, understand its invitation, and carry that pleasant thought on to the bakery or the lane beyond the hill.

There is a scent of paper in the shop, mingled with the polish he uses on the counter. Near the door, a mat has taken on the fresh smell of rain. The shelves make narrow, comfortable spaces, with enough room to turn and browse. Small lamps sit where their light can fall on the pages rather than into anyone's eyes.

You might choose the chair beneath the staircase. Its arms are broad, and its cushion has settled into a welcoming shape. From there, you could see the street through the lower part of the window. Shoes passing beneath umbrellas. The edge of a basket. A dog pausing to consider the damp base of the stone wall before continuing up the hill.

Niall keeps a stack of plain paper on his table. When he finds a passage he particularly likes, he makes a note for himself, sometimes no more than a word or the name of a tree. The notes are not a catalogue. They are a way of letting a small pleasure stay near him after he has closed the book.

On other sheets, he writes his own thoughts. This afternoon, he takes up a pencil and leaves a short sentence in the middle of a page. Leave a little room in the day for something you enjoy. He looks at it for a moment, then sets the page beside the window display, where it can accompany the books.

He is thinking of very modest pleasures. Cutting a pear into a bowl. Turning a pot so its leaves receive the light. Sitting beside an open window long enough to notice the scent of rain. There is room for any of these in the sentence, and plenty of room for a reader to imagine something entirely different.

A visitor arrives carrying a folded umbrella. Her name is Nora, and she lives in one of the houses along the garden lane. She is looking for a notebook with paper that will take a soft pencil. Niall brings out a few from a drawer, placing them on the counter so that she can feel the covers and turn the pages.

Nora chooses a small one bound in grey linen. The paper is cream, with a faint texture that pleases her fingertips. She has begun keeping notes about her garden, she explains. Not a strict record, just things she notices. Which patch of wall receives the last sun, or where the first leaves appear when the weather begins to warm.

There are drawings too, though she says they are very simple. A seed head, a row of pots, the shape a leaf makes when it curls. Niall tells her that simple drawings are often the ones he likes best. They leave a little space for the person looking, much as a few words can suggest a whole familiar afternoon.

He offers to wrap the notebook, and Nora watches him fold the brown paper around its corners. His fingers make neat creases without pressing them too hard. A length of cotton ribbon holds the parcel together. It is pale blue, almost the colour of the rain outside, and its soft knot will be easy to untie at home.

While he finishes, she reads the page beside the window. She smiles at the sentence and says that her notebook is exactly that sort of room. A little place in the day for looking at things. Niall asks whether she would like the page as well. She folds it carefully and slips it beneath the ribbon around her parcel.

They stand by the door for a moment, watching the rain fall beyond the awning. Nora can see her garden lane from there, and the walk home will be pleasant beneath her umbrella. She thanks him, then steps onto the cobbles. The blue ribbon disappears into her basket, and the umbrella opens like a small, moving roof above her.

Up the lane, her house has a porch with a chair facing the garden. Nora leaves her umbrella where it can dry and takes the parcel inside. The kettle goes on, and the notebook rests beside her cup. She smooths Niall's page with her palm before setting it between the cover and the first fresh sheet of paper.

Her first drawing is of the porch chair. She does not try to capture every curve. A few lines are enough to recall the broad seat, the worn arms, and the way the back leans towards the wall. Beneath it, she writes that the chair is a particularly good place to listen to rain falling from the garden leaves.

Then she closes the notebook and sits there herself. The rain has softened to a fine shower, barely reaching beneath the porch roof. Across the path, a row of pots shines in the mild light. Her tea is warm, the chair is comfortable, and the small new book lies within reach, with many pages she can leave for other days.

At the bookshop, Niall returns to the table. The collection is almost sorted. He places the last clothbound volume on a shelf near the chair beneath the stairs, where someone will be able to reach it without standing. He straightens the felt beneath his remaining papers, then brushes a little dust from the table with a broad, soft brush.

The shop is quiet enough now for reading. Niall takes the green essay collection from the window and opens it where a ribbon marks his place. He reads a page about a kitchen garden, then another about a walk beside water. The sentences are pleasant company. From time to time, he looks up at the changing light beyond the glass.

You do not need to know the words of the book to imagine the scene. The lamp beside him. The page held loosely in one hand. A little rain tracing the window while the street outside grows less distinct. There is a comfortable companionship between a quiet room and someone reading in it, with each leaving the other space.

When he has finished the short passage, Niall lets the book rest open on his knee. He remembers a garden he once visited, where thyme grew between paving stones and a shallow bowl collected rainwater beneath a tree. He had sat there with a cup of tea. The memory is complete enough without any need to know when it happened.

Later, he puts the book back in the window display. He replaces the page he gave Nora with another sheet, bearing the same simple thought in his own handwriting. Then he places the smooth pebble beside it and adjusts the little lamp. The light catches the grain of the paper, making the space around the words feel warm and generous.

The shop closes gently around its shelves. Niall draws the curtain over the door, leaving the window display softly lit for people walking home. Upstairs, his own room waits, with a kettle, a lamp, and a chair facing the valley. The last shop on the hill becomes a house for the evening, while its books rest quietly below.

On the postcard, the upstairs curtain holds that warm square of light. The cobbled street leads towards it, then continues out of sight among the gardens. You return the picture to the wooden box and hear the train again, its steady sound travelling beneath all the little rooms you have imagined. The green velvet supports you as comfortably as before.

Mara has quietly refreshed your tea. The cup is still warm enough to hold in both hands. A trace of steam rises near the window, where rain and distant lights soften the passing view. She places the next cards beside you without disturbing Pip, curled on his blanket; their pictures carry the journey towards a quiet coast and an orchard waiting beyond the hills.
