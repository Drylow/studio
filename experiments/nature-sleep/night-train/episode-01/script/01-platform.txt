The first postcard has the colour of honey left in a glass jar. A little station stands beneath a long, sloping roof, with a bench against its wall and a row of lamps along the platform. Someone has painted the sky with the faintest touch of pink. It is an evening caught just before the blue arrives.

Mara lays the card beside your cup. The corners have softened, and a small crescent of paper shows where a thumb has rested many times. On the back, beneath an address written in brown ink, there is a sentence about the station garden. The roses are doing very well this year. That is all the message needs to say.

Beyond your window, rain threads silver lines across the dark. Inside, the reading lamp shines over the postcard, bringing out a little more of the painted brickwork. You can almost feel the mild air beneath that platform roof. And, without leaving the green velvet seat, you begin to imagine the station as it was on the evening of the picture.

Elias is standing beside the garden gate with a folded cloth over his arm. His waistcoat has roomy pockets, and his shirt sleeves are turned back neatly at the wrist. Every evening, when the station grows quiet, he tends the lamps. He has done it for so long that the path between them feels like a familiar tune.

There is no grand ceremony to it. He opens each little door, wipes the glass, and makes sure that the brass reflects the light warmly. The lamps are electric now, though everyone still calls him the lamplighter. Elias likes the old name. It gives a certain dignity to the pleasant business of making a place welcoming after sunset.

Along the platform, the first lamp stands near a barrel planted with rosemary. When Elias leans close to the glass, his sleeve brushes the leaves. Their clean scent rises into the evening. He pauses to turn the barrel a little, giving the fuller side of the plant room beside the wall, then smooths a patch of soil with his fingers.

Farther along, a gardener is coiling a hose in broad, even loops. She has been watering the beds behind the waiting room, where cream roses climb a low trellis. Their petals are open but unhurried, and a few have settled on the gravel. Elias lifts one from the bench and places it with the others beneath the bush.

The gardener asks whether he would like a cutting in the spring. He says that he would, and they consider the sunny patch beside his kitchen door. It is a small conversation, the sort that can continue next week without either person forgetting where it ended. For now, they settle on a rose with a quiet, warm scent.

You might notice the station sounds around them. A brush passes over paving stones. A cup rests in its saucer inside the tea room. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a bicycle freewheels for a moment, then grows quiet. None of these sounds takes the whole evening. Each has its little place, and there is ample space between them.

Inside the waiting room, the walls are the colour of oatmeal. A wooden rail runs beneath a row of framed pictures, and the seats have cushions covered in blue ticking. Elias opens the upper part of a window to let in the garden air. A curtain lifts, settles, and lifts again, moving only enough to show the breeze.

On the broad windowsill stands a small table lamp with a milk glass shade. Its base is green, a softer green than the velvet of your train seat, and its cord is wound loosely beside it. The lamp has spent the afternoon here while its owner, Ruth, visits a friend in the village. She will collect it on her walk home.

Ruth once lived in the cottage beside the station garden. She has moved to a little house near the river, where the kitchen window receives the last of the afternoon light. The lamp is for a table in that window. She mentioned this to Elias earlier, showing him the curved shade and the tiny leaves painted around its rim.

He remembers the leaves now as he folds his polishing cloth. They are not quite alike. One turns outward, another bends towards the stem, and a third has a rounded tip. A person painted them patiently, leaving these small differences behind. Elias likes objects that still carry the movement of someone's hand, even after many years of ordinary use.

He brings the lamp to the tea room, where a clean cloth has been spread across a side table. The woman behind the counter finds a sturdy basket for the journey. She places a folded towel in the bottom, then sets out a little paper bag of biscuits. Ruth always likes something plain and buttery with her evening tea.

Outside, the platform lamps begin to glow. Their light is pale at first, hardly brighter than the sky, but it gathers warmth as the evening deepens. Elias stands beneath the roof and looks along the row. Each pool of light touches the next without quite joining it, so the platform becomes a gentle pattern of brightness and shade.

A train passes through on the farther track, its windows reflecting the garden. Elias raises a hand to someone in the last carriage. He has no need to know whether the person sees him clearly. The gesture belongs to the evening as much as the lamps do, and soon the rails are quiet again beyond the flower beds.

Ruth arrives by the garden gate carrying a bunch of sweet peas wrapped in damp paper. She has been sitting with her friend beneath an open kitchen window. There was tea, she tells Elias, and a plate of sliced pears. They spent most of the visit choosing fabric for new cushions, though they also watched a robin in the hedge.

She places the sweet peas in a jar on the tea room counter. The pale flowers lean together, pink and cream and lavender. Their stems show clearly through the water. Elias turns the jar slightly so that a long tendril can curl into the open space beside it. Ruth says that is exactly the right place for them.

They sit for a while at the table nearest the door. The biscuits are divided without fuss, and the tea is poured into the thick cups the station has always used. A clock moves its hands over a cream face. Outside, the gardener wheels her barrow through the gate, leaving a faint scent of cut grass behind her.

Ruth describes the corner where the lamp will stand. There is a chair with a broad back, a shelf for her books, and a view across the river to a line of willows. In the mornings, she can watch a neighbour tending beans. In the evenings, the glass will reflect the lamp, making a small, warm room within the window.

Elias can picture it easily. He has walked beside that stretch of river, where the path widens around an old pear tree. There is a smooth stone wall nearby, just high enough to sit on. He tells Ruth about the place, and she nods. She has already chosen it for reading when the weather turns warm again.

When they have finished their tea, the lamp settles into the basket. Its green base rests against the folded towel, and Ruth tucks the cord beside it. Elias puts the remaining biscuits on top, where they will travel without being pressed. The basket handle fits comfortably over her arm, and the little shade catches the light as she stands.

He walks with her as far as the gate. The path beyond is dry beneath the trees, with a narrow strip of dusky sky above it. Ruth's house is only a pleasant stroll away. They say good evening, and she follows the path towards the river, carrying the lamp at the same easy pace as her basket of shopping.

For a moment, you can imagine going with her. Past the pear tree, past the low wall, then through a gate painted the colour of sage. The house has a brass latch that fits the palm and a mat woven from thick, pale fibres. Indoors, the air holds the faint fragrance of the bread she baked that morning.

Ruth places the lamp on the little table by the window. The green base sits neatly between a book and a smooth stone brought home from a walk. When the shade lights up, the painted leaves become more distinct. There they are, each slightly different, making their modest circle around a light that has found another pleasant place to shine.

She takes off her cardigan and hangs it over the chair. There will be time to put the kettle on, and time to open the book. First, she draws the curtain partway across the window, leaving a view of the willows. Their branches have become a soft shape beyond the river, too delicate now to separate into individual leaves.

Back at the station, Elias has nearly finished his round. He returns the basket's spare towel to the tea room shelf and rinses the cups. The sweet peas remain on the counter. Tomorrow, people will pause beside them while they wait for tea, perhaps leaning closer to the flowers without quite noticing that they have done so.

He sits on the platform bench before going home. The wood still holds a little warmth from the day. Above him, a lamp shines through glass he has just cleaned, and beside him the rosemary makes its quiet contribution to the air. He rests his hands together and looks along the track towards the softly wooded hills.

On the postcard, this is the moment the painter seems to have kept. The little bench. The amber lamps. A garden settling into evening behind its gate. You do not need to see every detail to understand the place. A room made ready, a cup offered, a familiar path home. The light is there for whoever happens to arrive.

The train carries you on with its low, steady rhythm. Mara leaves the postcard beside the box, where the lamplighter's station glows beneath your reading lamp. Pip has tucked his nose a little closer to his paws on the folded blanket. You lift the cup in both hands, and the warmth of it brings you gently back to your seat.
