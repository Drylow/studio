The next postcard has a pale border that might once have been white. At its centre, a river harbour curves around a cluster of houses, and a flight of shallow steps leads from a green door to the water. Someone has written the name of the place in a careful hand. Below it, in smaller letters, are the words, Tea at Ruth's.

You tilt the card towards the lamp. A pot of lavender stands beside the door, its painted flowers scarcely larger than the marks of a pencil. Through your own window, the forest has thinned. Between the dark trunks lies an open stretch of land, and beyond it a soft grey distance that could quite easily be the sea.

Mara notices the picture resting against your cup. She remembers the green door, and the striped cushions Ruth brings outside when the weather is mild. The house, she says, always smells faintly of lemon peel. Then she continues down the corridor, leaving you with the card and the pleasant task of imagining what might be on the other side of that door.

In your imagining, the harbour is settling into evening. The boats rest along the quay with their covers neatly fastened, and a little water moves beneath the stone steps. Rain has recently passed through. It has left the paving dark and the air fresh, carrying the scent of wet rope, clean stone, and something baking in a house further along the street.

Ruth's lavender brushes against the hem of your coat as you reach the door. You touch the brass handle, polished brightest where other hands have held it, and step into a narrow hallway. There are pegs for coats, a chair for setting down a bag, and a tray where wet shoes may leave their small puddles without troubling anyone.

The room beyond is wider than you expect. Its ceiling is low, and the window looks across the harbour towards willows on the far bank. Ruth sits at a table with an open notebook and a pencil tucked behind her ear. A length of green wool lies beside her teapot. She has been measuring the stems of a plant that leans towards the glass.

She offers you the chair with the cushion sewn from an old striped curtain. Before sitting, you run your hand over the fabric. It has that comfortable softness which comes from years of being washed and dried in the open air. Ruth turns the teapot slightly, bringing its handle within easy reach, and asks whether you would like the blue cup.

You would. The cup has a broad handle and a small irregularity in its rim, a gentle rise made by the potter's thumb. There is no matching saucer. Instead, Ruth sets it on a glazed tile, blue around the edges and cream in the middle. Steam travels over the tile, then disappears in the light from the window.

Her notebook remains open between you. Along one side are the tide times, copied in tidy columns. On the other are observations which have gradually spread into the margins. The parsley needed turning. The lavender liked its new pot. Someone returned a borrowed dish with a pear resting in it. Ruth writes down things she enjoys noticing again.

She does not read every entry aloud. She simply moves the notebook so that you can see a small drawing of the harbour steps. Each step has been given a line of its own. Beside the drawing is a sketch of a flowerpot, and beneath that, a reminder to ask June about a plant with silver leaves.

You remember the greenhouse on an earlier postcard, its panes warmed from within. It is easy to picture June choosing a cutting and wrapping the roots in damp cloth. Perhaps the little parcel came here on this very train, nestled in a basket on the luggage shelf. For a moment, the line of railway becomes a thread between two windows.

Ruth lifts a pot from the sill to show you the plant. Its leaves are narrow and softly furred, with tiny beads of water caught along their edges. She has given it a quarter turn so it will grow evenly. A wooden label rests in the soil. The writing has faded, but June's rounded letters are still visible.

Beside the plant is a shallow bowl of smooth stones. They range from pale oat grey to the dark brown of a well used teapot. Ruth selects one and places it in your palm. It is pleasantly cool. A thin white line crosses its surface, bending in a shape that resembles the harbour road on the postcard.

You turn the stone over with your thumb while Ruth pours more tea. Outside, the water has reached the lowest visible step. It pauses there in small folds, reflecting a stretch of wall and a little of the cloudy sky. A gull stands on a post with its head tucked comfortably into its feathers. The whole view seems to have found a resting place.

On the kitchen shelf, an old clock moves through its quiet work. Its ticking is softer than the sound of Ruth setting down the teapot. The room has gathered many such modest sounds: the shift of a chair cushion, the light touch of a spoon against pottery, a cupboard door closing against a folded piece of felt.

Ruth tells you that Ada made the felt pad from an offcut. It is tucked into the corner where the cupboard used to tap against its frame. There are several small improvements like this throughout the house. A loop of ribbon helps open a stubborn drawer. A wooden bead makes the blind cord easier to find in the dusk.

She stands to bring a plate from the sideboard. On it are slices of bread, a small dish of butter, and a spoonful of something golden. The bread came from the orchard bakery. The golden preserve is made from pears, cooked slowly until the pieces hold their shape inside the syrup. Ruth spreads a little on the heel of a loaf.

You take a slice and let the warmth of the tea return to your hands. There is a faint taste of lemon in the preserve, and the bread has a soft centre beneath its floury crust. Across the room, a cupboard holds more jars, each labelled with a fruit and a small drawing of the tree or bush it came from.

When you ask about the drawings, Ruth looks pleased. They began with a label that had grown damp and difficult to read. A sketch of a pear proved useful, and then she found she enjoyed making the leaves. Now the jars carry whole little branches. One label even includes the corner of a fence where the raspberries grow.

She brings the pencil back to her notebook. Under the entry about the plant, she draws the stone you have been holding. You place it on the table so she can see its white line. Her drawing is simple and slightly larger than the real thing. It catches the curve of the stone without needing to describe every mark.

A fine rain begins again outside. It gathers first on the window ledge, beading along the paint, then settles into a soft brushing sound against the glass. Ruth watches it for a while before reaching for the wool beside her teapot. She is making a loose tie for the tall plant, giving its new stem something gentle to lean against.

You hold the little loop while she measures it. The wool is warm green, close to the colour of the train seat beneath you in that other, quieter place. It slides easily through your fingers. Ruth leaves room around the stem, tests the tie with a light touch, and turns the pot so that its fullest leaves face the room.

The small task changes the windowsill. There is more space between the plants now, and the teapot can sit near the glass without its spout brushing a leaf. Ruth seems satisfied by this. She wipes a ring of water from the painted wood and folds the cloth neatly over the edge of a bowl, ready for another time.

Beyond the window, a neighbour crosses the quay carrying a covered basket, passing beneath the old pear tree. She pauses at the green door long enough to leave a sprig of rosemary in the empty milk jug outside. Ruth sees it and smiles. Later, she says, it will go beside the kettle, where the warmth will bring out its scent whenever she makes a fresh pot.

You find yourself looking once more at the tide notebook. Its pages hold practical things, but they also hold a particular kind of attention. Someone remembered how the parsley grew. Someone noticed the white line in a stone. There is room beside the columns for an ordinary evening, and for the company of a person who stopped to share it.

Ruth closes the book with the pencil marking her place. She slides the bowl of stones back onto the sill and places the one you held at the front. Its pale stripe catches the last of the light. Then she turns on the lamp by the window, and the reflection of its shade joins the harbour beyond the glass.

You recognise the lamp from the little station, where Elias helped Ruth settle it into a basket for the journey home. Its base is green, and tiny leaves circle the rim of its milk glass shade. She has found exactly the right place for it here. Beside her notebook, it lights the page without shining into anyone's eyes, and its painted leaves keep the plants on the sill company.

You leave the green door in your imagination as gently as you entered it. The chair remains by the table, the plant rests against its woollen support, and Ruth carries the rosemary towards the kettle. All these things can continue without being watched. The rain makes its soft pattern on the quay, and the water folds around the harbour steps.

The postcard lies warm between your hands. You are still in the compartment, with the train carrying you past an open field. A raindrop follows a wavering path down the glass. Pip has stretched one paw onto the blanket, his toes loosely gathered, while the rest of him remains tucked into the generous curve of his sleep.

You return Ruth's card to the box, leaving it near the top. Beneath it waits a picture of trees behind a bakery, their branches heavy with leaves. Before reaching for it, you finish the last comfortable mouthful of tea. The flavour lingers, mild and warm, as the wheels find their steady rhythm beneath the polished wooden floor.
