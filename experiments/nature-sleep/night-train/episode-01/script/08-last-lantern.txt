The garden card rests beside the box, where the lamp can reach it. A path winds between low beds of flowers and leads towards a bench beneath a tree. Above the gate hangs a lantern with a deep green frame. Its light has been painted in a soft circle, touching the leaves nearest to it and fading gently into the evening.

You leave the picture there without lifting it. There is pleasure now in simply knowing what it contains. Your cup sits on its saucer, the handle turned towards your hand. The teapot has a small bead of water beneath its spout, and the folded napkin is still neatly placed beside it. These familiar objects have become the landscape closest to you.

Pip stretches beneath the lamp. He reaches his front paws across the blanket, opens them into small soft fans, and lets his body lengthen behind them. Then he gathers himself again with the unhurried care of a cat arranging a favourite place. His tail finds the curve of his side. A little while later, his eyes are closed.

The train enters a stretch of open country. You can sense the change before you see it, as the reflections in the window deepen and the nearby trees fall away. Beyond the wet glass, fields lie beneath a low veil of mist. A hedge passes close to the line, its individual leaves briefly visible before they join the dark again.

Mara comes to collect the empty dishes. She places them on a padded tray, so that the cups make only a small, rounded sound as they settle. The teapot she leaves with you. There is warm water in it still, and she knows how pleasant it can be to pour a little more simply for the comfort of holding the cup.

She notices the garden postcard and looks towards the window. The train will pass a place rather like it, she tells you, where a bench stands beneath a pear tree and the station keeps flowers beside the gate. The lantern there is left on through the evening. You can watch for its light if you happen to be looking out.

Then she draws the little curtain by the corridor a fraction further across. The compartment feels more private, though the doorway remains open enough for her quiet footsteps to be heard. She takes the tray away, carrying the dishes as easily as she brought them. A thread of warm light lies beneath the curtain and rests on the wooden floor.

You bring the postcard box a little closer. Its hinges are made of brass, dulled in the places where fingers rarely touch them. The lid has a shallow mark along one edge, as though it once rested against a buckle or a small stone. Inside, the corners of the cards have softened until the whole collection fits together comfortably.

You begin returning the pictures to their places. The bakery slips beside the harbour, and the greenhouse rests against the bookshop. As you touch their edges, details return without needing to be called back completely. A leaf pressed between recipe pages. The painted rim of a lamp. A cloth folded around a jar. Each is clear for a moment, then settles.

The places have been sharing things all along. Bread travels towards the river. A book travels towards a garden. A plant finds another window, and a length of cloth comes home with a careful line of stitching along its hem. It is a quiet sort of story, carried through familiar hands, with room for a cup of tea wherever it pauses.

You think of the railway beneath the train. From your seat, it is mostly a rhythm, steady under the carpet and the wooden floor. Yet it joins all those ordinary doorways. Somewhere along it, someone is placing a parcel on a shelf. Somewhere else, a kettle has just been filled. Their evenings need not be the same to belong to the same line.

Mara returns without the tray and rests against the doorway for a moment. She has brought a clean cotton cloth for the table, folded small enough to fit beside the box. In its corner is a little stitched leaf. Ada made several for the carriage, she says, choosing a thread that would sit quietly against the pale fabric.

You run a fingertip beside the stitching. The leaf is simple, a curved line and a slender stem, but the thread follows it beautifully. You remember the repaired hem of Tomas's bread cloth and the felt pad on Ruth's cupboard. Ada's work travels into places where she may never sit herself, making a drawer, a table, or a parcel a little more comfortable.

Mara settles the cloth beneath the teapot and watches Pip for a moment. He has tucked his nose into the fur of his foreleg. She smiles, lowers the little lamp shade slightly so that the light rests on the table, and wishes you a pleasant journey. Then her footsteps return to the corridor, soft against the runner that lies along its centre.

The compartment holds the warmth she leaves behind. You draw the loose edge of your blanket across your knees, feeling the weight of it settle evenly. Its weave is fine enough to be smooth, with a gentle roughness where the threads cross. Against the green velvet, the cream wool looks warmer than it did when you first came aboard.

Outside, the rain has become a fine mist. A few drops remain on the glass, catching the lamp in small golden points. One slowly gathers another, grows rounder, and begins to travel downwards. It follows an existing trail for a little way, then turns aside. Behind it, the countryside passes in broad, quiet shapes of field and tree.

You do not notice the train slowing until the rhythm changes beneath you. The intervals between the gentle sounds grow wider. Along the edge of the window, a garden begins to appear: a low stone wall, a gate, and the dark outline of a tree. Then a lantern comes into view, lighting a patch of leaves with the colour of warm honey.

The station is small enough for the garden to seem part of it. Flowers grow beside the waiting room, and a bench sits beneath the tree where its branches make a natural roof. There is no crowd on the platform. A basket rests inside the open doorway, lined with a cloth that has been folded back to show a parcel wrapped in paper.

The train eases to a pause. For a while, the familiar movement beneath your seat becomes stillness, and the soft rain is easier to hear. You remain where you are, with the blanket across your knees. Beyond the glass, the lantern shines on the wet leaves. Its reflection appears beside your reading lamp, as though the garden has borrowed a corner of the compartment.

A station keeper comes out carrying a small parcel. Mara meets her at the doorway of the carriage, and they exchange a few quiet words. The parcel is set beside the basket. A canvas mailbag changes hands with the same comfortable ease. You cannot hear the whole conversation, only its friendly shape and the soft sound of a greeting returned.

The mailbag reminds you of Iona's post office, of thick cream cards and the cup beside her desk. Perhaps a letter from that room is travelling tonight. Perhaps a book waits inside the parcel, with a greenhouse picture tucked into its wrapping. You do not have to decide. It is enough to watch these ordinary things being carried carefully from place to place.

Near the bench, the pear tree holds a few drops along its lowest branch. The lantern catches them when the leaves move. Behind the waiting room window stands a small pot of rosemary, turned so that its fullest side faces the light. You remember Elias brushing past a plant like that, and the clean scent rising from its leaves into the evening air.

The station keeper draws the basket indoors. Mara steps back onto the train, and the carriage door closes with a low, cushioned sound. Soon the movement begins again, almost too gently to notice. The bench slides towards the edge of the window. The green gate follows it, and the lantern remains visible for a moment longer between the branches of the tree.

Then the garden is behind you. Its light has become another quiet place along the line, continuing its evening after the train has gone. The station keeper can set the parcel on a table. The rosemary can rest beside the glass. There is no reason to hold the whole scene in your thoughts; the lantern will keep its small circle of light without your help.

You place the last postcard in the box and close the lid. The little brass catch meets with a soft touch beneath your thumb. You leave the box on the far side of the table, where Mara can find it later. Another passenger may choose the orchard first, or the bookshop, opening the collection into an entirely different, equally pleasant evening.

For you, the pictures have found their places. They sit together now without needing to be separated: warm rooms, wet leaves, flour on a wooden table, a lamp reflected in water. The train carries them as lightly as it carries its own window reflections. Beneath everything is the steady sound of the wheels, returning to the rhythm you have come to know.

Pip breathes quietly on the blanket. A small rise, a gentle fall, and then the same comfortable movement again. His whiskers catch a trace of amber light. You rest your hand nearby, feeling the warmth held in the wool without disturbing him. The compartment smells faintly of tea and clean fabric, with the soft, familiar scent of polished wood beneath it.

The trees outside have become silhouettes. Their edges pass slowly through the little distance your window allows you to see, then give way to other trees, other quiet stretches of the line. Your lamp is turned towards the table. The corridor curtain barely moves. Somewhere beyond it, Mara is settling a cup on another saucer with the same careful touch.

You let your head rest against the cushion. There is no further postcard to open tonight. The box is closed, the garden has been visited, and the small journeys of bread and books and letters can carry on along the line. Here, there is the blanket, the sleeping cat, and the low voice of rain returning to the glass.

The train continues through the evening, carrying its warm windows between the fields. A lamp shines beside you. The cup rests where you left it, and Pip sleeps on. The wheels turn beneath the quiet room, gently and evenly, while the countryside settles around the passing light. Good night. The rest of the journey can belong to the rain.
