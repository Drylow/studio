There is a little shop on the next postcard, with a bow window and a curtain drawn halfway across it. Behind the glass, a lamp shines on a piece of blue cloth. You cannot see the whole room, only a table, a comfortable chair, and shelves carrying folded fabrics. The picture offers just enough to make the rest easy to imagine.

A name has been written above the door in small, clear letters. Ada does not call herself a tailor, though she can alter a jacket beautifully. She thinks of herself as a mender. People bring her the things they have grown accustomed to: a cardigan, a tablecloth, a cushion softened by years in a favourite chair.

You can picture the sound the door makes when it opens. A little brass bell, low and round in tone, announces a visitor without disturbing the room. There is space to sit while Ada finishes a stitch. Often, someone will stay longer than they intended, enjoying the warmth and the faint fragrance of the cedar blocks among the wool.

On this particular afternoon, a blanket lies across her table. It belongs to the village reading room, where it usually rests over the back of the chair beside the window. It is a generous blanket, made from squares of many fabrics. Some are as pale as cream. Others carry the colours of moss, clay, and the blue edge of distant hills.

Ada has known the blanket for years. She helped make it when the reading room first opened, gathering pieces from sewing baskets all along the village street. Every so often, it returns to her table for a little attention. Today, she is renewing a length of binding and adding a soft patch where a corner has become thin.

There is no need to hurry. The reading room has other blankets, and its chair stands comfortably in the afternoon sun. Ada has chosen this time because the light is good and because she likes to give such work an open stretch of the day. She draws the cloth towards her and smooths it gently with both hands.

The new binding waits in a loose coil beside her scissors. It is a muted gold, not bright enough to call attention to itself, but warm against the softer colours. Ada cut it from a length of cotton she had been saving. Now it seems to have found the right purpose, curving around the blanket like a narrow line of late sunshine.

As she pins the first section, her sleeve brushes a square of pale green linen. She remembers where it came from. The village baker had made curtains for the window above her sink, and there was a generous piece left over. The cloth still carries the tiny slubs in its weave, giving the green a pleasantly uneven surface.

In the baker's kitchen, those curtains move when the back door is open. Ada has sat there with a cup of tea while loaves cooled on a wooden board. The curtain lifts towards the herbs outside, then settles beside the sink. A little piece of that same cloth now rests beneath her hand, becoming part of another comfortable room.

She draws a length of thread from a wooden reel. The reel turns against the table with a soft, dry sound. Ada chooses the length by habit, then threads the needle near the window. For a moment, the thin strand catches the light. After that, it becomes almost invisible against the binding, doing its work quietly among the fibres.

The first stitches are small and even. Her fingers hold the folded edge while the needle passes beneath it, gathering just enough cloth. She has a leather thimble worn smooth at the tip. It feels as familiar as the handle of her teacup, and she no longer notices it except when she takes it off to rest her hand.

Outside, someone is sweeping the pavement beneath an awning. Rain has left the stones dark, with little shining edges where they meet. A delivery bicycle stands beside the bakery, its basket covered by a square of waxed cloth. Beyond it, the street curves towards a small green where the benches face an old spreading tree.

Ada's visitor that afternoon is Ivo, who looks after the reading room. He brings the wool cardigan she finished for him earlier in the week, wearing it rather than carrying it. The new elbow patches are so comfortable that he wants to tell her. He turns his arm slightly to show how neatly the soft brown cloth follows the wool.

She is pleased, and invites him to take the chair near the stove. It is a small stove, used on damp days even when the season is mild. A kettle stands nearby, and the shelf above it carries a line of cups. None matches another exactly, though they all have handles generous enough for cold fingers.

Ivo notices the blanket and comes over to the table. His hand rests on a square of blue cotton with a fine white stripe. That piece came from a tablecloth, he remembers. It used to cover the long table at the village summer lunches, where bowls of strawberries stood among jugs of water and little jars of flowers.

Ada remembers it too. The table sat beneath a canvas awning, and the cloth reached almost to the grass. She can picture a place near the end where she and a friend shelled peas into a bowl. They had meant to help in the kitchen, but it was so pleasant outside that someone brought the basket to them instead.

Ivo runs a finger along the blanket's new edge. He likes the gold, he says. It will look warm against the reading room chair. Then he returns to the stove while Ada sets the needle in its cushion and brings the kettle to the table. The work can rest without losing its place, held neatly by the waiting pins.

They have tea from a brown pot with a lid that fits closely. Ada pours slowly, leaving room in each cup. On a saucer are slices of a plain loaf cake, wrapped until now beneath a cloth. Ivo takes a piece, and the conversation moves to the books he has been arranging on the reading room's lower shelves.

He likes to place a few covers facing outwards. A painting of an orchard, a drawing of a house, a photograph of a quiet shore. People sometimes choose a book simply because its picture reminds them of somewhere they know. Ada says that fabric can work in much the same way, holding a place in a colour or a pattern.

The blanket lies between them while they talk. It does not tell its stories all at once. A person might use it for years without knowing which square was a curtain, or where the striped cloth spent its summers. The stories have become something simpler in the making: warmth across the knees, a soft edge beneath the fingers, colours that sit well together.

When Ivo leaves, he takes an empty tin that belongs at the reading room. Ada has been using it to carry biscuits on her visits. She will bring the blanket herself when it is finished, she tells him. He says there will be tea, as there nearly always is, and the door gives its low, pleasant note behind him.

The light at the window has softened. Ada switches on the table lamp, and the fabric gains a warmer shade. She lifts the binding and finds the place where she paused. Soon the little rhythm of the needle returns. Stitch by stitch, the loose gold curve becomes an edge that can be held and folded and tucked around a seated person.

For the corner patch, she chooses a piece of wool the colour of a quiet lake. It has been washed until it is beautifully soft. She rounds its corners, lays it over the thin place, and turns the blanket a little to see the balance of colours. A green square nearby makes the blue seem deeper, like water beside reeds.

There is a pleasure in choosing without needing to search very far. Ada's shelves already hold what she needs. Linen folded beside cotton, wool tucked into a basket, small pieces arranged in a shallow drawer. She knows where each texture rests, and now and then she takes something out simply to feel it between her fingers before putting it back.

The patch settles into the blanket naturally. Ada follows its curved edge with small stitches, keeping the fabric smooth. She leaves enough ease for the layers to move together. A blanket must bend around elbows and knees, and around the shape of someone leaning towards a book. It should be comfortable even where the mending has been done.

When the sewing is finished, she spreads the blanket across the table once more. Her palm travels over the new corner and along the binding. The gold is quieter than it looked on the reel, softened now by the colours around it. She folds the blanket loosely, with the new blue patch visible on top, and places it in her basket.

The walk to the reading room takes her past the bakery window. Inside, trays have been put away, and a lamp shines on the clean counter. Ada follows the pavement to the green, where the old tree is beginning to gather evening shade. The reading room windows are bright, and one stands slightly open above a box of herbs.

Ivo takes the blanket and lays it over the chair by the window. It looks familiar at once, as though it has only been out for a stroll. Ada sits nearby with her tea. A reader turns a page. In the open window, the herbs move a little, and the blanket waits with its fresh binding folded neatly along the arm.

You can leave it there, ready for an ordinary evening. Someone will draw it across their knees without needing to know the path it took that afternoon. The baker's green curtain, the summer tablecloth, and the quiet blue lake will warm them together. Ada's stitches will hold their places, small and steady beneath the lamplight.

The blanket on your own seat feels pleasant beneath your hand. Beyond the train window, a line of trees moves slowly through the mist, its branches disappearing into the dark. The postcard of Ada's shop rests beside the station and the fern. Underneath them, another card shows a narrow bookshop on a hill, with a light in its upstairs window.
