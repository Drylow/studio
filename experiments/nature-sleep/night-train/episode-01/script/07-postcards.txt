The little post office has a bow window and a door painted the colour of forget me nots. In the postcard, a box of flowers rests beneath the glass. You cannot quite make out the flowers themselves, only their pale heads against the leaves. A wooden bench stands nearby, with enough room for a bag and someone stopping to enjoy the evening.

Mara pauses beside your open door. When she sees the card, she smiles and touches the corner of the box. Iona, she says, began the collection with pictures left over from the post office shelves. She thought they might be pleasant company for passengers. Over time, people added views of their own windows, gardens, and familiar stretches of the line.

You look at the cards already resting on the table. They seem different now that you know how they came together. Someone chose to send the harbour. Someone thought the orchard doorway was worth sharing. The collection has grown through these modest offerings, until a small wooden box can hold an evening's journey through places where a lamp is lit.

The post office you imagine is still warm from the day. Light rests on the honey coloured counter, and a brass bell sits beneath a handwritten sign. Behind the counter, wooden pigeonholes hold packets, envelopes, and rolls of paper tied with cotton string. A vase of flowers stands near the window, close enough to the glass to catch the last soft light.

Iona is sitting at a desk with her sleeves rolled neatly back. She is choosing paper to wrap a parcel, smoothing a sheet against the blotter before folding it around a book. There is plenty of paper to cover the corners. She works slowly, making each fold with the side of her hand, and sets the parcel aside when it is ready.

She recognises the card you are holding and brings out the little album it came from. The cover is green cloth, with a label written in blue ink. Inside are pictures of local places taken in different seasons. The bakery appears with bare branches, then with blossom, then with a basket of apples propped beside its open kitchen door.

You sit on a chair beside her desk while she turns the pages. There are photographs of the greenhouse, its roof shining after rain, and a close view of the station lantern. In one picture, you can see the bookshop window through a reflection of passing clouds. Another shows only a cup on a table, with sunlight falling across the handle.

Iona likes that last picture particularly. The person who sent it wrote that the tea had been very good. That was the whole message, and it seems to have been enough. She has kept the photograph beside a view of the harbour steps, because the blue in the cup reminds her of the water on a clear afternoon.

Her desk holds a tray of fresh postcards. Some have pictures on the front; others are entirely plain, made from thick cream paper. She offers you a blank one and a pencil with a smooth wooden shaft. The card feels substantial between your fingers, its edges cut cleanly and its surface soft enough to take a light mark.

You turn it over, considering what might fit into such a small space. Around you, the post office supplies several possibilities. There is the curved shadow of the vase, the brass rim of the bell, the pattern of the flowers reflected in the window. You could describe any of them, or simply draw the shape of the chair you are sitting on.

Iona places a cup beside you, leaving the handle clear of the card. Her tea has a gentle scent of orange peel. She keeps the peel in a jar above the desk, along with dried mint and a small tin of biscuits. The tin is covered in a picture of a garden whose paths wind around flowers larger than the benches.

She tells you the tin came with a parcel from a friend who thought the painted garden looked like a pleasant place to sit. Now the friend receives postcards describing the real flowers outside the post office. The messages follow the changing window box: small green leaves, the first pale petals, and then the generous fullness of plants that have settled into their pots.

There is something enjoyable about this conversation carried in pieces. Each card can be read while the kettle warms, or placed on a shelf until there is time to answer. Iona shows you a reply tucked into the album. Her friend has drawn a chair beside the painted path, adding a little table with cups waiting on it.

You begin your own card with a drawing of the train window. The pencil makes a quiet brushing sound on the paper. You add a curve for the teapot and a soft shape for Pip on his blanket. The cat comes out rather round, but that seems appropriate. Iona leans over to admire him and says he looks extremely comfortable.

Below the picture, you write about the rain moving down the glass. Then you mention Ruth's plant and the white line across the harbour stone. A short sentence is enough to bring each thing back. The page begins to hold the evening without trying to hold all of it. You leave a clear space beneath the last line.

Iona finds a small envelope to fit the card. Its flap is lined with pale blue paper, the same sort of blue as the painted door. There is no need to choose an address yet. You can keep the card until someone comes to mind, or place it among your own things as a note from this quiet part of the journey.

At the end of the desk is a tray of illustrated stamps. Iona has collected examples showing leaves, birds, pottery, and ordinary country buildings. She turns the tray so you can see them. One contains a tiny orchard, with a basket beneath the trees. You wonder whether its artist knew a place rather like Tomas's kitchen and the sheltered step outside.

You choose a stamp with a lamp on it. The lamp is drawn simply, a curved shade above a pool of warm colour. Iona gives you a little square of paper to rest it on while you decide whether the card will travel. Even an unsent postcard, she says, can keep a stamp nearby for the pleasure of the picture.

Behind her, the wrapped book waits in its place on the shelf. It came from Niall's shop, and it is going to a reader who asked for something about gardens. Iona has slipped a spare postcard inside the wrapping. It shows June's greenhouse in the evening, a view that seems well suited to the book and the person expecting it.

She runs a finger over the parcel's fold, then adds a length of soft string. The bow sits flat enough to be undone without scissors. You watch the care she takes with this small finishing touch. When the parcel is opened, the string can be kept in a drawer, and the paper smoothed for another useful journey.

The post office has a drawer devoted to such things. Iona pulls it open to show you short ribbons, clean paper, and buttons found in parcels that once carried mended clothes. Ada sometimes takes a ribbon for her work, then brings back another colour. The drawer has become a small collection of possibilities, changing quietly from week to week.

Near the door, a canvas mailbag hangs from a wooden peg. Its seams are strong, and the strap has softened where it rests against a shoulder. Later, it will travel on the train, taking these ordinary messages along the line. You picture the bag in a warm luggage space, its envelopes resting together while the wheels turn beneath them.

Iona steps outside to bring in the little cushion from the bench. You follow her as far as the doorway. The flowers in the window box have closed slightly in the evening air, their pale petals gathering around their centres. Down the street, a lamp shines through a green curtain, and the pavement holds a narrow reflection of its light.

She brushes a leaf from the cushion and carries it indoors. It belongs to the chair beside her desk, but on fine days she takes it out to make the bench more welcoming. You can see where the fabric has faded a little in the middle. It is the same comfortable sort of wear as the striped cushion in Ruth's harbour room.

Back at the desk, you slide your card into the blue lined envelope without closing the flap. The little lamp stamp rests beside it. Iona brings a plain paper band to hold them together, and you tuck the band around the envelope. The whole thing fits neatly into your coat pocket, ready to accompany you wherever the evening leads.

Before you leave the imagined post office, Iona adds a final card to the album. It shows the station garden after rain, with a lantern above a path of flat stones. There is no message on the back. She thinks it would make a good addition to Mara's box, a place for the journey to rest when the other pictures have been enjoyed.

You carry that garden with you as the post office grows still. The parcel waits on its shelf. The cushion is back on its chair. Iona puts the lid on the biscuit tin and rests a hand on the album before closing it. Through the bow window, her flowers hold the remaining light, each pale head turned slightly towards the glass.

The rhythm beneath your seat brings you fully back to the compartment. Your own hands are empty except for the postcard, and the envelope you imagined belongs to the story. Yet you can remember the feel of its paper as clearly as the velvet beneath your fingertips. The train has given these small places room to unfold, then gathered them gently again.

You arrange the cards in the box, without needing a particular order. The harbour can sit beside the greenhouse. The bakery can keep the bookshop company. Their borders overlap like the edges of familiar conversations, each picture lending a little colour to the next. On top, you find the station garden, waiting beneath the lamplight just as Iona described it.

Mara returns with the quiet step of someone who knows the carriage well. She leaves fresh water beside the teapot, glances fondly at Pip, and draws the corridor curtain a little further across. You keep the garden card on the table. Its small lantern shines in the picture, while the real lamp beside you warms the smooth grain of the wood.
