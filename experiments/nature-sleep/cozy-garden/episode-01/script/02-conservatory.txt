A fern leans over the conservatory path, its long fronds making a delicate pattern against the worn red tiles. Beyond it, the glass roof holds the soft reflection of a lamp. Rain slides down the panes. From the pergola, you can see June turning a pot on her workbench, a small, unhurried movement inside that inviting room.

Mara mentions that June has been choosing cuttings for the shelves beside the reading room window. June sends plants into every corner of this little garden. Beneath the pergola, beside the pond, and in the sheltered space behind the glass, something has usually begun its life on her potting bench. She knows each plant by its shape and texture.

You can follow the short path to the conservatory without leaving the garden's circle of light. Inside, the rain has a slightly different sound. Water falling on glass. A warm room within a cooler evening. The gentle nearness of leaves, making their own green enclosure around an ordinary wooden chair. There is a place beside it for your cup.

June brings her shallow tray closer to the lamp. Her apron is the colour of washed clay, and there is a crescent of soil beneath one fingernail. Earlier, she went among the garden pots choosing cuttings. Now they lie together on a damp cloth, their small stems pointing in different directions like the spokes of a wheel.

She sets the tray on the bench beneath the windows. The bench has been scrubbed so often that its grain feels almost silky. At one end, empty terracotta pots are stacked by size. At the other, a green watering can catches a narrow band of light. A pencil rests across a saucer filled with small, plain wooden labels.

June begins with the scented geranium. She lifts a stem, turns it slowly between her fingers, and chooses a pot. The plant has soft leaves with rounded edges, and when she brushes one, a clean, lemony fragrance reaches the air. She takes a little pleasure in that fragrance every time. Familiarity has made it more welcome, rather than less noticeable.

Rain moves steadily over the roof. It makes different notes on the sloping panes and on the narrow strip of zinc above the door. June knows those notes without having named them. In the corner where she keeps the ferns, the drops sound farther away. Beside the potting bench, they are close and clear, like water falling into a deep basin.

A broad leaf near the window holds a bead of water at its tip. It swells, hangs for a moment, then drops into the soil below. Another begins to gather in the same place. If you chose to sit in June's chair, you could watch this small process for a long time, with nothing more required of the evening.

The chair has a cushion covered in faded yellow cloth. June's sister made it from the sound pieces of an old curtain, keeping the little border of embroidered flowers. On the low table beside it, a bowl holds fallen petals. June changes the water each morning, and the petals float lightly, their reflections colouring the shallow white porcelain.

Rowan steps in from the garden with a covered dish beneath his arm. He pauses under the porch and wipes his shoes carefully on the mat. He has brought back a dish that held plums, now filled with small oat biscuits. June takes it with both hands, pleased by the easy circulation of dishes between the garden's little rooms.

He is making room for a plant beside the reading room window. There is a shelf, he explains, with light from the garden in the morning. June asks about the width of the shelf and whether he likes a plant that trails. They talk in the unhurried way of people describing a place they know by its daily light.

She shows him a pot of trailing leaves, then the geranium with its soft lemon scent. Rowan touches a leaf gently and smiles. The geranium seems right. There will be space for it beside the bowl where Mara keeps pears, and when the window opens, the garden air will pass through its leaves and into the room.

While June works, Rowan sits in the yellow chair. He watches her make a little hollow in the soil and settle the cutting into it. Her hands move without haste. She presses just enough earth around the stem to support it, leaving the surface loose and even. Then she turns the pot, noticing its shape from another side.

Some things become restful through the care someone gives them. The folding of a cloth. A brush being rinsed until the water runs clear. Here, it is June's way of touching the soil and then brushing the bench with the side of her hand. Each movement finishes before the next begins, without being measured or made into a lesson.

Beyond the glass, the garden is softened by rain. The narrow path curves between pots of lavender and low silver leaves. Farther back, reeds lean over the pond, where a lamp lays a warm reflection on the water. You can make out the pergola roof, but the ivy on the enclosing wall gathers into a mild grey green.

June places the new pot on a saucer and brings it to Rowan. The cutting is small, with room to become itself. She tells him that the shelf he described will suit it beautifully. Then she sets the kettle on a little hotplate near the door. There is a tin of tea beneath the bench, tucked between the clean cups.

The conservatory does not grow quieter while the water heats. It simply lets the new sound join the others. Rain on the roof, a leaf brushing glass, the soft beginning of the kettle. Rowan turns the pot in his hands. June takes the oat biscuits from their dish and arranges them loosely on a plate with a blue rim.

They drink their tea near the ferns. The leaves soften the lamplight, and the steam from the cups rises into air that already feels gently alive with water. Rowan tells June about a bird bathing beside the pond earlier. She describes the blackbird that comes to the same patch of soil by the garden wall each morning.

Their conversation moves as slowly as the weather. It touches the herbs Mara is drying, a path being edged with stones, the way the pond looks beneath its overhanging leaves. Nothing needs a firm conclusion. A thought can rest where it is, and a little silence can arrive between them without anyone feeling the need to fill it.

June has a habit of keeping old plant pots even when she no longer uses them for planting. A shallow one holds string. A taller one holds brushes, their wooden handles leaning against the rim. Rowan notices a tiny pot filled with sea glass on the windowsill. The pieces are pale, frosted colours, green and blue and almost white.

June takes down the tiny pot so he can see the pieces more clearly. They feel smooth against her fingers, their edges softened by water. She arranges them on a cloth beside the cups. The blue seems deeper against the linen, while the nearly white pieces catch the lamplight. Each has a different shape, though they sit comfortably together.

After tea, Rowan carries the geranium towards the door. June wraps the pot in a sheet of newspaper, folding it loosely so that the leaves remain free. He puts it in a broad basket, protecting it from the light rain on the short path to the reading room. The oat biscuit dish stays behind, ready for another pleasant visit.

June watches him cross the garden. There is something especially pleasant about sending a plant towards a window she can see from her own. She pictures it there already, its leaves turned towards the pond, with the bowl of pears nearby. In time, there may be more cuttings, bringing that familiar scent to another sheltered corner among the rooms.

The rest of the tray waits on the bench. June returns to it with her sleeves a little farther up her arms. She chooses a mint stem next, then a sprig with small, silver leaves. Each receives a pot, a label, and a place near the glass. The row becomes a quiet record of an evening spent among growing things.

She washes her hands in the little basin beside the door. The water turns briefly cloudy, then clears. A bar of pale soap rests on a ridged dish, and the towel smells faintly of the line in the garden. June dries between her fingers, folds the towel over its rail, and comes back to the yellow chair.

The rain is easing now. Larger drops fall from the edge of the roof, leaving longer spaces between their sounds. Through the clearing glass, the pond becomes distinct again. A reed moves, and the sheltered chair beyond it catches a little lamplight. June considers taking her cup to the pergola, then decides she is comfortable exactly where she is.

She opens a notebook, though she does not write immediately. Between its pages lies a pressed leaf, as thin and delicate as tissue. It has kept the shape of its veins and the warm colour of the season when she found it. She lays it on the table for a moment, beside the bowl of floating petals.

Perhaps the conservatory keeps the rain in this way. Not by holding it behind the glass, but in the shine of leaves, the darkened terracotta, and the stillness that follows a shower. Later, when the paths are dry, something of this evening will remain. Fresh soil, a full watering can, and a geranium in the reading room window.

June closes the notebook without adding a word. She can remember the new cuttings tomorrow. For now, the chair is warm, the cup is comfortable in her hands, and the garden is coming gently into focus beyond the window. She rests beneath the glass roof while the last few drops find their way down from the leaves.

From the pergola, the conservatory glows quietly on the left of the garden, its fern making a lacy shape behind the glass. Pip stretches a paw across the blanket, then settles back into the warmth. Mara leaves a folded cloth beside your cup. Beyond the plants, another window holds a pool of light where Ada has spread out her sewing.
