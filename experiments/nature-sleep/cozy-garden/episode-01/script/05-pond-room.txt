Beyond the reading room, a narrow path bends back towards the pond. The stones are dark with fine rain, and each holds a faint reflection of the lantern above it. You can see the pergola through the leaves. Pip is still asleep on its bench, while Mara folds a cloth beside the teapot, her movements small and familiar beneath the shelter.

On the other side of the water is an alcove with a roof of mellow tiles. Its wooden doorframe has been painted a soft green, and a lavender pot stands beside the opening. Inside, a table faces the pond. A chair has been placed where someone sitting down can see both the water and the warm windows of the greenhouse.

Mara notices you looking towards the alcove. Ruth is there, she says, writing in her garden notebook. There will be room beside her table if you would like to sit. Then Mara continues folding the cloth, leaving you to follow the short path around the pond, where the low branches of a little willow hang close to the surface.

The garden has the fresh scent that comes after rain has touched warm stone. Along the water's edge, leaves hold drops in their shallow folds. You pass a clump of mint growing in a pot, then a fern whose newest frond is still loosely curled. The alcove is only a few steps away, with lamplight resting on its wooden threshold.

Ruth's lavender brushes against the hem of your coat as you reach the opening. There is a peg for anything you would like to hang up, and a narrow shelf for setting down a book. Beneath it stands a tray where wet shoes can leave their small puddles. Someone has placed a smooth stone beside the tray to keep it steady.

Ruth sits at the table with an open notebook and a pencil tucked behind her ear. A length of green wool lies beside her teapot. She has been measuring the stems of a plant that leans towards the opening. As you arrive, she sets the pot down and moves her chair slightly, making a comfortable space at the table.

She offers you the chair with the cushion sewn from an old striped curtain. Before sitting, you run your hand over the fabric. It has that comfortable softness which comes from years of being washed and dried in the open air. Ruth turns the teapot slightly, bringing its handle within easy reach, and asks whether you would like the blue cup.

You would. The cup has a broad handle and a small irregularity in its rim, a gentle rise made by the potter's thumb. There is no matching saucer. Instead, Ruth sets it on a glazed tile, blue around the edges and cream in the middle. Steam travels over the tile, then disappears into the mild air beneath the roof.

Her notebook remains open between you. Along one side are observations about the pond, written in tidy columns. On the other are notes which have gradually spread into the margins. The parsley needed turning. The lavender liked its new pot. Someone returned a borrowed dish with a pear resting in it. Ruth writes down things she enjoys noticing again.

She does not read every entry aloud. She simply moves the notebook so that you can see a drawing of the pond's stone rim. A little mark shows where the water reaches after gentle rain. Beside it is a sketch of a flowerpot, and beneath that, a reminder to ask June about a plant with softly silver leaves.

You remember the greenhouse nearby, its panes warmed from within. It is easy to picture June choosing a cutting and wrapping the roots in damp cloth. Perhaps she brought it over in the same basket she uses for gathering leaves. From where you sit, you can see her potting bench through the glass, with a small row of pots beneath it.

Ruth lifts the plant to show you its leaves. They are narrow and softly furred, with tiny beads of water caught along their edges. She has given the pot a quarter turn so it will grow evenly. A wooden label rests in the soil. The writing has faded, but June's rounded letters are still visible just above the rim.

Beside the plant is a shallow bowl of smooth stones. They range from pale oat grey to the dark brown of a well used teapot. Ruth selects one and places it in your palm. It is pleasantly cool. A thin white line crosses its surface, bending in a shape that resembles the little path around the edge of the pond.

You turn the stone over with your thumb while Ruth pours more tea. Outside, the water touches the lowest part of the surrounding ledge. Small circles open where rain falls between the leaves, crossing one another without disturbing the larger reflection. A wren rests in the willow, its feathers softly gathered. The whole view seems to have found a resting place.

On the shelf, an old clock moves through its quiet work. Its ticking is softer than the sound of Ruth setting down the teapot. The alcove has gathered many such modest sounds: the shift of a chair cushion, the light touch of a spoon against pottery, a cupboard door closing against a folded piece of felt beneath its wooden edge.

Ruth tells you that Ada made the felt pad from an offcut. It is tucked into the corner where the cupboard used to tap against its frame. There are several small improvements like this around the table. A loop of ribbon helps open a drawer. A wooden bead makes the blind cord easier to find when the light grows dim.

She stands to bring a plate from the cupboard. On it are slices of bread, a small dish of butter, and a spoonful of something golden. The bread came from the garden kitchen. The golden preserve is made from pears, cooked slowly until the pieces hold their shape inside the syrup. Ruth spreads a little on the heel of a loaf.

You take a slice and let the warmth of the tea return to your hands. There is a faint taste of lemon in the preserve, and the bread has a soft centre beneath its floury crust. On the shelf are more jars, each labelled with a fruit and a small drawing of the tree or bush it came from.

When you ask about the drawings, Ruth looks pleased. They began with a label that had grown damp and difficult to read. A sketch of a pear proved useful, and then she found she enjoyed making the leaves. Now the jars carry whole little branches. One label even includes the corner of the fence where the garden's raspberries grow.

She brings the pencil back to her notebook. Under the entry about the plant, she draws the stone you have been holding. You place it on the table so she can see its white line. Her drawing is simple and slightly larger than the real thing. It catches the curve of the stone without needing to describe every mark.

The rain settles into a fine brushing sound against the tiled roof. Ruth watches the pond for a while before reaching for the wool beside her teapot. She is making a loose tie for the tall plant, giving its new stem something gentle to lean against. On the table, a slender wooden support waits beside the open page of her book.

You hold the little loop while she measures it. The wool is warm green, close to the colour of the leaves beside the pond. It slides easily through your fingers. Ruth leaves room around the stem, tests the tie with a light touch, and turns the pot so that its fullest leaves face the chairs instead of the wall.

The small task changes the shelf beside you. There is more space between the plants now, and the teapot can sit near the opening without its spout brushing a leaf. Ruth seems satisfied by this. She wipes a ring of water from the painted wood and folds the cloth neatly over the edge of a bowl, ready for another time.

Beyond the pond, Mara pauses beside a rosemary bush and takes a little sprig from its lowest branch. She brings it over and leaves it in an empty jug by the alcove. Ruth thanks her. Later, she says, the rosemary will sit beside the kettle in the garden kitchen, where the warmth will bring out its scent when the water is heated.

You find yourself looking once more at the garden notebook. Its pages hold practical things, but they also hold a particular kind of attention. Someone remembered how the parsley grew. Someone noticed the white line in a stone. There is room beside the columns for an ordinary evening, and for the company of a person who stopped to share it.

Ruth closes the book with the pencil marking her place. She slides the bowl of stones back onto the shelf and places the one you held at the front. Its pale stripe catches the lamplight. Across the pond, the greenhouse windows show through the willow, their warm reflections resting among the small circles made by the rain against the water.

The lamp beside Ruth has a green base and a milk glass shade, with tiny leaves painted around its rim. Elias brought it here when she was arranging the alcove. She has found exactly the right place for it beside her notebook. In its light, the painted leaves seem to belong with the real ones, giving the table a garden of its own.

You sit a little longer while Ruth places the rosemary in fresh water. The plant rests against its woollen support, and the notebook waits beneath the lamp. All these things can continue without being watched. The rain makes its soft pattern across the pond, and the leaves of the little willow move just enough to loosen the drops along their edges.

When you stand, Ruth leaves the chair where it is, its cushion still holding the warmth of your visit. You follow the stones towards the pergola. Pip has stretched a paw onto the blanket, his toes loosely gathered, while the rest of him remains tucked into the generous curve of his sleep. Mara has placed your cup beside the teapot.

You take a comfortable mouthful before looking towards the garden kitchen. Its window shines beyond the end of the path, and the scent of bread reaches you through the fresh air. Nearby, a few small fruit trees gather the rain among their leaves. The kitchen door is open beneath its shelter, with a light resting on the step and a chair just inside.
