The first light appears among the leaves beside the pergola. It has the colour of honey left in a glass jar, warm and clear without being bright. Beyond it, a narrow path curves between low plants towards a little alcove near the pond. Rain touches the garden gently, making the last of the evening sky seem soft and close.

Mara sets a small wooden box on the table beside your cup. Inside are the things she keeps for the garden lamps: clean cloths, a soft brush, and spare shades wrapped in tissue. There is nothing hurried about the preparations. She has been looking forward to this quiet part of the evening, when every corner begins to gather its own light.

From your place on the brown wooden bench, you can see almost the whole garden. The glowing conservatory stands to the left, its panes misted by rain. To the right, the pond holds a blurred reflection of the leaves above it. Behind the plants is a little reading room, and between these places, paths fit themselves into the space like gentle seams.

Elias is standing beside the pergola with a folded cloth over his arm. His waistcoat has roomy pockets, and his shirt sleeves are turned back neatly at the wrist. Every evening that he visits, he helps Mara tend the lamps. He has done it often enough that the short path between them feels like a familiar tune.

There is no grand ceremony to it. He opens each little door, wipes the glass, and checks the latch on its dark metal frame. The lamps are electric, though Mara sometimes calls him the lamplighter. Elias likes the old name. It gives a certain dignity to the pleasant business of making a place welcoming after sunset.

Beside the pergola, the first lamp hangs near a barrel planted with rosemary. When Elias leans close to the glass, his sleeve brushes the leaves. Their clean scent rises into the evening. He pauses to turn the barrel a little, giving the fuller side of the plant room beside the wall, then smooths a patch of soil with his fingers.

Near the conservatory, June is coiling a hose in broad, even loops. Earlier, she watered the pots beneath the eaves, where the rain seldom reaches. Cream roses climb a low trellis above them. Their petals are open but unhurried, and a few have settled on the gravel. Elias lifts one from the path and places it beneath the bush.

June asks whether he would like to help guide a new stem along the trellis in the spring. He says that he would, and they consider the sunny patch beside the conservatory door. It is a small conversation, the sort that can continue next week without either person forgetting where it ended. For now, the rose is growing comfortably where it is.

You might notice the garden sounds around them. A brush passes over paving stones beneath the shelter. A cup rests in its saucer inside the reading room. Water slips from a leaf into the pond, then the soft rain resumes its place. None of these sounds takes the whole evening. Each has its little space among the others.

The alcove by the pond is dry beneath its tiled roof. Its walls are the colour of oatmeal, and a wooden rail runs beneath a shelf of smooth stones. There is a chair with a blue cushion and a narrow window looking into the garden. Elias opens the window slightly, allowing the fragrance of wet leaves to enter the room.

On the broad windowsill stands a small table lamp with a milk glass shade. Its base is green, a softer green than the moss beneath the garden wall, and its cord is wound loosely beside it. The lamp belongs to Ruth, who spends many pleasant evenings here. Today, she has brought it for the table beside the comfortable chair.

Until now, Ruth has kept the lamp in the little room behind the conservatory. She has been arranging a corner there for writing, but she thinks its gentle light would suit this alcove even better. She mentioned this to Elias earlier, showing him the curved shade and the tiny leaves painted around its rim. He agreed that it would feel at home.

He remembers the leaves now as he folds his polishing cloth. They are not quite alike. One turns outward, another bends towards the stem, and a third has a rounded tip. A person painted them patiently, leaving these small differences behind. Elias likes objects that still carry the movement of someone's hand, even after many years of ordinary use.

Mara brings a clean cloth and spreads it across the little side table. She smooths the corners, leaving a pleasing border of dark wood around it. Then she adds a small dish and a little paper bag of biscuits. Ruth always likes something plain and buttery with her evening tea, especially while she watches rain gathering on the pond.

Outside, the garden lamps begin to glow. Their light is pale at first, hardly brighter than the sky, but it gathers warmth as the evening deepens. Elias stands beneath the alcove roof and looks along the path. Each pool of light touches the plants differently, leaving a gentle pattern of brightness and shade between the pond and your bench.

For a moment, the rain becomes fine enough to see the surface of the water clearly. The reflections stretch and soften around the circles made by falling drops. Elias turns one lamp slightly towards the path, leaving the reeds in their natural evening shade. Mara watches from the pergola, satisfied with how quietly the light sits among the plants.

Ruth arrives from the conservatory carrying a bunch of sweet peas wrapped in damp paper. She has been sitting with June beside the potting bench. There was tea, she tells Elias, and a plate of sliced pears. They spent most of the visit choosing places for new cuttings, though they also watched a small bird bathing beneath the ivy.

She places the sweet peas in a jar on the alcove shelf. The pale flowers lean together, pink and cream and lavender. Their stems show clearly through the water. Elias turns the jar slightly so that a long tendril can curl into the open space beside it. Ruth says that is exactly the right place for them.

They sit for a while beneath the alcove roof. The biscuits are divided without fuss, and Mara pours tea into the thick cups she keeps for garden evenings. The sound of rain moves softly between the leaves. June carries her empty tray back into the conservatory, pausing to enjoy the little circle of light that now reaches across the path.

Ruth describes the books she would like to keep on the alcove shelf. A few essays, perhaps, and the notebook where she sketches plants beside the water. From the chair, she can see the leaves of the reeds brushing together. Later, the window will reflect the lamp, making a small, warm room within the glass while the garden grows darker beyond.

Elias can picture it easily. He has sat in that chair on rainy afternoons, with the pond making its quiet movements outside. There is a smooth stone on the windowsill, just large enough to rest comfortably in a palm. He turns it gently, noticing a pale line through its surface, then places it back where Ruth has left it.

When they have finished their tea, he carries the lamp from the windowsill to the little table. Its green base settles neatly on the cloth, and Ruth arranges the shade. Mara places the remaining biscuits in the dish. The chair, the flowers, and the warm light seem to belong together already, as though the room had simply been waiting for this arrangement.

Elias steps back beneath the roof to see how it looks from the path. The lamp shines through its pale glass without reaching too far into the garden. A person walking past will notice the comfortable chair and perhaps think of sitting down. Ruth folds the empty wrapping paper into a tidy square and sets it in Mara's box.

For a moment, you can imagine taking that little path yourself. Past the rosemary, along the low plants, then into the dry shelter beside the pond. The stones are smooth beneath your feet, and the air holds the fragrance of rain. Inside the alcove, the chair faces the window, with room on its broad arms for a book or your hands.

The green lamp sits neatly between the dish of biscuits and Ruth's smooth stone. Its painted leaves have become more distinct in the light. There they are, each slightly different, making their modest circle around a glow that has found another pleasant place to shine. The milk glass softens everything nearby, even the folds of the cloth beneath it.

Ruth takes off her cardigan and hangs it over the chair. There will be time to open a book, and time to add something to her notebook. First, she draws the small curtain partway across the window, leaving a view of the pond. The reeds have become a soft shape beyond the glass, too delicate now to separate into individual leaves.

Elias has nearly finished his round. He returns the clean cloth to Mara's box and carries the empty cups beneath the pergola. The sweet peas remain on the alcove shelf. Tomorrow, someone may pause beside them while sheltering from a shower, perhaps leaning closer to the flowers without quite noticing that they have done so. The room will be ready.

He sits for a while on the end of the garden bench. The wood feels smooth beneath his hands, and the roof gathers the gentle sound of rain above him. Nearby, a lamp shines through glass he has just cleaned. The rosemary makes its quiet contribution to the air as he looks across the small, warmly lit garden.

You do not need to take in every detail to understand this place. A chair made ready. A cup offered. Leaves shining under the rain, and a light beside the pond. The garden seems to draw its corners a little closer as evening settles around it. Everything you might wish to visit is only a few unhurried steps away.

Mara closes the wooden box and leaves it beneath the table. Pip has tucked his nose a little closer to his paws on the folded blanket. You lift the cup in both hands, enjoying its warmth. Through the misted glass to your left, June moves among the conservatory plants, her lamp shining over the broad leaves and the quiet potting bench.
