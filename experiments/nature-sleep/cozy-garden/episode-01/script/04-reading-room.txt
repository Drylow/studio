The green book in Niall's hands has a plain cloth cover, with a little drawing of a leaf on its spine. He turns it towards the lamp, noticing how the fabric catches the light. Around him, the reading room has settled into evening. Ada's sewing is folded away, and rain traces clear, narrow lines down the window beside the shelves.

This little room stands just behind the conservatory, with its door opening directly into the garden. People come here for books, for tea, or simply for a comfortable place to sit. They like its warm window and the view of the pond between the leaves. Mara keeps the chairs arranged so everyone can find a quiet corner without being far apart.

From beneath the pergola, you can follow a line of small lights towards the open door. The path is short, with a gentle bend around a pot of lavender. You can imagine stepping inside, leaving the soft patter of the garden behind and finding the warmer sound of footsteps on an old wooden floor, with room to pause among the books.

Niall is at a table near the back, sorting a small collection of books. He has laid a square of felt beneath them to protect their covers. Some are clothbound, their colours mellowed by sunlight. Others have paper jackets with painted gardens, coastlines, or little houses among trees. He lifts each book gently before deciding where it belongs.

He does not arrange everything by subject. There are shelves where he keeps a more personal order: books for rainy evenings, books to take to a garden seat, books that feel companionable when read a few pages at a time. Visitors have grown familiar with these quiet distinctions. They often begin by telling him where they would like to sit.

Someone might read beneath the pergola while Mara prepares tea. Ivo prefers the chair near the stove, where he can stretch his legs a little. June sometimes carries a book to her seat among the ferns. Niall listens to these preferences carefully. The place can be as useful as the kind of book a person happens to mention.

Now, he is making a little display beside the window. A low stand holds a book about gardens, open at a drawing of a pear tree. Beside it, he places an essay collection with a green cover and a small volume of poems. A smooth pebble keeps a handwritten card from curling in the warm air above the radiator.

The card simply says that these are books to linger with. Niall wrote the words slowly, leaving generous space around them. He likes a window that does not demand too much attention. Someone crossing the garden can glance at it, understand its invitation, and carry that pleasant thought on to the conservatory or the sheltered chair beside the pond.

There is a scent of paper in the room, mingled with the polish Mara uses on the tables. Near the door, a mat has taken on the fresh smell of rain. The shelves leave comfortable spaces, with enough room to turn and browse. Small lamps sit where their light can fall on the pages rather than into anyone's eyes.

You might choose the chair beside the back window. Its arms are broad, and its cushion has settled into a welcoming shape. From there, you could see the pond through a space in the leaves. Small circles spread over its surface. Above them, the alcove lamp holds a warm reflection, bending a little whenever the water moves.

Niall keeps a stack of plain paper on his table. When he finds a passage he particularly likes, he makes a note for himself, sometimes no more than a word or the name of a tree. The notes are not a catalogue. They are a way of letting a small pleasure stay near him after he has closed the book.

On other sheets, he writes his own thoughts. This evening, he takes up a pencil and leaves a short sentence in the middle of a page. Leave a little room in the day for something you enjoy. He looks at it for a moment, then sets the page beside the window display, where it can accompany the books.

He is thinking of very modest pleasures. Cutting a pear into a bowl. Turning a pot so its leaves receive the light. Sitting beside an open window long enough to notice the scent of rain. There is room for any of these in the sentence, and plenty of room for a reader to imagine something entirely different.

Nora steps in from beneath the pergola, carrying the cushion she has been sitting on. She places it in a chair near the table and asks about the notebooks Mara keeps for visitors. Niall brings a few from a drawer, setting them out so that Nora can feel the covers and turn the pages beneath the comfortable light.

Nora chooses a small one bound in grey linen. The paper is cream, with a faint texture that pleases her fingertips. She has begun keeping notes about this garden, she explains. Not a strict record, just things she notices. Which patch of wall receives the last sun, or how the ferns look beside the conservatory lamp when evening arrives.

There are drawings too, though she says they are very simple. A seed head, a row of pots, the shape a leaf makes when it curls. Niall tells her that simple drawings are often the ones he likes best. They leave a little space for the person looking, much as a few words can suggest a whole familiar afternoon.

Niall opens the drawer again to find a pencil that suits the paper. He lays it on the notebook, then chooses a length of cotton ribbon to mark the pages. It is pale blue, almost the colour of the rain outside. Nora runs the ribbon through her fingers and sets it between the cover and the first fresh sheet.

While he returns the other notebooks, she reads the page beside the window. She smiles at the sentence and says that her notebook is exactly that sort of room. A little place in the day for looking at things. Niall asks whether she would like the page as well. She folds it carefully and places it inside the linen cover.

They stand by the door for a moment, watching the rain fall beyond the shelter. June has left a small tray here with spare leaves from her cuttings, their shapes arranged on a damp cloth. Nora chooses a round geranium leaf to draw. She carries it with her notebook along the short path to the dry bench beneath the pergola.

Mara places a fresh cup of tea near Nora's elbow. The notebook rests beside it, open to a clean page, while the leaf lies on the pale cloth covering the table. Nora smooths Niall's folded note with her palm before setting it aside. There is plenty of light from the lantern, and the sound of rain seems comfortable around her.

Her first drawing is of the geranium leaf. She does not try to capture every curve. A few lines are enough to recall its rounded shape, the soft stem, and the way the veins spread towards the edge. Beneath it, she writes that the leaf has a clean, lemony scent and looks especially lovely beside a warm lantern.

Then she closes the notebook and rests her hands around the cup. The rain has softened to a fine shower, barely reaching beneath the pergola roof. Across the path, a row of pots shines in the mild light. The tea is warm, the bench is comfortable, and the small new book lies within reach, with many pages for other evenings.

In the reading room, Niall returns to the table. The collection is almost sorted. He places the last clothbound volume on a shelf near the back window, where someone will be able to reach it without standing. He straightens the felt beneath his remaining papers, then brushes a little dust from the table with a broad, soft brush.

The room is quiet enough now for reading. Niall takes the green essay collection from the window and opens it where a ribbon marks his place. He reads a page about a kitchen garden, then another about a walk beside water. The sentences are pleasant company. From time to time, he looks up at the changing light beyond the glass.

You do not need to know the words of the book to imagine the scene. The lamp beside him. The page held loosely in one hand. A little rain tracing the window while the garden outside grows less distinct. There is a comfortable companionship between a quiet room and someone reading in it, with each leaving the other space.

When he has finished the short passage, Niall lets the book rest open on his knee. Through the window, he notices thyme growing between paving stones and a shallow bowl collecting rainwater beneath the fern. These are things he has often seen, yet the lamplight gives them a slightly different look. He enjoys them quietly from his comfortable chair.

Later, he puts the book back in the window display. He replaces the page he gave Nora with another sheet, bearing the same simple thought in his own handwriting. Then he places the smooth pebble beside it and adjusts the little lamp. The light catches the grain of the paper, making the space around the words feel warm and generous.

The room settles around its shelves. Niall leaves the window display softly lit, so its warm square can be seen from the pergola. Ada is resting nearby, and Ivo has set his book on his knee. For a while, the gentle turn of a page is the only sound within the room, beneath the patient rain falling outside.

You can see that warm square from your place on the bench. The path leads towards it, then curves among the plants to June's conservatory and the alcove near the pond. These little rooms hold their lights close, sharing them with the garden. The blanket rests across your knees, and the evening continues without any need to move.

Mara has quietly refreshed your tea. A trace of steam rises from the cup while Pip sleeps beside you, his paws gathered on the folded blanket. Across the pond, Ruth's green lamp glows beneath its milk glass shade. She settles into the alcove chair with her notebook, and you can imagine joining her there as another gentle story begins.
