The garden shed has a broad window and a door painted the colour of forget me nots. A box of flowers rests beneath the glass, its pale heads gathered among generous leaves. Beside the door, a wooden bench stands under the overhanging roof. A wicker basket has been set at one end, leaving a comfortable place to sit out of the rain.

Above the window is a small wooden sign. Its letters name the room as the seed library. Through the open doorway, you can see shelves divided into little compartments, each holding folded packets and labelled boxes. A lamp stands on the worktable. In its light, someone is arranging cream coloured cards beside a bowl of dried seed heads.

Iona looks up as you reach the doorway. She has been sorting the seeds gathered earlier in the season, choosing a place for each envelope where it will be easy to find. She draws a chair towards the table and brushes a few papery husks into a shallow dish. The room smells of clean wood, dried leaves, and rain on the threshold.

The library is small enough for everything to be close at hand. Wooden pigeonholes hold packets, notebooks, and rolls of paper tied with cotton string. A vase of flowers stands near the window, catching the soft lamplight. Beneath the table, an empty basket waits beside a stack of trays, their edges worn smooth by many careful afternoons in the garden.

Iona is folding an envelope from a sheet of brown paper. She smooths it against the table before turning the corners inward, making each crease with the side of her hand. Soon there is a little pocket, open at the top. She places it beside the bowl of seed heads and reaches for a pencil with a pleasantly broad grip.

She brings out the album that helps her arrange the shelves. Its cover is green cloth, with a label written in blue ink. Inside are pictures of plants growing in this garden at different times of year. The apple tree appears with bare branches, then with blossom, then with a basket of fruit resting in the grass beneath it.

You sit beside her while she turns the pages. There are photographs of the greenhouse, its roof shining after rain, and a close view of a lantern beside the pond. In one picture, the reading room window holds a reflection of passing clouds. Another shows only a cup on a table, with sunlight falling across its gently curved handle.

Iona likes that last picture particularly. The visitor who gave it to her wrote that the tea had been very good. That was the whole message, and it seems to have been enough. She keeps the photograph beside a view of the pond, because the blue in the cup reminds her of the water on a clear afternoon.

Her table holds a tray of botanical cards. Some carry printed flowers; others are plain, made from thick cream paper. Iona offers you a blank one and a pencil with a smooth wooden shaft. The card feels substantial between your fingers, its edges cut cleanly and its surface soft enough to take even the lightest mark from the pencil.

Visitors sometimes leave a drawing or a few words about something they noticed in the garden. Iona keeps these cards among the plants they describe. A picture of lavender rests beside the lavender seeds. A note about sitting under the pear tree accompanies a photograph of its leaves. Over time, the shelves have gathered many small ways of looking at the same place.

You turn the card over, considering what might fit into such a space. Around you, the room supplies several possibilities. There is the curved shadow of the vase, the pattern of the flowers reflected in the window, and the little bowl of seed heads. You could describe any of them, or draw the shape of the chair you are sitting on.

Iona places a cup beside you, leaving its handle clear of the card. Her tea has a gentle scent of orange peel. She keeps the peel in a jar above the table, along with dried mint and a tin of biscuits. The tin is covered in a painted garden whose paths wind around flowers taller than the little benches beneath them.

The tin came from a friend who visits to help with the flower beds. They both liked the painted garden, particularly a blue chair tucked beneath an enormous rose. Iona has placed the tin where they can see it while they work. Sometimes the biscuits inside are ones Tomas made in the kitchen; sometimes someone brings a different sort to share.

You begin your card with a drawing of the pond. The pencil makes a quiet brushing sound on the paper. You add a curve for the willow and a soft shape for Pip beneath the pergola. The cat comes out rather round, but that seems appropriate. Iona leans over to admire him and says he looks extremely comfortable in his corner.

Below the picture, you write about the rain moving across the water. Then you mention Ruth's plant and the white line in the smooth stone she showed you. A short sentence is enough to bring each thing back. The card begins to hold the evening without trying to hold all of it. You leave a clear space beneath the last line.

Iona finds a little wooden stand for your drawing. It is made from a branch with a narrow groove across the top. The card rests securely in that groove, leaning slightly towards the light. She sets it beside the album, where anyone sitting here can see the pond through your eyes before looking out at the real water beyond the door.

At the end of the table is a tray of seed packets. Their labels show leaves, flowers, and the shapes of the seeds themselves. Iona turns the tray so you can see it clearly. One packet carries a fine drawing of a poppy head, round beneath its little cap. Another shows a cluster of pale flowers among soft green leaves.

She opens an empty packet to show how it is folded. The corners tuck neatly into one another, making a small pocket that holds its shape. June taught her the fold during a rainy afternoon in the greenhouse. Since then, Iona has made packets from spare paper in several colours, choosing pale sheets so the writing will be easy to read.

Beside the packets lies a book from Niall's reading room. He found a chapter about saving garden seeds and left it here with a leaf marking the page. Iona has placed a clean sheet beneath it to catch any husks from the table. The open book and the envelopes make the room feel like a conversation between growing things and useful words.

She lifts a dried flower head and turns it gently over the shallow dish. A few seeds settle into the bowl, each making a sound too small to separate from the rain. She leaves the empty head beside its labelled envelope. It still has a lovely shape, and she may draw it later when the rest of the table is clear.

The shed has a drawer devoted to small useful things. Iona pulls it open to show you short ribbons, clean paper, and buttons left from the cloth bags Ada made for the shelves. Sometimes Ada takes a ribbon for her work, then brings back another colour. The drawer has become a modest collection of possibilities, changing quietly as the garden's work continues.

Near the door, a cotton bag hangs from a wooden peg. Its seams are strong, and its handles have softened through use. June takes it into the greenhouse when she chooses seeds for the next planting. Today, it holds a pencil and an empty notebook. A stitched fern curls along the bottom corner, its thread almost the colour of the canvas.

Iona steps outside to bring in the cushion from the bench. You follow her as far as the doorway. The flowers beneath the window have closed slightly in the evening air, their pale petals gathering around their centres. Across the path, the greenhouse shines through a curtain of fine rain, each bright pane carrying a soft reflection of the leaves outside.

She brushes a leaf from the cushion and carries it indoors. It belongs to the chair beside her table, but on dry days she takes it out to make the bench more welcoming. You can see where the fabric has faded a little in the middle. It is the same comfortable sort of wear as the striped cushion in Ruth's alcove.

Back at the table, you help Iona gather the finished envelopes into a wooden tray. They sit loosely enough to be lifted without bending, their labels facing the same way. She leaves an empty space beside them for the ones she will fold another day. The arrangement has the pleasing simplicity of tools returned to their hooks after a gentle afternoon's work.

Iona opens the album once more and adds your drawing near the back. She uses little paper corners to hold it, leaving the surface untouched. Around it, the cream page gives the pond and willow room to breathe. Pip is still round beneath his pergola, and the pencilled rain looks rather like fine thread falling through the spaces between the leaves.

You spend a quiet moment looking at the finished page. The greenhouse, the kitchen, and Ruth's table all belong to this same small garden. Their separate details fit together as easily as the envelopes in the tray. Someone has grown the mint. Someone has made the cups comfortable to hold. Someone has left a chair where another person might enjoy sitting.

Iona closes the album and puts the lid on the biscuit tin. She leaves the dried flower head in its bowl, where the lamp shows the delicate pattern around its rim. The room is ready for the next unhurried visit. Beyond the threshold, the path shines with rain, and the scent of the garden returns as you step into the sheltered doorway.

The pergola is close enough for you to see the edge of your blanket from here. Pip has settled against its fold, with his tail curved alongside his body. Mara is placing the teapot back on its tile. You follow the path towards them, carrying the quiet pleasure of the seed library and its rows of carefully labelled envelopes.

At the bench, Mara draws your cup a little nearer to where you will sit. The lantern above the pergola shines on the table and the blanket, leaving the pond beyond in soft shadow. Across the garden, Iona lowers the light over her shelves. You can still make out the open doorway and the flowers beneath the window, gathered together in the rain.
