The garden kitchen stands beside a miniature orchard tucked against the enclosing wall. A few small apple trees grow close to the building, with pear branches guided flat along the brickwork behind them. Above the open door, a wooden board has been painted with a small loaf and a leaf. The paint is a warm brown, almost the colour of the crust itself.

Through the window, you notice a blue cloth folded over an empty basket. Beside it lies a note in a rounded hand, thanking someone for a jar of pear preserve. There is a cheerful drawing beneath the message: a pear with a little leaf resting on its head. It seems quite content to keep the basket company on the shelf.

The kitchen light reaches the sheltered step, where a pair of garden shoes has been placed neatly against the wall. A sprig of rosemary rests in a jug nearby. From here, you can still see the greenhouse through the leaves, and the pond beyond it. The small garden holds its different pools of light together beneath the gentle evening rain.

You pause beside the doorway, drawn by the quiet scent of warm flour. There is a bench outside with a wooden back polished by use, and a rosemary bush brushes against its end. Tomas looks up from the kitchen table and welcomes you with a nod. He has set a chair where the oven's remaining warmth reaches it comfortably.

Tomas is standing at a broad table beneath the window. His sleeves are folded above his wrists, and there is a soft dusting of flour on the front of his apron. He has been preparing dough to rest until morning. Several covered bowls sit along a shelf, each beneath a cloth with a different pattern woven into its edges.

He moves a chair closer to the table and takes a cup from a hook. There is a kettle beside the oven, keeping warm in the heat that remains from the day's baking. Tomas pours water over a little bundle of mint. The leaves open slowly, and their clean scent rises through the round mouth of the cup.

From your chair, you can see into the orchard. The rain is light enough to show itself mostly where it catches the window's glow. Apple leaves shine along their upper edges, then turn dark as they move. Beneath the nearest tree stands an empty basket, tilted on its side so that any water can find its way out.

Tomas brings a small wooden board to the table. On it is a loaf that has cooled enough to cut, with a crust the colour of autumn leaves. He slices it with an easy, practised motion and sets the first piece beside your cup. The inside is pale and open, marked by little pockets where the dough has risen.

There is butter in a covered dish and a bowl of apple compote, left from a batch he made that afternoon. You take a little of each. The compote is soft without being entirely smooth. A piece of apple keeps its shape against the bread, tasting gently of the orchard and of the cinnamon Tomas stores in a small pottery jar.

The jar sits on a shelf among other jars of different sizes. None matches its neighbour, yet together they seem to belong. There is a tall one for flour, a broad one for oats, and a squat yellow pot holding spare wooden spoons. Tomas explains that the yellow pot began its life as a place for growing chives.

When the chives moved into the garden, the pot came indoors. Now its rim carries the faint marks of spoon handles, and the glazed flowers around its sides have become familiar companions to the baking. You touch one painted petal with a fingertip. The glaze is cool and slightly uneven, catching the light in a small, curved pool.

Tomas opens a drawer and takes out the recipe book. It is held closed with a length of cotton tape, and its corners have softened from many evenings on this table. There are flour marks on the cover. Inside, some recipes are written in a careful hand, while others appear on loose sheets tucked between the pages.

He finds the bread you are eating. His mother wrote the first version, with a short note about warming the bowl on a cold day. Tomas has added his own observations underneath. A little more resting time gave a softer loaf. The wide tin made a good shape for toast. Someone drew a smiling apple in the margin.

The apple, he tells you, is his mother's latest contribution. She visited recently and decided that a book containing so much useful advice should also contain something entirely unnecessary. Her little drawing now sits beside the ingredients with a leaf on its head. Tomas leaves it there, pleased each time he opens the page and finds it smiling back.

You like the way the recipe has room to grow. It holds familiar instructions, but it also allows for the weather, the feel of the dough, and the particular bowl waiting on the shelf. Tomas turns a page to show you a pressed leaf. He placed it there after an afternoon when the orchard apples made an especially good filling.

The leaf is golden brown and almost transparent around the veins. He keeps it inside a folded sheet of paper, so that the recipe itself stays clean. For a moment, it resembles a small map. Its lines branch from the stem like the garden paths, curving towards the greenhouse, the pond, and the places where a bench waits beneath the leaves.

On the table, the dough waits beneath a blue cloth. Tomas lifts one corner and checks it with a light touch. You watch the surface yield beneath his fingertips, then settle back into its rounded shape. He folds the cloth into place again. It needs only time now, and he is quite willing to let time do its part.

He uses a soft brush to gather the flour from the table. The brush is wide and made of pale wood, darkened slightly where his hand holds it. Each slow sweep leaves a clean strip of grain behind. Soon the table looks ready for supper, or a letter, or someone arranging a handful of seeds from the garden.

You ask about the blue bread cloth on the shelf. Tomas smiles and points towards the basket. Ruth brought it back with a jar of pear preserve, folded around it so that the glass would sit comfortably. Before that, June borrowed it for a loaf shared in the greenhouse. The cloth has become familiar with all the garden's tables.

Ada mended its edge, he adds, when the hem began to loosen. Her stitches are so tidy that you have to look closely to find them. Tomas brings the cloth over and spreads a corner across the table. Tiny threads, almost the same blue, follow the original border. The repair has made the cloth feel settled rather than new.

He folds it again with the mended corner showing. Tomorrow, it will line a basket of bread for the table beside the pond. For this evening, it rests beside the recipe book. The kitchen has the contented feeling of a place where the day's work has been put down carefully, leaving space to sit and enjoy what was made.

Through the open door comes the scent of the fruit trees. The little roof above the step keeps the doorway dry, so the fresh air can wander into the warm kitchen. You hear rain touching leaves, then the softer sound of drops falling from leaf to leaf. Somewhere close by, water slips into a clay saucer beneath a pot.

You walk with him as far as the covered step. The bench is dry where the roof reaches over it. From here, the trees appear less like a picture and more like a gathering of individual shapes. One leans slightly towards the path. Another has a branch low enough to shelter the basket, its leaves trembling whenever a drop gathers weight.

Tomas knows the trees by these small differences. He points out the one whose fruit keeps its shape when baked, and the one that makes a particularly smooth sauce. His explanations are simple and affectionate. He is describing neighbours whose habits have become familiar, including the pear tree that always seems to lean into the best patch of afternoon light.

Near the step is a shallow wooden tray with apples laid on folded paper. Their skins are green and russet, each carrying its own pattern of colour. Tomas chooses one, turns it in his hand, and places it on the windowsill inside. He likes to leave an apple there for anyone who may want it later in the evening.

When you return to the table, the mint tea has cooled to a comfortable warmth. The lamp shows the grain of the wood through the last traces of flour. Tomas puts the recipe book away, fastening its cotton tape with a loose bow. He leaves the blue cloth beside the basket, then joins you over the remaining slices of bread.

For a little while, the room needs very few words. There is the pleasure of the bread itself, and the soft sound of rain beyond the door. The oven gives up its remaining warmth slowly. You notice that Tomas has turned his cup so the handle faces the same way as yours, both resting beside the edge of the board.

The kitchen grows quieter as Tomas places a lid over the butter dish. You can see the garden reflected in the window, its lanterns making pale circles beside the shelves. Between those reflections, the apple still rests on the sill. Its rounded shape catches a little light, as familiar now as the yellow pot of wooden spoons beside it.

When you return to the covered step, Mara is crossing the path with a folded napkin. She holds it up to show you a leaf stitched into its corner, then carries it towards the pergola. Beyond her, Pip has raised his head just long enough to consider the teapot. Soon he lowers it again, content with the evening as it is.

You leave Tomas with his cup and the remaining warmth of the kitchen. The scent of bread follows you onto the path, mingling with the leaves and wet stone. Beside the greenhouse, a little garden shed stands open beneath its roof. Inside are shelves of small envelopes and a table with a lamp, waiting as quietly as everything else in the garden.
