Pearl Jam, Jeremy. On the morning of January 8th, 1991, a 15-year-old boy named Jeremy Waddell arrived late to his second period English class at Richardson High School in Richardson, Texas. His teacher asked him to go to the office and get an admittance slip. He left the room. He came back with a .357 Magnum revolver. He walked to the front of the class, looked at his teacher, and said, "Miss, I got what I really went for." Then he put the barrel in his mouth and pulled the trigger in front of his teacher and 30 classmates. Eddie Vedder read about it in a small newspaper article that spring, a brief paragraph about a 15-year-old boy most people would never remember. Vedder felt what he described as the need to take that small article and make something of it, to give the action a reaction, to give it more importance than a few lines of newsprint. So, he wrote Jeremy. The song won four MTV Video Music Awards in 1993, including Video of the Year. It became one of the defining rock songs of its decade. Jeremy Waddell became known around the world, but here is the part that complicates everything. His mother, Wanda, stayed silent for 26 years. When she finally spoke in 2017, she said, "That day that he died did not define his life. He was a son, a brother, a friend. He was talented." A classmate who was in the room added, "I was angry at them for writing that song. You don't know. You weren't there." Jeremy Waddell was 15 years old. He existed long before that morning. The song made sure we'd never forget the moment. His family wishes we'd remember the person. The Boomtown Rats, I Don't Like Mondays. Bob Geldof was at a radio station at Georgia State University in Atlanta waiting to do an interview when a telex machine started printing. He read the headline coming through in real time. A 16-year-old girl named Brenda Ann Spencer had opened fire on an elementary school playground in San Diego, California. She had shot and killed the school's principal and a custodian. Eight children were wounded, so was a police officer. Spencer had fired from her bedroom window across the street using a .22 semi-automatic rifle, a Christmas gift from her father. Police surrounded the house for 6 hours until she surrendered. During the standoff, a reporter managed to get her on the phone. He asked her why she'd done it. "I just don't like Mondays." She said, "This livens up the day." Geldof went back to his hotel and wrote the song that night. It went to number one in the UK, Ireland, Australia, and South Africa. It stayed at the top of the British charts for 4 weeks in the summer of 1979. Spencer eventually wrote to Geldof from prison. She thanked him for making her famous. Geldof has said, "That letter is one of the things he regrets most, that a song he wrote became something she could be proud of." In 2025, Brenda Spencer was denied parole again. She is 62 years old. The two adults she killed that morning were Burton Wragg, the principal, and Mike Sucher, the school custodian. They had names. The song never mentions them. Deep Purple, Smoke on the Water. December 4th, 1971, Montreux, Switzerland. The band had come to record their album Machine Head at the casino on the edge of Lake Geneva. They had rented the Rolling Stones mobile recording studio, parked it outside, and were waiting for Frank Zappa and the Mothers of Invention to finish the final concert of the venue's season. After that show, the casino would be theirs. During Zappa's set, someone in the audience fired a flare gun at the wooden ceiling. The building went up in less than an hour. One of the oldest entertainment venues in Europe, a casino that had hosted symphony orchestras since 1881, reduced to a smoking shell. Zappa's band lost all their equipment. The casino director, Claude Nobs, ran in and out of the burning building pulling people to safety. No one was killed, but Deep Purple had nowhere to record. They eventually found a way, first at a sports pavilion, then at the Grand Hotel in nearby Territet, where they soundproofed the rooms with mattresses and musicians had to pass recordings through balconies to each other. The conditions were absurd. The album they made there, Machine Head, became one of the most important rock records ever made. Bassist Roger Glover woke up one morning with a title in his head. He'd watched the fire from across the lake, the smoke drifting out over the water in the dark. He wrote it down before he'd fully registered what it was. In January 1971, Jim Morrison recorded his final vocal in a Los Angeles studio. The song was Riders on the Storm, 7 minutes long, built on piano, rain sounds, and a bassline that felt like it was already moving away from something. He whispered an additional vocal track over his own voice. You can barely hear it in the final mix, just underneath everything, a whisper fading away into the rest of the song. The lyrics talked about a killer on the road, an existential traveler, someone who could not be reached. Morrison had been writing about versions of that figure for years. Ray Manzarek later said, "Morrison seemed to already know something when he sang it, that the recording session had a specific weight to it." By the time L.A. Woman was released in April 1971, Morrison had already left for Paris. He didn't tell the band he was going, he didn't say goodbye to the studio, he just left. On July 3rd, 1971, he was found dead in a bathtub in his Paris apartment by his girlfriend Pamela Courson. He was 27 years old. The cause of death was never officially determined. Riders on the Storm was issued as a single in June 1971, weeks before his death. It climbed the charts through the summer as he was dying and after he was gone. February the 3rd, 1959, Don McLean was 13 years old delivering newspapers on his route in New Rochelle, New York when he saw the headline. Buddy Holly was dead, Ritchie Valens was dead, J.P. the Big Bopper Richardson was dead. A small chartered plane had gone down in a cornfield near Clear Lake, Iowa killing all four people on board, the three musicians and their pilot, Roger Peterson. They had been flying between tour dates on the Winter Dance Party Tour skipping a cold bus ride. Holly was 22, Valens was 17, the Big Bopper was 28. McLean loved Buddy Holly, had followed everything he recorded and on a cold February morning reading a newspaper on a delivery route the music stopped being something that would always be there. He never forgot it. 12 years later in 1971 he wrote American Pie, an 8 and 1/2 minute journey through the decline of American innocence starting from that single frozen morning. He called February 3rd, 1959 the day the music died. He dedicated the album to Buddy Holly in small print on the back cover. He refused to explain the song's lyrics for decades. He said doing so would require more set up than any interview could contain. What he did say was that it started with an ache. That music used to make him smile and then one morning it was gone before he'd even gotten to school. American Pie reached number one and stayed there for four weeks. It is now considered one of the greatest songs ever written. The ache that started it was a 13 year old boy reading a newspaper on a cold morning. It never fully left him. Kiss, Detroit Rock City. The song starts with a news report. A radio announcer describes a car accident in Detroit. A young man driving on the wrong side of the road hitting a delivery truck head-on, dead at the scene. Then, the music begin. One of the most anthemic riffs in rock history. And tells you that the driver was on his way to a KISS concert. Paul Stanley heard about a real accident during a KISS tour. A fan had been killed in a car crash while driving to see them play. The show was actually in North Carolina, not Detroit. And the exact details have never been fully confirmed, but the image lodged in him. Someone on their way to something that was supposed to be a celebration of being alive dying in the process of getting there. He couldn't write a ballad about it. That wasn't the right shape for the grief. So, instead, with producer Bob Ezrin, he built the song as a kind of tragic irony, anthem loud music, crushing final verse. The opening riff makes you want to move. The last lines of the song are a young man laughing because he knows he's about to die. Detroit Rock City was the opening track of Destroyer released in 1976. Radio stations flipped it over and played the B-side, a Peter Criss ballad called Beth, which became KISS's biggest US hit. The song about a fan dying on the way to a show got buried under commercial concerns. The crowd still sings it every night without quite realizing what it's about. That tension between what a song sounds like and what it says is what makes it linger. Metallica One James Hetfield had an image in his head, a person with no arms, no legs, no face, no voice, just a mind alone inside a body that had become a prison. His manager, Cliff Burnstein, handed him a novel, Johnny Got His Gun, written by Dalton Trumbo in 1939. Trumbo's book tells the story of Joe Bonham, an American soldier who survives an artillery shell in World War I. He wakes in a hospital and slowly realizes what has happened to him. He has lost his arms, his legs, his eyes, his ears, his mouth, his nose. He cannot move, he cannot speak, he cannot hear or see anything, but he is completely conscious and he is completely alone. The only way he can communicate is by tapping his head against his pillow in Morse code. The message he taps over and over to anyone who might hear, "Please kill me." Metallica built One around that story. The song begins quiet, almost delicate, and spends 10 minutes escalating into something that sounds like a mind tearing itself apart from the inside. For their music video, they licensed footage from the 1971 film adaptation of the novel. They eventually bought the rights to the film outright so they would never have to stop showing it. One was Metallica's first ever music video. It won them their first Grammy in 1990 for best metal performance. The character in the song was fictional, but Trumbo based the novel on real accounts from World War I soldiers who had survived with catastrophic injuries. Men who lived for years inside that exact silence. One is 8 minutes of what that might actually feel like. Avenged Sevenfold, So Far Away. Jimmy Sullivan, The Rev, had been Avenged Sevenfold's drummer since the beginning. He and guitarist Synyster Gates had been best friends since middle school. They had grown up together in Huntington Beach, California, started playing music together as teenagers, and built one of the most successful metal bands of their generation together. By late 2009, The Rev was 34 days away from walking into the studio to begin recording what would become the album Nightmare. All the music had been demoed. He had been writing obsessively. Other band members said he was on fire, taking every idea and adding something to it. On December 28th, 2009, he was found unresponsive at his home. He was 28 years old. The coroner later confirmed the cause was an accidental overdose, a combination of prescription opioids, and anti-anxiety medication, and alcohol. An enlarged heart contributed to his death. The band had to decide what to do. They finished the album with drummer Mike Portnoy filling in on drums, performing parts The Rev had already mapped out. The album was dedicated to him. Synyster Gates wrote "So Far Away" during that process. He had originally started the song for his grandfather, but somewhere during the writing, it became a goodbye to his best friend. At live shows, when the band performs it, the crowd holds up lighters and phones. A banner of The Rev goes up behind the stage. The band doesn't speak much during those moments. The last song The Rev ever wrote, completed 3 days before he died, was called "Fiction". He had originally titled it "Death" before we get to the final two. On March 20th, 1991, Eric Clapton's 4-year-old son, Conor, died in an accidental fall at a New York City apartment where he was staying with his mother, Italian actress Lori Del Santo. It was a Thursday morning. Clapton had plans to see him that day. He stepped away from music for 9 months. No performances, no recording sessions. When he returned, he was working on the soundtrack for a 1991 film called "Rush", collaborating with songwriter Will Jennings. He had already written the first verse of a song. Jennings later said, "That first verse was, in his words, the whole song." Jennings wrote the remaining lines around it. The result was "Tears in Heaven". It won three Grammy Awards in 1993, Record of the Year, Song of the Year, and Best Male Pop Vocal Performance. It reached the top 10 in over 20 countries. In his 2007 autobiography, Clapton wrote that Connor's birth in 1986 had been the main reason he got sober. He had been struggling with alcohol and cocaine for years. His son changed that. In 2004, Clapton stopped performing Tears in Heaven at live shows. He told interviewers he had moved on and felt he could no longer connect to the song the way it required. He didn't want to perform it as a formality. The song exists because a father needed somewhere to put something that had no other place to go. That's the whole story. Alice in Chains, Nutshell. In September 1993, Alice in Chains went into a studio in Seattle with no plan. They had just finished an exhausting tour, 50,000 miles, loud music every night. They wanted to decompose. Layne Staley insisted they record in analog without digital editing software. Just the band playing honestly in a room. What they made in those sessions became Jar of Flies, the first EP in music history to debut at number one on the Billboard. The second track is called Nutshell. Layne Staley wrote every word of the lyrics. The music was built around him after the fact. He wrote about fighting a battle alone, about having no place to call home, about the press following him, misrepresenting him, reducing him, about the feeling of having given everything he had of himself and having it taken for someone else's use. And then, at the center of the song, the line that stops everything. "If I can't be my own, I'd feel better dead." He wasn't speaking metaphorically. He was speaking about heroin, what it costs to be a person inside an addiction that had already consumed most of his life, about the version of himself that existed before the drug and the version that existed after, and the impossibility of getting back. Bassist Mike Inez later said, "In a nutshell, he really put everything in a nutshell for everybody. That song still gets me choked up whenever I play it." The last time Layne Staley sang it live was at the band's MTV Unplugged performance in April 1996. You can hear in that version, compared to the studio recording, what two more years of heroin had done to his voice. It is a different instrument, still extraordinary, just heavier, more tired. He died six years later, alone in his apartment in Seattle. He had not left it in months. His body was not found for 2 weeks.
