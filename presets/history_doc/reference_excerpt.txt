It is just after sunrise on the first day of October, 331 years before the Common Era, on a wide, deliberately flattened plain in northern Mesopotamia, and you are about to discover the brutal way Alexander's army actually fought at Gaugamela, which is nothing like the movies. The dust has not risen yet. That will come. Right now, if you are standing in the front rank of Alexander's Macedonian phalanx, you can see everything. And everything is wrong. The Persian line in front of you is not a line. It is a horizon. It stretches so far to your left and right that you cannot see where it ends. Somewhere in that mass are perhaps 100,000 men. Cavalry from Bactria, horse archers from the Scythian steppe, elephants, 200 chariots, their axles fitted with iron scythes, waiting on ground that has been personally scraped smooth by Persian engineers, so that nothing will slow the blades down. You are 25 years old, or 30, or 40, and you are holding an 18-foot pike, and there are 47,000 of you. In the next few hours, one of these two armies will cease to exist as a fighting force. The empire that has ruled the world from the Aegean to the Indus for 200 years is about to be decided by roughly the amount of daylight it takes to walk from breakfast to evening. And here is what almost every retelling gets wrong. You have seen the movies. A single glorious cavalry charge, Alexander sweeping across a clean, empty field, hair streaming straight into Darius. That is not what happened. The real Macedonian answer to those 200 scythed chariots was so ugly, so mechanical, and so brutally practical that ancient audiences barely wanted to write it down. And there is a second question that the standard story hides. Alexander had Darius. He had the Persian king in sight, running nearly within a horse's stride, and Alexander turned around. He gave up the chance to end the war in an afternoon. Why? The answer is not glory. The answer is that on the other side of the battlefield, an old man was about to lose everything. Now, back to that leveled plain in Mesopotamia, and how two armies ended up standing on it. The plain has a name that most people have never heard. Gaugamela. Somewhere near modern Tell Gomel, in the flatlands northeast of Mosul, ancient writers often called this fight the Battle of Arbela, because Arbela, modern Erbil, was the nearest real city, and the place where the Persian treasury sat waiting. But the fighting happened here, on ground chosen by a king who understood he was running out of options. That king is Darius the third. He is somewhere between 45 and 50 years old. He was not supposed to be king at all. He came to the throne in 336 before the common era, after the murder of his predecessor, Artaxerxes the fourth. A court killing, a scramble, a distant cousin suddenly wearing the crown of Cyrus. Before that, he had been a soldier. He had personally put down a revolt in Egypt. He is not a coward, whatever Greek writers will say about him later.
